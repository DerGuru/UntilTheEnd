The potters lived where a slow river cut through banks of gray clay, and the smoke of their firing pits hung over the village most days of the dry season. Mei read it from a ridge away: a dozen pits, a great deal of wood, and work for anyone who knew fire. She knew fire. They took her on to feed the pits through the nights. Within a month the old potters had stopped checking her work.

The girl was the youngest of a family that had been potting longer than anyone could remember. She was ten, perhaps, and every pot she made came out of the fire cracked, every single one. Her brothers had stopped teasing her about it, which was worse than the teasing. She sat at the edge of the firing ground each morning while the pits were opened. She watched her own pots come out in pieces and did not cry. There was a set to her jaw that Mei recognized from a long way back.

It was not the clay. Mei watched the girl's hands for a week and knew that much. The clay was good. The fire was the trouble. The girl packed her pots into the hottest part of the pit, because that was where her father packed his, and her thin walls could not take the heat as fast as it came.

Mei could have told her in a sentence. Instead she sat down beside the girl's pit one night, when no one else was awake to see, and showed her how to hear it.

"Listen to it first," she said. "Hear that? It's still hungry. You don't put anything thin near it while it sounds like that."

The girl listened with her whole face. "It sounds like fire."

"It sounds like a lot of things. You'll learn them."

They were the old woman's words. Mei heard that, and this time she did not have to stop.

So that season she taught the girl the fire. Which sounds meant wait. Which colors meant now. How to stand the pots at the cool edge and walk them in slowly, a finger's width at a time, as the heat came up. The girl was stubborn and quick. She argued with everything, and she was right about some of it. Mei found she was getting up in the gray before dawn to check the girl's pit before the girl did. She found she minded which way the wind blew on firing nights. She did not look at any of that too closely.

The pot came out whole on the last firing of the season.

The girl did not shout. She knelt by the pit with the pot in both hands, turning it, looking for the crack that had always been there, and there was no crack. Then she carried it across the firing ground, past her father and her brothers, straight to Mei. She held it up.

"Say it's a fine pot," she said.

For a breath Mei was somewhere else, across a table from a gourd.

She looked at the pot. It was a fine pot. She could see every way it was fine: the even wall, the clean ring it would give if you flicked it, and the warm gray where the fire had come up slow.

"It's a fine pot," Mei said.

The girl kept looking at her. Something went out of her face, not all of it, only the part that had been waiting.

"You said it like the weather," she said.

Mei had no answer to that. The girl took the pot back against her chest and went to find her mother.

Her mother saw it from across the firing ground and shrieked. She ran. She snatched up the pot, held it over her head, and turned in a circle with it, shouting for the whole village to come see. The girl lit up, all of her, as a pit lights when the wind comes round.

Mei had used the same words. She stood by the pits a long while afterward and went back over them, looking for whatever the mother had put into them that she had not. She could not find it.

She left in the night, before the next firing. The girl was asleep by her family's fire with the pot beside her, one hand on it. This time Mei had nothing to set by a sleeping hand.
