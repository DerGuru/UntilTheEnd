The world got busier while I was not feeling much of anything.

It happened over such a stretch that I caught it only in pieces. Paths I had walked when they were game trails were roads now, with stones set in them. Places I remembered as a few huts by good water had grown walls. People had begun to stay put, to make the land feed them in rows, to keep more than they could eat, and to need walls because of the keeping.

Some of it genuinely stopped me. The first time I saw marks pressed into wet clay, and watched a man read yesterday's words off yesterday's mud, I stood and stared like a child. I had been alive when speech was the only way to carry a thought across a night, and here was a thought outliving the mouth that made it. I thought it was the most extraordinary thing anyone had ever done. For a little while I went around learning the marks in every town, as I had once learned words for water and fish and cold.

But wonder needs a short memory, and mine had gotten very long.

I watched the marks go from marvel to ledger inside a few lifetimes. The same hands that had written a song wrote tallies of grain, then debts, then the names of who owed and who was owed. I watched walls go up to keep raiders out and then get used to keep farmers in. I had seen all of it before, in miniature, around a hundred fires: the strong taking the surplus, the surplus buying more strength, the whole hungry turn of it. Cities were not a new thing under the sun. They were an old thing grown large enough to see from a distance. Nothing about the shape was new. There was just more of it, built of stone now instead of talk, and it would rise and harden and fall exactly as everything smaller had risen and hardened and fallen. Once I saw that, the wonder went out, and it did not come back on for anything the cities ever did.
