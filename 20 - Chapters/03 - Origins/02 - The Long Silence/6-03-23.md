Her hands healed on the way down. She did not watch them do it. Below the pass she knelt and scrubbed them with snow, then her face, then the front of her clothes, until it stopped coming away pink. Where the scree began, the new nails had come in, pale and soft at the edges. By the first herders' huts there was nothing left on her for anyone to run from.

Half a day out of the foothills she heard it: a low noise with no edges to it, rising and falling without a break. She stopped in the road and looked back at the slopes. They were brown to the ridgeline. There was no snow on them.

It was only the city ahead, one of the nameless ones. By then they had all blurred together into the same city getting bigger. However long she had truly been up on the mountain, it had gone on growing without her.

She arrived during a festival, at the height of it. The streets were packed shoulder to shoulder, and nobody looked twice at one more ragged stranger. Music came from three directions in three different keys. Smoke from a hundred cook-fires hung low over the crowd, thick with fat and burnt honey. Torches burned on every wall, and after the long blue evenings on the mountain the light hurt. An elbow found her ribs. She stepped away from it into a broad, sweating back that did not even turn. For years the loudest thing near her had been an eagle shifting its feet on a rock.

The noise was the smaller part. Every one of them hummed. On the mountain there had been one hum near her, the eagle's, small enough to leave alone. Here there were thousands, packed wall to wall, and they came at her from every side at once. The weight of them sat on her ears and her chest, as the lake did at the bottom of a deep dive. She had forgotten how much pressure so many people in one place could exert.

She found a doorway and put her back to the wood. She stood there a long time with the crowd going past a hand's width from her face, and watched a whole people spend itself on being alive for one loud night. The mountains were four days behind her. She could be back in the snow before the moon turned.

She stepped out of the doorway and went into it on purpose. She had come down off the mountain for this, though she would not have said so: to stand in the middle of the loudest living thing she could find and see whether any of it would catch. A dance ring came wheeling down the street. A girl with a painted face grabbed her hand and pulled her in. Mei knew the steps. She had known steps like them for longer than the city had stood. In the ring it was easier. Her feet had something to do, and the noise had a beat in it she could follow. After a turn of the ring the girl let go of her hand and reached for someone else. Mei stepped out of the ring and it closed behind her.

Still, her hands did the small good things. That is the part I hold onto when I am trying to be fair to what she had become. I am looking at them as I say it. They have not changed. Later that night a child went down under the feet of the crowd, as children do. She had it up, out, and set on a wall in three movements. The mother came shoving through, snatched the child off the wall, and caught Mei's hands in both of hers. She held on, weeping, saying something over and over that I doubt she even heard above the music. Mei stood and let her. She waited for the woman to let go. When she did, Mei went on, and had forgotten them both by the next corner. But she had done it. Some worn thread in her still pulled toward it, without heat, without choosing. She did not know then how much would one day hang on that thread.

Toward the end of the night a man with a tray of honey cakes pushed a basket into her arms. "Hold this," he said, and went shoving off into the crowd, shouting a name. Mei held it.

The music thinned. The torches burned down to stubs. The crowd went home in twos and threes, and gray light came up over a street of trampled flowers. She was still standing where he had left her when the sweepers came through. One of them stopped in front of her and leaned on his broom.

"You selling those?"

"No."

"Then what are you doing?"

Mei thought about it. "Holding them."

He looked at her for a while. "Well, put them down somewhere. I've got to do that bit."

She set the basket against the wall and stepped aside so he could sweep.

That was what she had become by the time the world got around to wanting her: smooth, portable, and empty. She watched everything and needed nothing. A woman like that is not at peace. A woman like that is only available. A thing with no reasons left will do whatever the first strong hand asks of it. She did not know she was waiting. But she was ready, and ready was enough.
