I do not know how many fires lay between that valley and the day I walked into Lin Feiyun's doorway. Enough that I had stopped counting them too. I had become good at my bargain by then, thin and portable, a woman who could sit at any hearth for a season and take no mark and leave none. I had that confused with wisdom for a long time.

She lived at the edge of a village I meant to pass straight through. It was raining, the steady gray rain that settles in for days, and her door stood open and gave off a smell I had no name for. Something green being crushed, something bitter, and something warm underneath it all. I stopped in the road, caught by my own nose. She looked up from her grinding and saw a soaked stranger dripping in her road, and she said the thing that undid me, though it took me years to understand it had.

She said, "You're letting the rain in. Come or go, but shut the door."

I came in. I shut the door. I meant to stay until the rain let up.

She did not ask where I was from or where I was going, the two questions everyone asked, the two questions I had a hundred smooth answers ready for. She handed me the pestle and told me my arm was fresher than hers. So I ground her herbs while she cut, and the rain went on, and the room filled with that smell, and she hummed while she worked, tuneless and absent, the sound of a person who has forgotten anyone is listening. That was the whole of it. A dry room, a smell, a woman humming, and a task set into my hands as though I had always been there to do it.

The rain let up on the third day. I did not go.

I told myself the usual thing. A few more days, the roads are mud, she can use the hands. But the roads had been mud before and never held me. What held me was the smell of the room before she woke, the sure small work of her hands, and the plain fact that she asked nothing of me and gave anyway. I had spent an age teaching myself to want less. And here, over a bowl of crushed leaves, I caught myself wanting to stay. Not to pass through. To stay. It frightened me more than the scree slope had, more than the cold water, more than anything the world had ever tried on my body, because my body had never once been in danger, and this was.

Her name was Lin Feiyun. I have let a hundred thousand names fall behind me like leaves. Hers I kept, and I keep it still, and I will say it as often as this telling lets me, because for a long time it was the only warm thing I owned. She was the first person who was kind to me for no reason at all. She wanted nothing from me. She had more warmth than her small life needed, and she let the overflow fall on whoever was standing in her doorway. One rainy day that was me.
