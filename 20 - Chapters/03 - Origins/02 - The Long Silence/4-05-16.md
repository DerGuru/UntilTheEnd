The first snow came a month after the porcupine, dry and fine, the kind that lies on the thatch and does not melt. Lin Feiyun sat by the door in the last of the daylight with a quill in her fingers and a torn shirt across her knees. She had worked out how to split the point to take a thread, and she was pleased with herself. She was also coughing, a small dry cough she kept turning her head away from, as if it belonged to someone else in the room.

"That cough," Mei said.

"Winter cough. Half the valley has it." Lin Feiyun bit off the thread. "The other half will have it by the new moon."

It did not break. By midwinter it had gone down into her chest and settled there. A tiredness came with it that she could not sleep off. She knew what it was before Mei did. She had spent her life at other people's sickbeds. One evening she put the sewing aside, listened to her own breathing for a while with her head tipped, and named it as plainly as she named everything.

"Wet lungs," she said. "Old woman's winter."

She was not afraid of it, or if she was, she folded the fear small and kept it out of the way, as she kept everything.

She told Mei what to grind for it: bitterleaf, and the root she had kept to herself since the first wet afternoon. "You may as well have the name now," she said, and gave it to her. Mei ground them at the table while the snow ticked against the shutters. The smell came up out of the bowl as it had on that first day. Green, then bitter, then the warm thing at the bottom of it.

"Finer," Lin Feiyun said from the bed. "I need a powder, not crumbs."

Mei ground it finer.

Here is the part I have understood least kindly about myself, and I have had a long time to try.

Mei knew things by then. She had years of Lin Feiyun's trade in her hands, and under it she had the warm, which Lin Feiyun had never once asked about. There were things she could have tried. The last thing she had tried to mend with the warm was a boy's foot in the red-stone country. She had not forgotten the sound he made. So she ground what Lin Feiyun told her to grind, and nothing else. She told Lin Feiyun, and told herself, that the illness was past medicine. And that was even true. But under the true thing was another thing she would not look at. To save her would be to keep her. To keep her would be to do this again in twenty years, or forty. And some part of the girl that had grown very tired had already begun, without asking her leave, to let Lin Feiyun go.

And there was a thing under even that, though she would not have found the words for it then, and I am in no hurry to find them now. A season before, she had got both hands under a spirit beast, quills and all, and turned it over in the gourd rows. There is nothing to get your hands under in a winter. Nothing to turn over in an old woman's lungs going slowly to water, or in the plain arithmetic of a body that has done its years. The one strength she had was good for exactly the wrong thing. She could break anything that came at Lin Feiyun from the outside. She could not lift a finger against the thing that came from within.

So Mei did the other thing instead, the only thing she has ever really been able to do for the dying. She stayed. She kept Lin Feiyun warm. She brought her water she could barely take and broth she could not. When Lin Feiyun could no longer get out to the garden, Mei described it to her instead: what was up, what the frost had taken, where the gourd vine had finally given out, and what she would set in come spring.

"How deep?" Lin Feiyun asked, with her eyes shut.

"A knuckle for the gourds. Deeper for the beans."

"And the birds?"

"Thorn brush," Mei said. "And somebody near enough to throw a stone."

She covered her when she shivered. She kept the fire high enough for her to see. She sat through the nights, because the nights were when the fear came for people. She took her own sleep in the afternoons instead, in pieces, when Lin Feiyun slept. Whatever those last days had in them to give, she gave. It was not much. It was what there was.

People still came to the door, because a village does not stop hurting for its healer's winter. Lin Feiyun saw to them from the bed. She sent Mei to do the work of the hands and called the instructions across the room: which leaf, how tight, and the serrated cap, never the smooth one. The woman who had fallen out of the walnut tree brought broth every other day. Each time she stood a while in the doorway, looking from the bed to Mei, and went home without saying what she had come to say.

Once, near the end, on a night when the wind came through every gap in the shutters, Lin Feiyun asked for the tune. Mei hummed it for her, the small falling thing with the catch in the middle. She got the catch right.

"That's cheating," Lin Feiyun said, without opening her eyes. "You've had longer to practice."

Lin Feiyun went out with the winter, near morning. Mei was awake for it, but Lin Feiyun left as she had lived, without fuss, sliding under so quietly that the fact of it arrived after she was already gone. One breath was the last one. Mei only knew which by the ones that did not come after.

She buried her at the edge of the garden, in the good soil they had argued over. Half the village stood in the snow for it. The two men who had carried the porcupine broke the frozen ground with antler picks, and Mei let them, because it was a thing they needed to do with their own hands.

Then she did a thing she had not done in a very long time. She stayed. Weeks. She kept the fire. She tended the beds. The village still brought its small hurts to the door. She set their bones, ground their pastes, and did not let any of them call her the healer. She told herself she was letting the ground settle, letting the winter pass, and being sensible about the roads. The truth was that she did not want to walk out of the last place that still smelled like Lin Feiyun.

One morning just before spring, when the house had lost all of Lein Feiyun's scent and and begun to smell only of Mei, she banked the fire, shouldered her water, and went before the sun rose. The garden would keep a while without her. She did not look back at it from the ridge, and told herself that was because there was nothing new to see.

She did not notice, walking down out of that valley, that something in her had gone still and would not start again. You would think a thing like that would announce itself. It does not. I have gone back over that morning more times than you would believe, looking for the moment it happened, and I never find it. It happens quietly, some hour when no one is watching the water, and by the time you see it the surface is already hard enough to stand on. She walked down out of the valley lighter than she had climbed up into it, and she thought that was grief lifting. It was not grief lifting. It was grief being the last thing to go.
