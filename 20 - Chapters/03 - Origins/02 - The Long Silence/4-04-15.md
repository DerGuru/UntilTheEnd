Lin Feiyun got to her feet, slowly, as she got up from everything now. She came to Mei first. She took Mei's wrist without asking and turned the hand palm up. She looked at the quills standing through it as she looked at every hurt that came to her door. Then she took hold of the nearest one and pulled it out.

Mei hissed. Blood welled up in the hole. Lin Feiyun watched it, and Mei watched her watch it. The edges drew in. The bleeding slowed, then stopped, and the hole closed over into plain skin.

"Hold still," Lin Feiyun said, and pulled the next one.

She did them all. The palms, the forearm, the shoulders, and the hip. She made Mei sit on the edge of the sledge to get at the thigh. She worked at her usual pace, without hurrying. Each quill went onto a growing pile by her knee, and after each one she watched the hole close. Mei sat still under the old woman's hands and watched her face for the change. None came.

When the last one was out, Lin Feiyun wiped her fingers on her knee. She walked over to the dead thing, this woman who had just pulled a double handful of quills out of a stranger she took off the road and watched every hole close behind them. She looked it over with a healer's frank appraisal. She pulled down an eyelid with her thumb to see the green. She drew a long quill out of its crown and tried the point on the pad of her finger.

"Well," she said. "The quills are worth something. I've been sewing with fishbone half my life." She looked at the porcupine a moment longer. "And I'm not hauling it." She looked at Mei. "You'll haul it."

The shelf gourd had come through the whole thing without a mark. Lin Feiyun picked it up herself. Mei got the porcupine onto the sledge by its hind feet, the only part of it without quills, and felt Lin Feiyun watch her do it. The gourds that had survived came off to make room. Then, at Lin Feiyun's direction, they went back on top.

"No sense making two trips," she said.

Lin Feiyun looked at Mei then, at the wrap hanging off her in bloody tatters, and pulled the shawl from her own shoulders. It came across the sledge and hit Mei in the chest.

"Put that on," she said. "You look like a sieve."

Mei took up the poles. Lin Feiyun walked beside her with the shelf gourd in the crook of one arm and her other hand tucked into her sleeve against the cold. In all their years it was the first time she had come home from the plot carrying almost nothing, and she seemed to be enjoying it.

At the first houses Mei slowed. She bent into the poles until her back was flat, set her feet wide, and let her breath come ragged through her teeth. Nobody had asked her to. She had done it at the first houses of every village she had ever walked into hauling more than a woman her size should.

Lin Feiyun glanced at her sidelong. "You're good at that."

"I've had practice."

Lin Feiyun said nothing to that. She shifted the gourd to her other arm and walked on a little ahead, so that she would be the first thing the village saw.

Half the village was out by the time they reached the middle of it. A man put down his adze. Two boys ran to fetch their fathers. Lin Feiyun did not wait for the questions.

"It came out of the trees sick," she said, loud enough for the back of the crowd. "Spirit-rot. Look at the eyes on it. It ran itself to death in my gourds and took half of them with it." She nudged the snout with her foot. "Burn the eyes and the guts. Whatever you mean to eat, you boil till it falls apart. The quills are mine. And somebody help the girl. She's dragged it all the way from the plot."

Two young men took the poles from Mei. They grunted at the weight and gave her a nod of plain respect for getting it this far. Nobody looked at her harder than that. Every one of them had come into the world through Lin Feiyun's hands, or had a bone set by them, or had buried someone she sat beside at the end. In all that time she had never told any of them a thing that was not so. Mei had never heard her do it either.

That night the village boiled it till it fell apart, as Lin Feiyun had told them, and ate it with beans. Lin Feiyun kept the quills.

Lin Feiyun's house smelled of woodsmoke and of the bitter green paste she rubbed into her knuckles at night. Smoke from the village fire, where they were burning what she had told them to burn, crept in under the door. Mei sat on her bedroll with her pack open across her knees.

She was folding her spare wrap into it, small and square, as she had folded it at more than a hundred fires. The bowl went in after it. Then the flint and the water skin. Each thing went where it had always gone. Then she turned the pack over, looking for the cord, and remembered what had become of it.

"Round that girl's arm," Lin Feiyun said from across the fire. She did not look up from her cup to say it. "The one who fell out of the walnut tree. She's got two of her own now, and they're both up the same tree."

Mei's hands went still on the pack.

"You never looked for another one," Lin Feiyun said. "I noticed." She worked her stiff fingers around the cup. "Leave that. Come and sit where I can see you. I'm too old to talk to the side of your head."

Mei set the pack down at the foot of the bedroll, still open, and came to sit at the hearth. Lin Feiyun watched the fire a while before she said the rest of it.

"I've known a while," she said. "That you're not—" She waved a hand at Mei's unlined face, at the years, at the bundle of quills drying by the door, at all of it. "That you're not like us." She drank. "You've had your hands in every fever in this valley since the rain brought you, and you never once coughed. My hair went white waiting for yours to go gray. I'm old, girl, not blind, and not stupid."

Mei waited. She knew what came next. She had watched it start on more faces than she could count, and she had watched it finish. In the red-stone country a mother had carried her son up the path without a word. After that had come the looks, and the gap at the fire.

"How old—" Lin Feiyun stopped. She shook her head at herself. "No. Don't tell me. I'd only have to carry it." She drank again. "I decided a long time ago that it changed nothing I cared about."

Mei looked at the open pack. "Do you want me to go?"

It cost her something to ask, because she already knew the answer everywhere else she had ever been.

Lin Feiyun looked at her as though she had said something genuinely foolish. "Why would I want that? Who'd haul the next one?"

She reached for the pot on the hearthstones and poured the tea. She pushed Mei's cup across the stones with two fingers, without looking at her, as she had pushed a bowl of millet across the table every morning for years. Mei drank. Before her cup was empty she reached over and slid the pack back under the bedroll, still open.

That was the last time either of them ever spoke of it.

You have to understand what she gave me, that old woman with the aching hands and the gourds she was too proud of. Everywhere else, being seen had been the end of it. With her it was a porcupine to haul and a cup of tea.
