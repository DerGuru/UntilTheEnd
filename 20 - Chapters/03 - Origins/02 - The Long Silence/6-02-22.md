After the cities Mei went up into the mountains, where there was no one to watch and nothing to check off. She went higher than the herders went, higher than the trees, up into a country of rock and old snow where the only sound was wind. She found a ledge under an overhang that looked out over a whole valley of white. She sat down on it.

It was easier there. Alone on that ledge there was no gap to keep between herself and anyone, no face to get right, and no pot to call fine. There was only the cold, and she had stopped minding the cold a long time ago. She did not make a fire. She had not made one for herself in longer than she could remember now. Snow came and lay on her shoulders. She let it lie.

Seasons went over her. She watched the light change on the far wall of the valley, gold in the mornings, blue in the evenings, the same every day for a hundred days and then different. An eagle took to sitting on a rock below her ledge. After a year it no longer turned its head when she moved. She thought, once, on a still morning with the snow glittering, that this might be the answer. This, then. This I can bear.

The slope above her let go in a winter. I no longer remember which one. The snow and rock came down with a roar that shook the ledge.

She heard it before anything else. A crack, high up, flat and huge, as if the mountain had split along a seam. Then a sound that was less a sound than a weight coming down through the air. She looked up. The whole white face above the overhang was moving, sliding, coming apart into a cloud that poured down toward her faster than anything that size had any right to move.

She could have moved. There was time, just. She sat and watched it come.

It hit the overhang, came over the lip of it, and the world went white, then dark, then heavy.

She was on her back. She did not know how she had come to be on her back. The snow was packed around her so close she could not move a finger, and it was not soft. It was stone. It pressed on her chest, her face, and her open eyes.

She tried to breathe. There was a little air in front of her mouth, a pocket the size of a cupped hand. She breathed it. She breathed it again. It was already warm, already used, already thinning.

She waited for herself to panic. Nothing came.

So, she thought. This, then. It was very quiet under the snow. The weight on her chest had stopped hurting, or she had stopped noticing it. The air in the little pocket was nearly gone, and she found she did not mind. She lay in the dark and let it go.

For a moment everything waited.

Then something came up out of the bottom of her that she had not known was still there. It had no words. It was older than words. It was the thing that had kept a cast-out child alive in the dark, the thing that had opened her mouth around a sound on the scree slope. It came up through all the cold she had made of herself as if none of it had ever been there, and it was screaming. Her hands were clawing at the snow. Her throat was making a sound. Every part of her that she had spent lifetimes teaching not to want was wanting one thing, all at once, so hard it shook her.

And the world stopped.

The snow did not press. The dark did not move. Her hands were caught mid-claw in the pack. Her heart was caught between one beat and the next. She could feel it there, held, a fist that would not open.

Somewhere above her, through all that weight, a seam ran through the packed snow. She did not hear it. She felt it, as a line of cold drawn across her face.

Then it went on.

The pack shifted. Air came down the seam, thin, bitter, and real. She dragged it in with a sound like tearing cloth. She was digging. She did not remember starting.

It took her three days to reach the light. She came up out of the avalanche field at dusk on her elbows, with her fingernails gone and her hands raw to the bone. She knelt in the new snow and breathed. Her body went on breathing whether she attended to it or not, deep, greedy, as though the ledge, the quiet, and the little pocket of air she had been willing to let go had never happened.

A hunter was standing on the far side of the slide.

He had watched the whole face of the mountain come down three days before. He had come to see what it had buried. Now he stood with his spear in one hand and a dead hare in the other, staring at a woman who had climbed out of it. Mei looked back at him. She did not hide her hands. She did not get up.

He dropped the hare and ran.

She watched him go down the mountain, small and fast, until the dusk took him. She thought she ought to go after him. She thought it quite clearly, as she might have noted that the wind was changing. Then she looked down at the blood on her hands and could not find the part of her that had wanted so badly to live. It had gone back down to wherever it kept itself. It had not asked her anything on the way.

The hare lay where he had dropped it. After a while she got up, left it there, and walked down the other side of the mountain toward wherever the next city was.
