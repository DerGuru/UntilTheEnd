By the second or third year of walking I had stopped keeping count. Counting meant the number mattered, and it did not. I came down out of dry hills one evening into the smell of woodsmoke and wet reeds. A lake lay in the fold of the land, and a people lived along the near shore of it, in shelters of bent poles and hide.

They watched me come, as people watch anything that walks out of the hills alone. But I was young, and my hands were empty, and hunger had worn me thin enough to look like need instead of threat. A woman older than the rest lifted her chin toward the fire, which is a gesture that means the same in every tongue I have ever met. Sit.

The fire was hers. I understood that before I understood anything else about them. She fed it and banked it and carried its coal when the people moved, and the others arranged themselves around her without seeming to decide to. She showed me how she split the wet wood to reach the dry heart of it. I showed her, without meaning to, that I already knew, and instead of taking offense she laughed, a short bark with no cruelty in it, and handed me the next log.

I stayed. I learned their words for water and fish and cold and enough. I learned which of the children were brave and which only loud. I set fish traps in the reeds and was foolishly proud when they held. In the evenings the old woman kept her fire and I sat in the ring of its heat among people who had decided, for no reason I had earned, that I belonged in it. Some nights I laughed until my chest ached. I had a place to sleep and hands that were glad to see me in the morning.

It was small and warm, and I thought nothing of it, because I believed there would always be more of it. That was the shape I thought my life had taken: from one warm ring to the next, welcomed, useful, and gone before anyone minded. I was right about the walking. I had the rest of it exactly backward.
