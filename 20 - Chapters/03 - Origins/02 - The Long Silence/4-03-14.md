It came the autumn Lin Feiyun grew too old to pretend she was not old. The first frost was a night or two off. Mei could smell it in the mornings, a thin edge of metal on the air. The gourd vines had gone limp and gray at the leaf. They were at the plot late in the day, bringing in the last of the gourds before the cold got to them. Lin Feiyun was on her knees in the dirt, cutting them free with a flint knife. Mei was carrying.

For years they had done it together. Lin Feiyun had hauled her share of every harvest, one gourd at a time if she had to, and glared at anyone who reached for her basket. This autumn she cut, Mei carried, and neither of them mentioned it. Her hands were bad in the cold. The knife skidded off a stem as thick as a thumb. She set her jaw and sawed at it again.

"I can do that," Mei said.

"You can carry it." Lin Feiyun held the gourd up to the low sun and turned it, looking for soft spots. It was a big one, pale and ridged. "This one's for the shelf. Don't knock it about."

Mei took it down the row to the sledge, a mat of woven withies on two poles for dragging the harvest home. She set it on top of the others with more care than a gourd needed.

Then the birds went quiet all at once, the whole edge of the forest gone still, and Mei knew what that meant before the thing came out of the trees.

A moment later she heard it. A dry, busy clatter came out of the shadow under the trees, like the seed gourd on Lin Feiyun's shelf when somebody shook it. Mei actually glanced toward the house. The bracken parted.

It was a porcupine, or it had been. Something had gotten into it, a spirit gone rank in its blood, and it had swelled to the size of a crouching man, with a green wrongness lit in its eyes. Its quills had grown with it. They stood up off its back in a great black-and-white crown, each one as long as Mei's arm and as thick as her finger. The hollow ones at its tail shook together at every step with that dry seed-rattle. It came out of the trees with its nose low, snuffling. It did not look at Mei. It looked at the gourds, and at the old woman kneeling in the middle of them.

Lin Feiyun did what she had done to every pest that had ever come near that plot. She got one foot under her, waved the flint knife at it, and said, "Out. Out of my gourds, you."

The porcupine stamped. It grunted, deep and wet, and swung its back to her. Every quill on it went up at once. The rattle at its tail rose to a roar. Then it began to back toward her, fast, the whole bristling crown of it dropped into a hedge of points, and Lin Feiyun was still trying to get her other foot under her. Her knees would not do it fast enough.

Mei had spent longer than the village had stood being careful. Being small. Being a useful pair of hands and nothing more, so that no one would ever look at her as the beast was about to make them look. She had left a hundred fires to keep that carefulness whole.

She did not think about it at all.

She hit Lin Feiyun first, with her shoulder, as gently as anything can be done at that speed. The old woman went over sideways into the gourd leaves with a squawk of pure outrage. Then the porcupine came backing into the place where she had been, and Mei was there instead.

Its rump hit her first. Quills went into her thigh, her hip, and the forearm she threw down to cover herself. It was like being kicked by something with a hundred teeth. She heard herself yell. The porcupine bounced off her, spun, and came again.

On the second pass she had qi in her skin, packed as hard as she could pack it. Half the quills snapped against her. The other half went in anyway.

So she hit it. It was the obvious thing, and she did it well: flat-handed, square into the crown, with qi behind the blow, as she had hit the lion at the waterhole. The porcupine skidded a pace through the gourd vines. Otherwise the blow went into the quills and stayed there. Mei's hand came away with four of them straight through it, their points standing out of the back.

She swore and went for the head.

The head was the only part of it worth going for, small, blunt, and bare of quills, down low at the far end. The porcupine kept it there. She went left, and it turned. She went right, and it turned again, keeping that rattling crown toward her whichever way she came. They went around the gourd row. They went around it again. Gourds burst under its feet. 

"The other end!" Lin Feiyun shouted from the gourd leaves. "Its head, girl!"

"I know which end," Mei said through her teeth.

It backed into her again before she had finished saying it. She took it on the hip and felt three more go in. Her leg had begun to feel like someone else's. The porcupine was blowing hard now, green froth at its mouth, and the rattle never stopped, that idiotic, cheerful sound of seeds in a shell.

She stopped chasing the head.

The next time it came backing at her she did not step aside. She dropped to her knees in the ruined vines, let the crown come over her, and got both hands under its flank, quills and all. They went through her palms. She felt them grate on the small bones. She did not let go. She pulled qi into her arms, all of it she could hold, and heaved.

The porcupine came up off the ground squealing, legs going at the air. Mei turned it over. It came down on its back in the tilled earth, its whole crown driven in beneath it, and for a moment it could not get up. It lay there pinned on its own quills, feet paddling, belly turned up to the sky. The belly was pale, soft, and almost bare.

Mei laid her bloody hand flat on it.

This time she listened first. Under the hot skin the heart was going as fast as a bird's. The rot was in everything, sour and green, a wrongness she could feel the edges of. She did not flood it. She sent in one thread, no more than she would have given a coal, and put it exactly where the heart was.

Something in it came apart. The feet went on paddling a while, slower, then stopped. After a moment the birds started up again.

Mei got up. She stood in the wreck of the gourd row with quills standing out of her thigh, her hip, her shoulders, her forearm, and both palms. A few were snapped off short. The rest were as long as her arm and swung when she moved.

I can tell you now what it was. Tier one, and a sorry one at that. In a later age a sect's youngest disciple would have killed it before breakfast and spent the rest of the day complaining about the quills. Mei had never heard of tiers, or sects, or later ages. All she knew was that a porcupine had very nearly taken her apart in a gourd patch.

Mei turned around. Lin Feiyun was sitting up in the gourd leaves where Mei had knocked her. She was not looking at the dead thing. She was looking at Mei, at the quills, and at the blood running out from under them. Mei watched her finish the sum she had been circling for years and reach the total. And Mei felt the old thing come up in her: the leaving, the pack-and-go, the it-was-good-while-it-lasted.

Lin Feiyun got to her feet, slowly, as she got up from everything now. She came to Mei first. She took Mei's wrist without asking and turned the hand palm up. She looked at the quills standing through it as she looked at every hurt that came to her door. Then she took hold of the nearest one and pulled it out.

Mei hissed. Blood welled up in the hole. Lin Feiyun watched it, and Mei watched her watch it. The edges drew in. The bleeding slowed, then stopped, and the hole closed over into plain skin.

"Hold still," Lin Feiyun said, and pulled the next one.

She did them all. The palms, the forearm, the shoulders, and the hip. She made Mei sit on the edge of the sledge to get at the thigh. She worked at her usual pace, without hurrying. Each quill went onto a growing pile by her knee, and after each one she watched the hole close. Mei sat still under the old woman's hands and watched her face for the change. None came.

When the last one was out, Lin Feiyun wiped her fingers on her knee. She walked over to the dead thing, this woman who had just pulled a double handful of quills out of a stranger she took off the road and watched every hole close behind them. She looked it over with a healer's frank appraisal. She pulled down an eyelid with her thumb to see the green. She drew a long quill out of its crown and tried the point on the pad of her finger.

"Well," she said. "The quills are worth something. I've been sewing with fishbone half my life." She looked at the porcupine a moment longer. "And I'm not hauling it." She looked at Mei. "You'll haul it."

The shelf gourd had come through the whole thing without a mark. Lin Feiyun picked it up herself. Mei got the porcupine onto the sledge by its hind feet, the only part of it without quills, and felt Lin Feiyun watch her do it. The gourds that had survived came off to make room. Then, at Lin Feiyun's direction, half of them went back on top.

"No sense making two trips," she said.

Lin Feiyun looked at Mei then, at the wrap hanging off her in bloody tatters, and pulled the shawl from her own shoulders. It came across the sledge and hit Mei in the chest.

"Put that on," she said. "You look like a sieve."

Mei took up the poles. Lin Feiyun walked beside her with the shelf gourd in the crook of one arm and her other hand tucked into her sleeve against the cold. In all their years it was the first time she had come home from the plot carrying almost nothing, and she seemed to be enjoying it.

At the first houses Mei slowed. She bent into the poles until her back was flat, set her feet wide, and let her breath come ragged through her teeth. Nobody had asked her to. She had done it at the first houses of every village she had ever walked into hauling more than a woman her size should.

Lin Feiyun glanced at her sidelong. "You're good at that."

"I've had practice."

Lin Feiyun said nothing to that. She shifted the gourd to her other arm and walked on a little ahead, so that she would be the first thing the village saw.

Half the village was out by the time they reached the middle of it. A man put down his adze. Two boys ran to fetch their fathers. Lin Feiyun did not wait for the questions.

"It came out of the trees sick," she said, loud enough for the back of the crowd. "Spirit-rot. Look at the eyes on it. It ran itself to death in my gourds and took half of them with it." She nudged the snout with her foot. "Burn the eyes and the guts. Whatever you mean to eat, you boil till it falls apart. The quills are mine. And somebody help the girl. She's dragged it all the way from the plot."

Two men took the poles from Mei. They grunted at the weight and gave her a nod of plain respect for getting it this far. Nobody looked at her harder than that. Every one of them had come into the world through Lin Feiyun's hands, or had a bone set by them, or had buried someone she sat beside at the end. In all that time she had never told any of them a thing that was not so. Mei had never heard her do it either. She did it well.

That night the village boiled it till it fell apart, as Lin Feiyun had told them, and ate it with beans. Lin Feiyun kept the quills.

Lin Feiyun's house smelled of woodsmoke and of the bitter green paste she rubbed into her knuckles at night. Smoke from the village fire, where they were burning what she had told them to burn, crept in under the door. Mei sat on her bedroll with her pack open across her knees.

She was folding her spare wrap into it, small and square, as she had folded it at a hundred fires. The bowl went in after it. Then the flint, then the water skin. Each thing went where it had always gone. Then she turned the pack over, looking for the cord, and remembered what had become of it.

"Round that girl's arm," Lin Feiyun said from across the fire. She did not look up from her cup to say it. "The one who fell out of the walnut tree. She's got two of her own now, and they're both up the same tree."

Mei's hands went still on the pack.

"You never found another one," Lin Feiyun said. "I noticed." She worked her stiff fingers around the cup. "Leave that. Come and sit where I can see you. I'm too old to talk to the side of your head."

Mei set the pack down at the foot of the bedroll, still open, and came to sit at the hearth. Lin Feiyun watched the fire a while before she said the rest of it.

"I've known a while," she said. "That you're not—" She waved a hand at Mei's unlined face, at the years, at the bundle of quills drying by the door, at all of it. "That you're not like us." She drank. "You've had your hands in every fever in this valley since the rain brought you, and you never once coughed. My hair went white waiting for yours to go gray. I'm old, girl, not blind, and not stupid."

Mei waited. She knew what came next. She had watched it start on more faces than she could count, and she had watched it finish. In the red-stone country a mother had carried her son up the path without a word. After that had come the looks, and the gap at the fire.

"How old—" Lin Feiyun stopped. She shook her head at herself. "No. Don't tell me. I'd only have to carry it." She drank again. "I decided a long time ago that it changed nothing I cared about."

Mei looked at the open pack. "Do you want me to go?"

It cost her something to ask, because she already knew the answer everywhere else she had ever been.

Lin Feiyun looked at her as though she had said something genuinely foolish. "Why would I want that? Who'd haul the next one?"

She reached for the pot on the hearthstones and poured the tea. She pushed Mei's cup across the stones with two fingers, without looking at her, as she had pushed a bowl of millet across the table every morning for years. Mei drank. Before her cup was empty she reached over and slid the pack back under the bedroll, still open.

That was the last time either of them ever spoke of it.

You have to understand what she gave me, that old woman with the aching hands and the gourds she was too proud of. Everywhere else, being seen had been the end of it. With her it was a porcupine to haul and a cup of tea. I have been trying to deserve that for a very long time, and I am not certain I ever have.
