It came the autumn Lin Feiyun grew too old to pretend she was not old. The first frost was a night or two off. Mei could smell it in the mornings, a thin edge of metal on the air. The gourd vines had gone limp and gray at the leaf. They were at the plot late in the day, bringing in the last of the gourds before the cold got to them. Lin Feiyun was on her knees in the dirt, cutting them free with a flint knife. Mei was carrying.

For years they had done it together. Lin Feiyun had hauled her share of every harvest, one gourd at a time if she had to, and glared at anyone who reached for her basket. This autumn she cut, Mei carried, and neither of them mentioned it. Her hands were bad in the cold. The knife skidded off a stem as thick as a thumb. She set her jaw and sawed at it again.

"I can do that," Mei said.

"You can carry it." Lin Feiyun held the gourd up to the low sun and turned it, looking for soft spots. It was a big one, pale and ridged. "This one's for the shelf. Don't knock it about."

Mei took it down the row to the sledge, a mat of woven withies on two poles for dragging the harvest home. She set it on top of the others with more care than a gourd needed.

Then the birds went quiet all at once, the whole edge of the forest gone still, and Mei knew what that meant before the thing came out of the trees.

A moment later she heard it. A dry, busy clatter came out of the shadow under the trees, like the seed gourd on Lin Feiyun's shelf when somebody shook it. Mei actually glanced toward the house. The bracken parted.

It was a porcupine, or it had been. Something had gotten into it, a spirit gone rank in its blood, and it had swelled to the size of a crouching man, with a green wrongness lit in its eyes. Its quills had grown with it. They stood up off its back in a great black-and-white crown, each one as long as Mei's arm and as thick as her finger. The hollow ones at its tail shook together at every step with that dry seed-rattle. It came out of the trees with its nose low, snuffling. It did not look at Mei. It looked at the gourds, and at the old woman kneeling in the middle of them.

Lin Feiyun did what she had done to every pest that had ever come near that plot. She got one foot under her, waved the flint knife at it, and said, "Out. Out of my gourds, you."

The porcupine stamped. It grunted, deep and wet, and swung its back to her. Every quill on it went up at once. The rattle at its tail rose to a roar. Then it began to back toward her, fast, the whole bristling crown of it dropped into a hedge of points, and Lin Feiyun was still trying to get her other foot under her. Her knees would not do it fast enough.

Mei had spent longer than the village had stood being careful. Being small. Being a useful pair of hands and nothing more, so that no one would ever look at her as the beast was about to make them look. She had left a hundred fires to keep that carefulness whole.

She did not think about it at all.

She hit Lin Feiyun first, with her shoulder, as gently as anything can be done at that speed. The old woman went over sideways into the gourd leaves with a squawk of pure outrage. Then the porcupine came backing into the place where she had been, and Mei was there instead.

Its rump hit her first. Quills went into her thigh, her hip, and the forearm she threw down to cover herself. It was like being kicked by something with a hundred teeth. She heard herself yell. The porcupine bounced off her, spun, and came again.

On the second pass she had qi in her skin, packed as hard as she could pack it. Half the quills snapped against her. The other half went in anyway.

So she hit it. It was the obvious thing, and she did it well: flat-handed, square into the crown, with qi behind the blow, as she had hit the lion at the waterhole. The porcupine skidded a pace through the gourd vines. Otherwise the blow went into the quills and stayed there. Mei's hand came away with four of them straight through it, their points standing out of the back.

She swore and went for the head.

The head was the only part of it worth going for, small, blunt, and bare of quills, down low at the far end. The porcupine kept it there. She went left, and it turned. She went right, and it turned again, keeping that rattling crown toward her whichever way she came. They went around the gourd row. They went around it again. Gourds burst under its feet. 

"The other end!" Lin Feiyun shouted from the gourd leaves. "Its head, girl!"

"I know which end," Mei said through her teeth.

It backed into her again before she had finished saying it. She took it on the hip and felt three more go in. Her leg had begun to feel like someone else's. The porcupine was blowing hard now, green froth at its mouth, and the rattle never stopped, that idiotic, cheerful sound of seeds in a shell.

She stopped chasing the head.

The next time it came backing at her she did not step aside. She dropped to her knees in the ruined vines, let the crown come over her, and got both hands under its flank, quills and all. They went through her palms. She felt them grate on the small bones. She did not let go. She pulled qi into her arms, all of it she could hold, and heaved.

The porcupine came up off the ground squealing, legs going at the air. Mei turned it over. It came down on its back in the tilled earth, its whole crown driven in beneath it, and for a moment it could not get up. It lay there pinned on its own quills, feet paddling, belly turned up to the sky. The belly was pale, soft, and almost bare.

Mei laid her bloody hand flat on it.

This time she listened first. Under the hot skin the heart was going as fast as a bird's. The rot was in everything, sour and green, a wrongness she could feel the edges of. She did not flood it. She sent in one thread, no more than she would have given a coal, and put it exactly where the heart was.

Something in it came apart. The feet went on paddling a while, slower, then stopped. 

Mei got up. She stood in the wreck of the gourd row with quills standing out of her thigh, her hip, her shoulders, her forearm, and both palms. A few were snapped off short. The rest were as long as her arm and swung when she moved.

I can tell you now what it was. Tier one, and a sorry one at that. In a later age a sect's youngest disciple would have killed it before breakfast and spent the rest of the day complaining about the quills. Mei had never heard of tiers, or sects, or later ages. All she knew was that a porcupine had very nearly taken her apart in a gourd patch.

Mei turned around. Lin Feiyun was sitting up in the gourd leaves where Mei had knocked her. She was not looking at the dead thing. She was looking at Mei, at the quills, and at the blood running out from under them. Mei watched her finish the sum she had been circling for years and reach the total. And Mei felt the old thing come up in her: the leaving, the pack-and-go, the it-was-good-while-it-lasted.
