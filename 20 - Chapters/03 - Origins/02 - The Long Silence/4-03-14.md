It came the autumn Lin Feiyun grew too old to pretend she was not old.

They were at the plot, late in the day, bringing in the last of the gourds before the frost. Lin Feiyun was on her knees in the dirt. Mei was carrying. Then the birds went quiet all at once, the whole edge of the forest gone still, and Mei knew what that meant before the thing came out of the trees, because she had known what that meant since before the village's grandmothers were born.

It was a boar, or it had been. Something had gotten into it, a spirit gone rank in its blood, and it had swelled past the size of any boar, with a green wrongness lit in its eyes. Tier one, perhaps two. The kind of thing that flattens a hunting party and feeds on what is left. It came out of the trees with its head low, and it did not look at Mei, because Mei did not read as prey. It looked at the old woman on her knees in the dirt.

Mei had spent longer than the village had stood being careful. Being small. Being a useful pair of hands and nothing more, so that no one would ever look at her as the beast was about to make them look. She had left a hundred fires to keep that carefulness whole.

She did not think about it at all.

She was between the old woman and the boar before it finished its first stride. She put out her hand, almost gentle, and met a charging, spirit-maddened weight with the flat of it. It was like a river meeting a stone. The boar stopped as though the world had ended at the flat of her hand, which, for it, it had. Something in it came apart. It dropped in the dirt at her feet with all its wrongness gone quiet, and after a moment the birds started up again.

Mei turned around. Lin Feiyun was still on her knees. She was not looking at the dead thing. She was looking at Mei. Mei watched her finish the sum she had been circling for years and reach the total. And Mei felt the old thing come up in her: the leaving, the pack-and-go, the it-was-good-while-it-lasted.

Lin Feiyun got to her feet, slowly, as she got up from everything now. She walked to the dead boar, this woman who had just watched a stranger she took off the road stop a spirit beast with one hand, and she looked it over with a healer's frank appraisal. "Well," she said. "The tusks are worth something. And I'm not hauling it." She looked at Mei. "You'll haul it."

That night, by the fire, she said the rest of it. "I've known a while," she said. "That you're not—" she waved a hand at Mei's unlined face, at the years, at the boar, at all of it. "That you're not like us. I'm old, girl, not blind, and not stupid. I decided a long time ago that it changed nothing I cared about." Mei asked if she wanted her to go. It cost her something to ask, because she already knew the answer everywhere else she had ever been. Lin Feiyun looked at her as though she had said something genuinely foolish. "Why would I want that? Who'd haul the next one?" She poured the tea. That was the last time either of them ever spoke of it.

You have to understand what she gave me, that old woman with the aching hands and the gourds she was too proud of. Everywhere else, being seen had been the end of it. With her it was a boar to haul and a cup of tea. I have been trying to deserve that for a very long time, and I am not certain I ever have.
