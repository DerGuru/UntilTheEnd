After that I stopped letting the roots go deep. I told myself I was only passing through, everywhere, always, and for a long while the telling and the truth stayed close enough together.

There was a boy at a mountain crossing who gave me a whole fish because I had made his sister laugh. He wanted to come with me. I was gone before he woke, which was the kindest thing I had to give him.

There were two sisters who worked a ford, hauling travelers across on a rope raft, arguing without heat from before dawn to after dark. I poled the raft with them through one summer. I have forgotten the river. I have not forgotten the exact pitch of their arguing, which was the sound of two people who would never truly be alone. I left before autumn, before they could count my birthdays.

There was a man who cut flutes from the wing bones of birds and sold them along a trade path. He taught me to play a little. He grew old on that path. I came back that way once, on purpose, and he was gone, and someone else was selling worse flutes off the same stone and did not know his name to tell me.

So I stopped asking. Not out of coldness, not yet. Out of arithmetic again. A name is a handle you fit to a person so you can carry them with you, and I was already carrying too many, and none of them fit anything I could keep. So I let the names go by. I learned faces instead: hands, habits, the small ways one person was unlike the next. I told myself I would remember what mattered without them.

I was wrong about that too. But it would be a long time before I learned how much a face needs a name to stay a face, and how fast, without one, it goes to weather.
