The old woman taught her the fire by the only method she had, which was to hand it over and expect it to be carried.

Not how to make it. Anyone could make fire, she said, in a tone that put making fire somewhere below remembering to breathe. What took a lifetime was keeping one. She showed Mei how to choose a coal, how to pack it in the right dry moss inside a horn of bark so it would live a whole day's walk in the dark of itself and wake to a breath at the far end. She was fierce about it. She cuffed Mei on the ear the first time a coal died on her watch. It was an old woman's cuff, more insult than injury. Mei, who had killed the strongest man in her world with one open hand, took it and packed the next coal better.

She could have broken every bone in that thin arm without meaning to, and she took the cuff instead. The old woman did not strike her as a threat strikes, or as a leader. She struck her as you strike someone you have decided to keep. No one had ever decided to keep Mei before. She had no idea what to do with it, so she packed the next coal better and called that obedience, when it was something else entirely.

The seasons came round to the same low water, and then again, then past counting. And the fire-keeper got old in front of her.

It happened slowly enough that each change looked like nothing. Her hands took longer at the wood. She sat to do what she had done standing. A cough came one cold season and did not leave with the cold. Her voice wore down to paper. Through all of it Mei did not change. The old woman watched her not change with those frank, unbothered eyes, and one evening she said it plainly, because she said everything plainly. "You're the same. Ten winters, and you're the same girl who split my wood wrong." Mei said nothing. "I'm not asking. I'm too old to be frightened of a thing that hasn't hurt me yet. I only wanted you to know I saw it." And she went back to her fire.

She did not last that winter out. The cough took her chest and then her strength. Near the end she could not keep her own fire, and Mei kept it for her, which was the only thing either of them could think of that was worth doing. Mei sat with her the last night. There was nothing to be done, and they both knew it. So she did the one thing there was, which was to be there and keep the fire high enough for the old woman to see. Near the end the old woman was not troubled. She had kept a fire her whole life and was handing it on. That seemed to be enough for her. Her breathing stopped, and Mei did not mark when. The fire was loud and the old woman was quiet. Somewhere in the night the quiet had become the whole of her.

She buried the fire-keeper as the lake people buried their own, then went down to the water to wash the digging off her hands.

The lake was still that morning. Mei had not looked at herself in a long time; there is rarely a reason to. Her face came up out of the gray water and it was the same face. The same face that had walked down out of the hills ten winters before, thin and young. The old woman had gone from upright to bent to gone in the time it took Mei's reflection to do nothing at all.

She knelt there a while. What she felt was not grief, or not only grief. It was colder than grief and lower down. It did not have a name yet. She looked at her young face in the gray water and understood, for the first time, with no drama about it, that she was going to do this again. That she was going to keep doing it. Every face she let herself sit beside would age, bend, and go into the ground. Hers would keep walking down out of the hills, thin and young, for as long as there were hills.

She did not have the word for what she was yet. It would be a long time before anyone did, and longer still before she believed it. That morning by the lake she had only the fact, cold and plain: a fire can be handed on, coal to coal, forever. She was the coal that never went out. She thought, kneeling there, that this was a lonely thing to be. She had no idea.
