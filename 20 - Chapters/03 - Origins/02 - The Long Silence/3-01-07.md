The old woman taught her the fire by the only method she had, which was to put it in Mei's hands and expect her to do it right. And what the old woman taught her was how to make it. Her way.

Not that Mei couldn't make fire. She had done it since she was a child, by force, tearing the heat out of cold air with her hands and enough anger to spare. The old woman had seen as much in the storm, Mei's whole flood of it poured down into a drowned hearth. She had never in her life had power like that to spend. What she had instead was a lifetime of doing the small thing exactly right, and she set about handing that to Mei through the only thing they shared, the fire.

"Not so hard," she would say, when Mei forced a flame too fast and killed it. "Listen to it first. You're not fighting it." She talked about the fire as if it were a living entity. But Mei sat with her hands in the qi and heard something else underneath the words: a craft she had never bothered to learn, because raw force had always been enough. It had always worked. That did not make it good.

She gave Mei a coal of her own to keep, not because Mei would ever need to carry one, but so she would have to watch one live. Fire, it turned out, had rules. It needed air, but a gust scattered it. It needed feeding, but too much at once buried it. Left to itself it died slowly. Rushed, it died faster. The only way to learn the difference was to sit with one coal through a night, fail it, and begin again. Mei had made a thousand fires but never bothered to understand any of them. Now she studied one until she knew what it was: not a thing she summoned, but a thing that lived, with needs of its own that had to be met in their own order. She had been making fire like a flood makes a valley. Now she had to learn making it with single rain drops.

The old woman was fierce about it. She made Mei bring the same small flame up out of nothing again and again, until she could do it without a sound. She cuffed Mei on the ear the first time she got lazy and ripped a flame up out of the air by main force. It was an old woman's cuff, more insult than injury. Mei, who had killed the strongest man in her world with one open hand, took it and brought the next flame up clean.

She could have broken every bone in that thin arm without meaning to, and she took the cuff instead. The old woman did not strike her as a threat strikes, or as a leader. She struck her as you strike someone you have decided to keep. No one had ever decided to keep Mei before. She had no idea what to do with it, so she brought the next flame up clean and called that obedience, when it was something else entirely.

I have forgotten faces I loved better and knew far longer. Hers I kept. I kept her hands on the fire, and the thing they taught me. I am still using it more than a hundred thousand years later, on things that have nothing to do with fire. She would have told me that I am still trying too hard. And she would be right.

The seasons came round to the same low water, and then again and again, until they went past counting. The fire-keeper got old in front of her.

It happened slowly enough that each change looked like nothing. Her hands took longer at the wood. She sat to do what she had done standing. A cough came one cold season and did not leave with the cold. Her voice wore down to paper. Through all of it Mei did not change. The old woman watched her not change with those frank, unbothered eyes, and one evening she said it plainly. "You haven't aged a day," she said. "Many winters have passed, and you're the same girl who split my wood." Mei said nothing. "I've known what you are since the night you took the fire back out of the lake." And she went back to her fire.

She did not last that winter out. The cough took her chest and then her strength. Near the end she could not even keep her own fire, and Mei kept it for her, which was the only thing either of them could think of that was worth doing. Mei sat with her the last night. There was nothing to be done, and they both knew it. So she did the one thing there was, which was to be there and keep the fire high enough for the old woman to see and feel its warmth. Near the end the old woman was not troubled. She had kept a fire her whole life, and that seemed to be enough for her. It seemed, at the very end, to be the one thing she had wanted all along, and never dared to want out loud. Her breathing stopped. The fire was loud and the old woman was quiet. Somewhere in the night the quiet had become the whole of her.

She buried the fire-keeper as the lake people buried their own. Afterwards she went down to the water to wash the digging off her hands.

The lake was still that morning. Mei had not looked at herself in a long time; there was rarely a reason to. Her face came up out of the gray water and it was the same face. The same face that had walked down out of the hills many winters before, thin and young. The old woman had gone from upright to bent to gone in the time it took Mei's reflection to do nothing at all.

She knelt there a while. What she felt was not grief, or not only grief. It was colder than grief and lower down. It did not have a name yet. She looked at her young face in the gray water and understood, for the first time, with no drama about it, that she was going to do this again. That she was going to keep doing it. Every person she let herself sit beside would age, bend, and go into the ground, while she would keep walking down out of the hills, thin and young, for as long as there were hills.

She did not have the word for what she was yet. It would be a long time before anyone did, and longer still before she believed it. That morning by the lake she had only the fact, cold and plain: a fire can be handed on, coal to coal, forever. She was the coal that never went out. She thought, kneeling there, that this was a lonely thing to be. She had no idea.
