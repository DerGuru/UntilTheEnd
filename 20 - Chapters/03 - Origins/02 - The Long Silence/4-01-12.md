It was about to start raining. The steady gray rain that settles in for days. The door of a house at the edge of a village stood open, giving off a smell she had no name for. The humid air transported the scents. She stopped in the road, caught by her own nose. Then the rain came, but instead of the small drops here and there, which Mei had anticipated, it started pouring down all at once. She was soaked instantly, standing in front of the door, breathing in that smell. A woman looked up from a grinding stone inside and saw a wet stranger dripping on her doorstep.

The woman said the thing that undid her, though it took Mei years to understand it had. She said, "You're letting the rain in. Come or go. But would you shut the door, please?"

Mei came in and shut the door. She meant to stay until the rain let up.

Inside it was dry, close, and warm, so warm after the road that it stung. Steam came up off her shoulders. Water ran out of her hair, down her neck, and onto the packed-earth floor around her feet. The woman looked at the puddle, then at Mei, and reached behind her without getting up. A blanket came across the room and hit Mei in the chest.

"Wring yourself out by the fire," she said. "Not on my herbs."

She did not ask, not yet, where Mei was from or where she was going, the two questions everyone asked, the two questions Mei had a hundred smooth answers ready for. When Mei was dry enough not to drip, the woman looked at her arms, said they were fresher than hers, and put a pestle in Mei's hand.

"Make yourself useful, as long as you're here," she said.

So Mei ground herbs while the woman cut. The leaves went to pulp under the stone, dark and wet. The smell came up out of the bowl so strong that Mei's eyes watered. Green, first. Then bitter. Then, at the bottom of it, something warm she could not name, like bread left near a fire.

"What is it?" Mei said.

She heard herself ask it and nearly stopped grinding. She could not remember the last time she had asked anyone anything.

The woman did not look up. "Bitterleaf. And a root of my own you're not getting the name of. For the boy two doors down. He coughs like a fox." She tipped her head at the bowl. "Finer still. I need a powder, not crumbs."

The rain settled into the expected drizzle and went on. The room filled with the smell. The woman hummed while she worked, tuneless and absent, the sound of a person who has forgotten anyone is listening. Rain ticked on the thatch. The fire settled. Mei ground the leaves finer. A task set into Mei's hands as though she had always been there to do it.

At dark the woman set a bowl of millet in front of her. "Sit," the woman said, and Mei sat. It was hot. It tasted of smoke, salt, and some green thing the woman had thrown in without measuring. Mei found she was eating slowly, to make it last.

The woman sat down across the table with her own bowl. For a while there was only the rain and the sound of two people eating. Then the woman looked at Mei over her bowl, at her wrists, at the hollows under her cheekbones, and something in her face set.

"Where are you from?"

There it was. Mei had a hundred smooth answers for it. She found she did not want any of them. "South of here," she said. "I couldn't say exactly."

"And going?"

"North."

"Why north?"

Mei thought about it. "I haven't been there. And it's the direction I was walking."

"What do you think you'll find there?"

"Nothing in particular."

The woman made a sound that might have meant anything. She reached over, took Mei's bowl, and filled it again from the pot without asking. "When did you last eat something hot?"

Mei opened her mouth to answer and found she did not know. She had eaten what the road put in front of her for a long time now, and only as much as it took to keep walking. It had not occurred to her to look at what that had made of her. She saw it now, in the woman's face.

"Eat that," the woman said. "Slowly. When are you leaving?"

"When the rain stops."

The woman glanced at the door, where the rain had eased but was still coming down, and went back to her own bowl. "Do you have somewhere to sleep?"

"Not yet."

"Then sleep here. By the fire."

She meant to sleep by the door anyway. It was where she always slept, at the cold edge, where a person could be gone before morning without stepping over anyone. The woman looked at the bedroll laid out there, then at Mei.

"I said by the fire," she said. "There's a draft. I'm not nursing you through a chill on top of everything else."

Mei moved her bedroll to the warm side of the hearth. She lay a long time listening to the rain and the woman's slow breathing across the room. Once in the night she got up and banked the fire properly, as she had learned to by a lake a very long time ago, so that it would hold until morning.

In the morning the woman crouched at the hearth, looked at the coals, and grunted. "You know fire."

"A little," Mei said.

People came to the door all that day, out of the rain, with the small hurts a village saves up for its healer. A man with a finger set crooked from an old break. A girl with a burn. The boy who coughed like a fox, carried in on his father's hip. Then a child with a gash across his palm from a flint he had been told not to touch. He would not hold still, so the woman took his wrist, looked around, and held it out to Mei.

"Hold that," she said.

Mei looked at her own hands. She thought of a foot in her lap, red and hot. A child screaming over it.

"Hold it," the woman said again, not unkindly. "He won't break."

Mei took the small wrist. She held it still while the woman cleaned the cut and bound it. The boy howled the whole time. It was an ordinary howl, the kind that stops the moment the hurt is over, and it stopped.

The rain kept falling for about two weeks. And when it finally stopped, Mei did not go.

She told herself that she would stay just a few more days. The roads are mud. The woman can use the hands. But the roads had been mud before and that had never held her. What held her was the smell of the room before the woman woke, the sure small movements of her hands, and the plain fact that she asked nothing but gave anyway. Mei had spent an age teaching herself to want less.

Mei was grinding at the table, and while the woman was out at her plot, Mei caught herself humming. It took her a while to place the tune. A small falling thing with a catch in the middle. She had not known she still had it.

She stopped humming. The last remnants of the rain were gone, the roads dry, and she had no excuses not to leave. But here, over a bowl of crushed leaves, she caught herself wanting to stay.

It frightened her more than the scree slope had. More than the snowmelt river or the lion at the waterhole, more than anything the world had ever done to her body. She had given herself a rule after Kesh: less in, less out, be gone before anything could take hold. That rule had carried her whole through more leavings than she could count. And she was standing in a stranger's warm dry room breaking it on purpose, for a smell, a hum, and a pair of unhurried hands.

No, not a real stranger anymore. That woman's name was Lin Feiyun. 

I still remember her name. One of the few that stuck with me through all the years. I have carried it longer than the languages you speak, longer than the oldest thing you would call old. I had let ten thousand names go by in the years I have been describing, and I would let ten thousand more go by after her. You may wonder why hers is a name I hand you whole, when everyone else since I stopped asking their names reaches you as a hand, or a fire, or a gap where a face used to be. The reason is not a large one. She was the first person who was kind to me for no reason at all. She wanted nothing. She simply had more warmth than her small life needed, and she let the overflow fall on whoever was standing in her doorway. One wet afternoon that was me.

But Mei had to go. And soon. Before she no longer could. 

That night she lay down by the fire with her pack tied and ready beside her.
