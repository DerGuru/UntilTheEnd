The illness came in Lin Feiyun's last winter, and it came quietly, as the bad ones do, a cough that did not break and then a tiredness she could not sleep off. She knew what it was before I did. She had spent her life at other people's bedsides. She was not afraid of it, or if she was, she kept the fear folded small and out of the way like everything else.

I have to be careful here, because it is the part I have understood least kindly about myself. I knew things by then. A hundred sickbeds behind me, herbs from ends of the earth she had never walked. There were things I could have tried. I did not try them. I told her, and told myself, that the illness was past medicine, and that was even true. But underneath the true thing sat another thing I would not look at. To save her would be to keep her, and to keep her would be to do this again in twenty years, or forty, and some part of me that had grown very tired had already begun, without asking my leave, to let her go.

So I did the other thing instead, the only thing I have ever really been able to do for the dying. I stayed. I kept her warm. I brought her water she could barely take and broth she could not, and when she could no longer manage the garden I described it to her instead, what was up, what the frost had taken, and what I would put in come spring. I covered her when she shivered. I fed the fire so she could see it. I sat through the nights, because the nights were when the fear came for people, and I had long since stopped needing sleep. Whatever those last days had in them to give her, I gave. It was not much. It was what there was.

She went out with the winter, near morning. I was awake for it, and still I could not tell you the exact moment, because she left as she had lived, without fuss, sliding under so quietly that the fact of it arrived after she was already gone. One breath was the last. I only knew which by the ones that did not come after.

I buried her at the edge of the garden, in the good soil we had argued over. Then I did a thing I had not done in a very long time. I stayed. Weeks. I tended the beds. I kept her fire. I told myself I was letting the ground settle, letting the winter pass, being sensible about the roads. In truth I did not want to walk out of the last place that still smelled like her.

But one morning I banked the fire, filled my horn from it, shouldered my water, and went. The garden would keep a while without me. I did not look back at it from the ridge. I told myself that was because there was nothing new to see.

I did not notice, walking down out of that valley, that something in me had gone still and would not start again. You would think a thing like that would announce itself. It does not. It happens quietly, some hour when no one is watching the water, and by the time you see it the surface is already hard enough to stand on.

Xu Ran has stopped writing. He notices it a moment after she does, the pen still on the page, the ink pooling at the tip. He does not remember deciding to stop.

The woman is looking into the fire and not at him, as she does when the telling has cost her something she will not name.

He should let it lie. He has learned by now that the questions he wants most are the ones she answers least. But the pen has already stopped, so he says the thing he is thinking.

"Your voice was different," he says. "For that one."

She does not pretend not to understand. "She was kind," she says.

"And dead."

"And dead." The fire pops, and she watches a spark climb and go out. "Longer than some of your languages have been alive. Everyone else from those years comes to you as a hand, or a fire, or a gap where a face used to be. And I still know her name." She says it once more, quietly, like a person saying a word to keep it. "Lin Feiyun."

Then she looks at him, and the door in her face has shut again, and she nods at his pen. "Write it down. So there are two of us who keep it."
