The happy years had people in them too. I have made them sound like a solitude, and mostly they were. But not always. And the not-always is where the story starts to turn.

Some years into her walking, Mei came down out of dry hills one evening into the smell of woodsmoke and wet reeds. She had stopped counting the years by then. The number only mattered if you meant to go back. A lake lay in a fold of the land, long and gray in the failing light. A people lived along its near shore, in shelters of bent poles and hide.

They watched her come, wary of anyone who walks out of the hills alone. But she was young, her hands were empty, and hunger had worn her thin enough to look like need instead of threat. An old woman sat at the large fire. She looked Mei over with the frank, unbothered attention of someone too old to bother pretending, then lifted her chin at the fire, which is a gesture that has meant the same thing in every tongue Mei will ever learn. *Sit.*

So Mei sat. The old woman handed her a strip of smoked fish without a word and watched her eat it. When it was gone, she said, "You split wood?" Mei barely understood the foreign sounds but enough of it, to get the gist. She nodded. "Then you'll eat," the old woman said. That was the whole of the bargain, and it held for a long time.

The fire was hers. Mei understood that before she understood anything else about them. The old woman fed it, banked it, and carried its coal when her people moved. All the others arranged themselves around her without seeming to decide to. She had kept it, she told Mei one night, since she was a girl younger than Mei looked. Not the same flame, but the same fire, mothered from coal to coal across her whole life. She said it as other people spoke of their children.

Mei filed it away as a thing she would never need. She could make her own fire any night, with nothing but her hands and enough anger to spare. Carrying an ember seemed like work invented for people who couldn't.

She was wrong about that, and it took her a long while to see how wrong. For the People, fire was not a thing you made when you wanted it. It was a long, kneeling labor of spun sticks and coaxed breath. A wet spell could beat it for days, and wet spells, by that lake, came often and hard. A coal kept alive was the difference between a warm night and a killing chill. The old woman was not hoarding a skill. She was carrying all their tomorrows, and she knew it. That was why her fire mattered more than any man's spear.

Mei stayed. She learned their words. The word they used for enough, was the same as their word for good. She set fish traps in the reeds and was absurdly pleased when they held. She learned which of the children were brave and which were only loud. She learned which of the men would work and which would stand near the work looking busy. She learned it all as you learn a house you mean to live in.

The old woman ran the fire and, through the fire, most of the rest of them, though she would have laughed at anyone who said so. She did it by knowing things. She knew when the fish would come into the reeds and when they would go. She was right often enough that being right had stopped being remarkable. She knew which cough would pass and which would not. She knew the weather in her knees. When two families quarreled over a net, they did not go to the strongest man. They came and stood near her fire, talking loudly about it until she told them, without looking up from her work, what they were going to do. Then they did it. She was not gentle about it. "You're both wrong," she would say, "and now you're loud as well." And that would be the end of it.

She had outlived a good deal. Mei worked that out slowly, from what was missing and never spoken of. There was no man at her fire and had not been for a long time. There were no children of hers among the children, though half of them treated her fire as a second home. She had buried more of her own than she said, and she said none of it. What she had instead was the fire. She mothered it as other women mothered the living: fed it before she fed herself, woke in the night to check it, and carried its coal against her chest in a horn of bark when the people moved, so that not one cold dawn ever found them without it. It had outlived everyone she had started it for. She spoke of it, when she spoke of it at all, as the one child that had never disappointed her.

Once or twice, watching her feed the coal into a new night's fire, Mei felt something. A thread of the warm, thin as a hair, running from the old woman's cupped hands down into the ember, exactly as it ran out of Mei's own hands when she made fire from nothing. It was so faint she decided she had imagined it. Nobody she had met so far carried the warm. She had walked among many and never once felt it in another living hand.

But there was another thing, and that one she could not explain away. The old woman did more with her thread than Mei had ever done with the flood of hers. Mei made fire like she did everything, by force. She reached in with all of it and tore heat out of cold air. The air gave way because it had no choice in the matter. The old woman had nothing like that to reach with. What she had was a long life of doing the small thing exactly right. She knew exactly when to breathe on a coal and when to leave it be. She knew how little was enough. She was miserly with her thin warm, and got more out of one banked ember than Mei got out of a bonfire. Mei watched those knotted old hands and understood, with some discomfort, that the old woman was better at this than she was. Not stronger. Better. Mei had not been the clumsy one in a very long time.

In the evenings Mei sat in the ring of that fire among people who had decided, for no reason she had earned, that she belonged in it. The old woman would talk while she worked a hide soft or mended a trap, a low unhurried stream of it, half to Mei and half to the fire. Mei would split the wood and listen, putting in a word now and then to keep it going. It was nothing. It was the smell of fish and smoke. An old woman's voice. The children going quiet one by one as they dropped off where they sat. Some nights the old woman said something so dry and so true that Mei laughed before she could stop it, a real laugh, up from somewhere she had forgotten she owned. The old woman would look pleased with herself and pretend she had not been trying. Mei did not once think about leaving.

The storm came in a late summer years later. It was almost autumn and the leaves already started turning red and yellow. It came at dark and it came down all at once, off the lake, with almost no warning. Not rain so much as the lake flung back at them, sheet on sheet, on a wind that bowed the shelter poles. The People pulled the children under hides, held the frames down with their own weight, and waited it out. Mei sat among them and felt little. She had outlasted worse than weather.

The old woman had not come in. Mei found her out at the fire in the teeth of it, on her knees in the mud, curled over the little hollow where the fire lived, holding her own back up against the wind like a wall, a thin steam rising from it. It was not enough. A gust off the water took the fire like a snuffed candle. The hollow flooded. The coals scattered and drowned. Her whole life's fire was out in a breath.

Yet, she did not cry out. She put her old hands down into the drowned hollow as though warmth might still be found there. And then Mei felt it. Real, this time, unmistakable, because it was the only warm thing left in all that streaming dark. The old woman was pushing at the dead coals with something that was not her hands: a thread of the warm, running out of her into the wet ruin, straining to wake an ember that was already gone. She had done it like this her whole life, and never once known it for a thing at all. She thought her gift was only knowing fire. She thought that what she did now was only wishing. But Mei had spent longer than the people around her had existed learning the taste of that exact thread, because it was like her own. And here it was in another living hand at last, thin, old, and failing.

That was what broke her carefulness. Not the cold, not the frightened children. She had walked away from many fires rather than let a soul see what she was. But she had never once found another hand that carried the warm. She would not kneel there and watch the only one she had ever met fail at the one thing Mei could do without trying.

She knelt in the mud beside her. She put her hands into the ruin beside the old woman's. She did in full what the old woman had strained toward and could not do. Where the old woman had a thread, Mei had a flood, and no technique at all to soften it. She gathered the warm, pushed it down into the wet, and held it there until the wood forgot how drowned it was. An ember woke under her palm. Then a flame, small and impossible, standing up out of the mud where nothing had any business burning.

To the People it was only that their fire woman had a gift for fire, and thank whatever they thanked. They would tell that story the rest of their lives: the wet year the fire went into the lake, and the fire woman coaxed it back. They never saw the warm. There was nothing in them to see it with.

But the old woman saw. She sat back on her heels in the rain and looked at Mei. For a long moment she did not speak. Then she said one word, low, more breath than voice. *Qǐ.*

Mei did not have that word. Her own people had a sound for *how*, and it was nothing like this small hooked thing thrown up at the end. She did not know what it meant. She only knew she liked it, and that it seemed to belong to the thread between their hands more than any word she owned. So she took it. From that night to this one, through every tongue since, I have called the warm *qi*, and I have never once wanted another name for it.

It was a long time before I understood what the old woman had really asked. *How can this be.* By then the name had set, and it did not trouble me, because it fit better than she could have known. Every soul who has ever watched the impossible done with qi has asked her question, in her tone, and never once guessed they were quoting a dead woman by a lake.

"Yes, qi. I call it warm, but I like the sound of qi. You were doing it too," Mei said. She had not meant to speak. It was the first time since the People that she had said a word to anyone about it, but it came out of her anyway, because she could not bear to be the only one who knew what had passed between them.

The old woman chuckled. "Doing what?"

"Pushing the warm or qi into the fire. Every day of your life. That is why it never goes out for you." Mei heard how it sounded and did not stop. "It was you, wasn't it."

The old woman was quiet for a while. The rain was going off. "I always thought it liked me," she said. Then she reached toward the flame and did it on purpose. She reached with whatever she had used all her life and never named. The fire leaned toward her hand, half a breath of it bending against the wind. Then she stopped and became nothing more than a tired old woman with wet hair, far into her life.

They made nothing more of it. There was no word for what they were. She was too near the end to become something new, and Mei had long stopped expecting the world to explain itself. But a thing had passed between them in that mud that neither had ever had from anyone. The old woman had spent a lifetime as the only one who could keep the fire. Mei had carried qi for even longer, and started to wish she didn't. For one wet night, each of them was less alone. It did not last. But it happened, and I have kept it, where I have lost so much else.

It was small and warm, that life by the lake, and she let herself believe there would be more of it. That was the mistake. Not the staying. The believing that staying was a thing the world would let her keep.
