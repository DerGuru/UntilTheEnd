The happy years had people in them too. I have made them sound like a solitude, and mostly they were. But not always. And the not-always is where the story starts to turn.

Two or three years into her walking, she came down out of the dry hills one evening into the smell of woodsmoke and wet reeds. She had stopped counting the years by then. The number only mattered if you meant to go back. A lake lay in a fold of the land, long and gray in the failing light. A people lived along its near shore, in shelters of bent poles and hide.

They watched her come, as people watch anything that walks out of the hills alone. But she was young, her hands were empty, and hunger had worn her thin enough to look like need instead of threat. An old woman sat at the largest fire. She looked Mei over with the frank, unbothered attention of someone too old to bother pretending, then lifted her chin at the fire, which is a gesture that has meant the same thing in every tongue Mei ever learned. Sit.

So Mei sat. The old woman handed her a strip of smoked fish without a word and watched her eat it. When it was gone, she said, "You split wood?" Mei said she could. "Then you'll eat," the old woman said. That was the whole of the bargain, and it held for a long time.

The fire was hers. Mei understood that before she understood anything else about them. The old woman fed it, banked it, and carried its coal when the people moved, and the others arranged themselves around her without seeming to decide to. She had kept it, she told Mei one night, since she was a girl younger than Mei looked. Not the same flame, but the same fire, mothered from coal to coal across her whole life. She said it as other people spoke of their children.

I have carried a great many fires since. I want you to know that she was the first person who taught me how, and that I did not thank her for it, because it did not once occur to me that she was giving me a thing I would keep.

Mei stayed. She learned their words for water, fish, and cold. She learned the word they used for enough, which was the same as their word for good. She set fish traps in the reeds and was absurdly pleased when they held. She learned which of the children were brave and which were only loud. She learned which of the men would work and which would stand near the work looking busy. She learned it all as you learn a house you mean to live in.

The old woman ran the fire and, through the fire, most of the rest of them, though she would have laughed at anyone who said so. She did it by knowing things. She knew when the fish would come into the reeds and when they would go. She was right often enough that being right had stopped being remarkable. She knew which cough would pass and which would not. She knew the weather in her knees. When two families quarreled over a net, they did not go to the strongest man. They came and stood near her fire, talking loudly about nothing until she told them, without looking up from her work, what they were going to do. Then they did it. She was not gentle about it. "You're both wrong," she would say, "and now you're loud as well." And that would be the end of it.

She had outlived a good deal. Mei worked that out slowly, from what was missing and never spoken of. There was no man at her fire and had not been for a long time. There were no children of hers among the children, though half of them treated her fire as a second home. She had buried more of her own than she said, and she said none of it. What she had instead was the fire. She mothered it as other women mothered the living: fed it before she fed herself, woke in the night to check it, and carried its coal against her chest in a horn of bark when the people moved, so that not one cold dawn ever found them without it. It had outlived everyone she had started it for. She spoke of it, when she spoke of it at all, as the one child that had never disappointed her.

In the evenings Mei sat in the ring of that fire among people who had decided, for no reason she had earned, that she belonged in it. The old woman would talk while she worked a hide soft or mended a trap, a low unhurried stream of it, half to Mei and half to the fire. Mei would split the wood and listen, putting in a word now and then to keep it going. It was nothing. It was the smell of fish and smoke. An old woman's voice. The children going quiet one by one as they dropped off where they sat. Some nights the old woman said something so dry and so true that Mei laughed before she could stop it, a real laugh, up from somewhere she had forgotten she owned. The old woman would look pleased with herself and pretend she had not been trying. Mei did not once think about leaving.

It was small and warm. She thought nothing of it, because she believed there would always be more of it. That was the mistake. Not the staying. The believing that staying was a thing the world would let her keep. She had found, she thought, the pattern her whole new life would take: from one warm fire to the next, welcome, useful, and home for as long as she cared to be. She was right about the fires. She had the rest of it exactly backward, and the old woman at the center of that one would be the one to begin teaching her how.
