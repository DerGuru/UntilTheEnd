The pack stayed tied beside her bedroll for most of that season. Lin Feiyun stepped over it every morning on her way to the grinding stone and never once said a word about it. Then one evening a man carried his daughter in with her forearm bent where no forearm bends, and Lin Feiyun needed something to bind the splint. Mei untied the cord from her pack and handed it over. By the time she thought about it, it was wound three times around a child's arm.

After that she stopped pretending she would leave soon.

I could give you the years that followed as a list of what they did. It would sound like nothing. It was everything to me. They rose before the village did. Mei went down for water while it was still dark. By the time she came back in out of the cold, Lin Feiyun would already be at the grinding stone, working a paste of something green and humming. In time it was Mei's tune she hummed, and she never once got the catch right. She would push a bowl of millet across the table with her elbow without looking up, and after they ate, they started their day. 

Lin Feiyun kept a plot at the edge of the trees. She worked it herself, bent-backed and stubborn. Half the village would have done it for the woman who set their bones and pulled their children into the world, but she refused. She grew her medicine there. She ground it into pastes and teas. Along the sunny edge she grew food: millet, beans, and gourds she was unreasonably proud of. She was a healer first and a farmer second, but she would have told you the two were one trade. Things that grow. Things that mend. She had no patience for anyone who thought either was beneath the other.

When the next spring came, they made the garden together.

It is a strange thing to be older than every tree in the world and never once to have planted a seed. Mei had seen more plants than Lin Feiyun would ever see. Yet she had never put a seed in the ground and stayed to find out what it did. So she pushed the first seeds in deep with her thumb and packed the earth down hard over them with her fist.

She had done a dozen when Lin Feiyun stopped her.

"Not like that. You bury them like you're ashamed of them." Lin Feiyun dug one back out and held it up between finger and thumb. It was a gourd seed, flat and pale. "This one can climb a knuckle to the light. No farther. Any deeper and it rots in the dark before it gets there." She set it again and firmed the soil over it only slightly with her flat hand instead of a fist. "Every seed has its own depth. You'll learn them."

"I thought I'd protect them from the birds," Mei said.

"That would protect them, sure. But put this deep, they never come up at all." Lin Feiyun brushed the soil off her fingers. "Birds you keep off with thorn brush, and somebody near enough to throw a stone. Once they have sprouted and grown a bit, the birds will let them be. And as they grow up, they grow down as well. Building roots to hold and nourish them. But nothing manages on its own the first season. It wants watering. It wants watching." She sat back on her heels and looked at her.

Mei had no answer to that. She took the next seed from the dried gourd shell where Lin Feiyun kept them over the winter, and set it as she had been shown.

The gourds came good that year. Lin Feiyun had been threatening them for years. Every spring she sowed them, told them this was their last chance, and got nothing back but knuckles. Mei watered them, kept the beetles off them, and left their roots alone. That autumn the vines hung heavy with fruit the size of children's heads, pale green, ridged, and absurd. Lin Feiyun carried one in from the plot like a midwife and set it on the table in front of Mei.

"Say it's a fine gourd," she said.

Mei looked at it for a long moment. It was the first thing she had ever grown.

"It's a fine gourd," she said. It came out rougher than she meant it to.

"It is a fine gourd," Lin Feiyun agreed, satisfied, as though Mei had needed convincing.

They ate them through the winter. Mei kept this one on the shelf and looked at it every morning until they ate it too. It was a perfectly ordinary gourd. I still remember the taste.

Lin Feiyun taught her herbs without ever calling it teaching. Which leaf for a fever. Which root for a cough that would not break. Which mushroom fed you, and which of its twins fed the ground with you: serrated at the edge to live, smooth to die. Never a smooth one, however sweet it smelled. Then the rest of the trade. How to set a bone so it healed straight, how to turn a breech child, and how to sit with someone who was not going to live, making the leaving smaller by not being afraid of it yourself. I have taught pieces of that trade to more people than I can count since, in more tongues than you will ever learn, and I never once told any of them where I learned it. Mei had killed more people than the village held. But in that house she learned how to keep them alive and how to let them go. The second was harder.

There was a winter the fever came through, the bad kind, and they did not sleep for a stretch of days. They worked side by side in one smoky house after another, Lin Feiyun's hands and Mei's hands. When it was over, when the village had lost three where it should have lost twenty, they walked home in the gray dawn too tired to speak. Lin Feiyun stopped in the road. She took Mei's face in both her cold hands, looked at her a moment, and said, "I'm glad the rain brought you." Then she let go and they went inside. She slept a day and a night. She never said a thing like that again. She did not need to. Of all the words in all the tongues Mei has swallowed since, she kept those.

The years went by and did what years did to everyone except Mei. Lin Feiyun's black hair went iron, then white. Her hands, so sure at the grinding stone, began to ache in the cold and to fumble the small work. And Mei did not change. She saw Lin Feiyun begin to see it, caught her once or twice looking across the fire with the particular still attention of a woman adding a sum and not liking the total. Lin Feiyun said nothing. Mei said nothing. It sat between them, the one unsaid thing in a house that had no others. Mei waited, with the old cold waiting she knew too well, for the evening it would have to be spoken, and for the leaving she was certain it would bring.

I have been fair to almost no one in this telling, least of all the girl I used to be. Let me be fair to her here. She knew what she was doing. She knew every one of those good years was a debt that would come due. She knew that every season she stayed, her roots went down farther than she could dig them out, and that the deeper they went, the more it would hurt to pull them. She stayed anyway, burying herself in that small warm life as if she were ashamed of how much she wanted it. She did it with her eyes open. It was the bravest thing she ever did, and no one will ever know to call it that but me.
