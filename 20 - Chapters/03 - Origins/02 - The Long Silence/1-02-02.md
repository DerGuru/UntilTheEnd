The years that followed are the ones that people most likely would want me to skip. Nothing seems to have happened in them. No war. No journey. No one you have ever heard of. Just a woman at a fire, slowly becoming a stranger to the people around her. But that is the stretch I would have you sit through with me, Xu Ran, because this is where the thing was done that shaped all the rest. And it was done so slowly that she never felt it being done. Only in hindsight do I recognize it myself. So watch her, then. It is easier if you watch her, and not me.

The winter the game went thin, a hunter came to Mei's fire in the dark. He crouched at the edge of the light where the heat did not quite reach, and waited to be noticed. His name was Teo, and he was young. He had been born after she took the People, which meant he had never known a world in which she was anything but the woman in the best seat. It made him more careful than the older ones had ever been.

"Speak," she said.

"Three days, no deer." He kept his eyes on the fire. "The children —" He stopped. They did that around her, started a thing and let it fall, as though a whole sentence might be heard as a demand. "The children are hungry."

She closed her eyes and let the warm go out of her, across the snow, past the sleeping camp, up the white slope to the north where the cold sat heaviest. She felt them there. A knot of small lives yarded up in the deep drifts past the ridge, breathing against the cold, waiting out the same winter as everyone else.

"Past the ridge," she said. "Where the pines break the wind. Go at first light, take four men, and you will find them."

Teo looked at her then, one flick of the eyes, and in it was the question none of them ever asked aloud. How do you know. She looked back and did not answer. He dropped his gaze, nodded, and backed out of the light.

They found the deer where she said. They always found them where she said. The men came home at dusk dragging the carcasses through the snow, loud and proud. By the time the meat was on the fire, the finding had become theirs. Their tracking. Their eyes. Their long cold walk. She let them have it.

That was the whole trick of it, and I will tell you now that she believed she was being kind. Or she told herself so, which is not the same thing, and she knew it even then. A man who thinks he fed his children stands straighter than one who knows a monster did it. So she read the world for them and handed them the reading. She let them keep the doing, so that no one ever had to watch her lift a full-grown stag with one hand. They ate. The children got their color back. No one thanked her. She had stopped expecting it, and by then the not-expecting did not even leave a bruise.

I am compressing, you understand. There were forty winters like that one, near enough. If I gave you each of them you would close the book, and be right to. But do not mistake the sameness for stillness. Something was moving in those years, slow as ice down a mountain and just as hard to feel while you are standing on it. The ones who had known her were dying. And the ones being born had never known her as anything but the thing at the fire.

Koh brought her the news of each of them as they went. She was the only one who still came to the fire uncalled, and the only one who still said things that were not requests. She was going gray by then. The hands that had climbed every tree and thrown truer than any of the men had begun to knot at the knuckles when the cold came.

"Asa died," she said one night, lowering herself down with a grunt she would once have been ashamed of. "In her sleep. Lucky."

"I felt it," Mei said. She had. A small warmth two huts over, guttering out before the light came. She felt all of them go now. It was one of the many things she did not say.

The fire spat. A dog turned over somewhere in the dark.

"You don't get older." Koh was not asking. She had said it before, in one form or another, across the years, and long ago it had stopped being wonder. "I keep thinking I'll catch you up. Then a winter goes, and I'm further behind."

Mei said nothing. There was nothing to say that was not a cruelty or a lie.

"The young ones are afraid of you," Koh said. "My own girl won't walk past your fire after dark." She said it in her plain flat way, but she said it to the flames, and not to Mei. Even Koh. Even those eyes, that had never once in a whole life looked away from a thing, had learned that the fire was easier to look at than Mei's face.

"I know," Mei said.

"I told her you saved my life once, when we were small." The corner of Koh's mouth moved. "She thought I made it up to feel important." The almost-smile went out. "She grew up with you already like this. To her you were always the thing at the fire. She never knew there was a Mei underneath it."

And there it was, the whole of it, laid down plain by the only person left who could lay it down. To the girl there was no before. Koh was the last place the before still lived, and the cold was getting into Koh's hands.

Let me show you the girl, since Koh has raised her. Koh's daughter. The same round-faced baby who had leaned on Koh's side the night Mei walked out of the trees, killed a man, and sat down in his place. The girl had grown while Mei wasn't watching, as they all did, and she had a child of her own now, a fat toddling thing that had not yet been taught the one lesson every other soul in that camp had learned before it could speak.

It came to her one night at the fire. It came straight for the light on its unsteady legs, as small things go for anything bright, and it reached up toward her face with two fat hands, as though her face were a good thing, a thing a person would want to touch.

Its mother crossed the whole camp in three strides.

She snatched the child back and pressed it to her chest. She would not raise her eyes. Into its hair she breathed the thing they all breathed to their children now, the thing Mei was never meant to hear and heard every time.

"Not her. Don't wake her."

The child, who had done nothing, who had wanted only the light, began to wail.

Thirty years Mei had sat among those people and never once lifted a hand against a child. Still the mothers taught their babies to fear her before they taught them to walk. She did not blame the woman. Standing where that woman stood, she would have taught her own the same. But she sat with the fire on her face, the child's fright still souring the air, and the thought came to her that she never afterward entirely set down. I could lift that baby with one hand, and it would scream the roof down. There is no one in this whole camp I could hold.

Her own mother died that spring.

Mei felt it happen from across the camp, at midday, with her hands in a basket of roots. The thin, familiar warmth she had circled her whole childhood and never once been let inside simply went out, like a lamp no one had thought to fill. She set the basket down. She crossed to the body and sat. The other women drew back to leave her the room they believed she wanted. She waited to feel the thing a daughter is meant to feel.

What came was the old cold nothing.

Her mother had stood in that same clearing a lifetime before and watched them drive her only child into the trees to die. She had kept her eyes on the ground the whole while. Whatever rope there had once been between them was cut long before the woman's heart stopped, and both of them had always known it. Mei buried her with her own hands, because no one else would come close enough to help. She felt the size of the not-feeling. And she understood, standing over the turned earth, that there was exactly one person left alive whose death would reach her.

And that person's knuckles were swelling in the cold.

That is how it is done to a thing like me, Xu Ran, and the cruelty of it is that no one ever has to choose to do it. No door is slammed. A door is only never opened, then forgotten, then walled over so smoothly that the young ones never learn a door was ever there. She did those people nothing but good for forty years, and let the years do the rest, and by the end she was a story their children told one another to feel brave in the dark.

And the last person alive who could still say her name as though it belonged to a girl, and not to the thing at the fire, sat across the camp with a half-mended net going slack in her lap. Gray now. Her hands slow. Looking away every time their eyes met, as all the rest of them looked away.

She stayed anyway. You already know she did. As long as Koh was there to look away from her, there was one person left in the world who had known her before, and she was not ready yet to be no one's. She would not be ready for a long time. And by the time she was, Koh was a long time gone.
