In the red-stone country the people lived in the mouth of a cave above a river, and a young boy found her before she had properly arrived. He came down the slope at a hopping run, one foot turned in, and planted himself in her path to look at her. He was seven, perhaps, all knees and questions.

"You came from over there," he said, pointing at the whole horizon behind her. "What's there?"

"More of this," Mei said.

He seemed to find that a fine answer. He walked beside her the rest of the way up, talking. By the time they reached the cave she knew his foot had been born that way, that he could throw a stone farther than his brother anyway, and that one day he meant to catch a wolf cub to raise as his own. It would follow him everywhere. Then the others would see.

She meant to stay a season and keep him at arm's length with the rest of them. He followed her down to the water every morning. She did not send him back. He told her his name the first day, again the second, and most days after that, in case she had lost it. She let it go by every time.

She walked slower than she needed to on the way down, so his foot could keep up. She told herself it was for his foot.

He talked the whole way, about the wolf mostly. What it would eat. Where it would sleep. Whether it would let his brother touch it, which it would not. One morning he stopped on a rock halfway down and turned to her with the face of someone about to ask the most important question there was.

"If you had a wolf," he said, "what would you call it?"

"I wouldn't have a wolf."

"But if you did."

She thought about it longer than the question deserved. The river went on below them. "Koh," she said.

He wrinkled his nose. "That's not a wolf name."

"No," Mei agreed. And she laughed, because he looked so offended on the wolf's behalf, a short surprised sound that came up out of her before she could stop it. He grinned. He had made her do it. He knew it, and he was pleased with himself all the way down to the water.

It came at the water on a gray morning late in the dry season, when the river had shrunk to a chain of pools and every animal in the red country came down to the same few to drink. The boy was ahead of her, as he always was on the way down, hopping from stone to stone on his good foot and talking over his shoulder. Mei was watching him and not the reeds.

The lion came out of the reeds low, silent, and so fast that the boy was still mid-word when it hit him.

It was a cave lion, a great pale male gaunt from the dry months. It took him down onto the stones with one forepaw and stood over him with its head already lowering. Mei was already running. There were people on the slope above. Women at the cave mouth. A man with a spear halfway down the path. She knew exactly where every one of them was, and she knew what they would see if she reached for qi. So she did not reach for it. She went in with her body alone, as anyone would have, and hit the lion from the side with every bit of her weight behind her shoulder.

It was like running into a hillside in motion. The lion rolled off the boy, came up already turning, and the turn ended with its jaws in her shoulder.

She heard the bone go. Then she felt it. The teeth went in above the collarbone and closed. The whole weight of the animal came down through them, through her, into the stones. Her knees hit rock. Its breath was in her ear, hot and rank, a wet roar with no end to it. It shook her. Her arm came out of its socket with a pop she felt in her teeth.

She got her other hand into the fur at its throat and pulled. She could not move it. She could no more have moved it than the river. Claws found her back and raked down it. Her own blood ran warm across her ribs, then cold. The world closed to the weight, the breath, and the grinding at her shoulder. Somewhere past the mane the boy was screaming.

She tried to call his name. She did not have it.

The lion let go of her. It let go because the boy was moving, crawling backward over the stones on his elbows with his bad foot dragging, and a second kill meant more meat than the one already down. It stepped over her toward him. Mei was on her knees in her own blood with one arm hanging. She watched it lower its head.

Then she stopped caring who saw.

She did not reach for qi so much as let go of everything that had been holding it back. It came up through her all at once, the whole raw flood she had spent years by a lake learning not to use. She put all of it into the flat of her one good hand and drove that hand into the lion's side.

Its ribs went in. The sound was a dry branch breaking under water. The lion was dead before it finished turning toward her, and it came down across the boy's legs. The boy screamed again, but this time it was only fear.

Mei dragged the carcass off him one-handed. Then she sat down hard on the stones, because her legs would not hold her any longer, and the healing began.

It was not quick. It never was, in the body; only in the telling. She felt the shoulder grind back toward itself under the torn meat, a slow wet working she could not hurry. The arm found its socket again with a jolt that whited out the morning. The skin across her back drew closed in ridges, then smoothed, as a hand smooths a hide. She sat in the blood and breathed through it with her teeth locked. There were people watching. She would not give them screaming on top of everything else.

They were all watching. The women at the cave mouth. The man with the spear, who had come all the way down and stopped ten paces off with the spear forgotten in his hands. The boy, sitting up on the stones, staring at her shoulder as it closed.

His mother came down the path at a run. She went past the dead lion without looking at it. She did not look at Mei either, not at first. She scooped the boy up off the stones, bad foot and all. She held him so hard he squeaked. Then she looked. Mei watched the sum go across her face, start to finish, faster than the lake people had ever done it.

It came out somewhere new. The woman set the boy down. She came across the stones, knelt in Mei's blood without seeming to notice it, and pressed her forehead to Mei's. She was shaking. She said something in their tongue that Mei had not learned yet, then said it again, and did not lift her head for a long time.

That night they gave Mei the lion's skin and the place nearest the fire. The boy slept against her side, with his bad foot tucked under her knee for the warmth. She let it stay there.

It lasted most of a moon.

The mother watched her. Mei noticed it in small things at first. The woman would be scraping a hide at the cave mouth when Mei came up from the water, and the scraper would stop. Her eyes went to Mei's shoulder, where the teeth had gone in, and found smooth skin. They went to Mei's back, where the claws had opened her, and found nothing there either. Then they went to her son, hopping up the path behind Mei on his one good foot, and stayed on him a long time.

Once Mei heard her at the fire, low, to another woman, not meaning to be heard. "She mends like water closing. Every mark. Every bone." A pause. "And mine walks like that."

The boy did not hear it. Or he heard it, and it went into him as such things go into children. A few days later, at the water, he sat down on his rock and held his bad foot out to her in both hands, as if offering her something he had found.

"Do mine," he said.

Mei had never mended anything but herself. Her own body did that without asking her, as it always had. She did not know how it was done. But she had learned one thing by a lake about putting qi into something that could break. *Not so hard. Listen to it first.*

So she took his foot in her hands.

She tried to do it as the old woman had taught her. She let out a single thread of qi, no more than she would have given a coal, and sent it into the bones. A foot was not a coal. She did not know what it was telling her. The thread went into the twisted place and found it hard, old, and set, grown that way since before he was born. It would not move. She pushed, a little. Then a little more.

The boy went rigid. For a breath he made no sound at all. Then he screamed, high and thin, a sound with no breath behind it. His whole leg jerked in her hands. She let go at once. The foot lay in her lap as twisted as it had ever been. The skin over the bones had gone an angry red, hot to the touch, as if she had held it in the fire.

He was still screaming when his mother reached them.

She did not say anything. She looked at the foot, red, swelling, and no straighter than it had been. She looked at Mei's hands. Then she looked at Mei. Whatever had been in her face that first morning on the stones was gone. She gathered the boy up and carried him toward the cave without a word.

The boy looked back at Mei over his mother's shoulder the whole way up.

I can still feel that foot in my hands. It would be a very long time before I learned to mend anything but myself.

The looks came after that, the whole camp's, as they always came. A gap opened around her at the fire.

She left a few nights later, before the boy woke.
