She stayed with the lake people after that, longer than was wise, because leaving would have meant admitting the old woman had been the only thing that held her there. She kept the fire. She took the old woman's place at the center of it. For a while the people let her, out of habit and the respect they had paid the old woman.

But the old woman had known her ten winters and made her peace with it. The children had not. They grew, as children grow, and a child measures you against her own growing. She gets taller. You do not. By the time the girl who had once ridden on Mei's shoulders was a woman with children of her own, she had done the arithmetic her elders were too careful to do out loud. She said it to Mei's face one evening, not cruelly, almost gently, which was worse. "You held my mother's hand when she was small. You held mine. And you'll hold my daughter's, and you'll look exactly like this." She was not asking either. None of them ever asked. They only, one by one, arrived at it, and then the looks began. Not hatred. Something more careful than hatred. A gap opened around Mei at the fire, the width of a question no one wanted answered.

She knew the looks. She had grown up inside them, in the tribe, before she was strong: the sidelong measuring, the drawing-back, and the quiet closing-over of a camp that has decided a person is wrong. Last time the looks had come first, then the pointing, then a child driven out into the trees to die. She remembered how that had gone, though the girl it had happened to had worn thin in her memory by then.

So this time she did not wait for the pointing. One night the fire was banked and the lake people were breathing in the dark. She filled a skin with water and walked out of the ring of them while they slept. No argument. No goodbye to spoil. She chose the hour and the door herself. That was the only part of the whole business she was allowed to keep: to be gone before she was sent.

Out in the dark, with the lake going silver behind her and the cold coming up off it, she understood what she had just taught herself to do. She would be doing it for the rest of a life she was beginning to suspect had no end. This was the pattern now. Find the warmth. Take what it gave. Go before it turned.

This is where I stopped asking their names.

Not that night, not all at once. But somewhere in the leavings that followed, I worked the arithmetic myself. There were so many of them by then, each one built the same as this. It was simple. A name is a handle you fit to a person so you can carry them with you. I was already carrying too many, and not one of them fit a thing I was allowed to keep. So I let the names go by. I learned faces instead: hands, habits, and the small ways one person is unlike the next. I told myself that would be enough to remember them by.

It was not. But it would be a long time before I learned how fast a face goes to weather without a name to hold it in place.
