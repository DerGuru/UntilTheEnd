She stayed with the lake people after that, longer than was wise, because leaving would have meant admitting the old woman had been the only thing that held her there. She had been the only one, too, who ever carried qi in her own hands and knew Mei for what she was. Now there was no one alive who did. Mei kept the fire. She took the old woman's place at the center of it. For a while the people let her, out of habit and the respect they had paid the old woman.

But the old woman had known her since she had arrived many winters ago and made her peace with it. The children had not. They grew, as children grow, and a child measures you against her own growing. She gets taller. You do not. By the time the girl who had once ridden on Mei's shoulders was a woman with children of her own, she had done the arithmetic her elders were too careful to do out loud. She said it to Mei's face one evening, not cruelly, almost gently, which was worse. "You held my mother's hand when she was small. You held mine. And you'll hold my daughter's, and you'll look exactly like this." She was not asking anything. None of them ever asked, how Mei did not age. They only, one by one, arrived at it, and then the looks began. Not hatred. Something more careful than hatred. A gap opened around Mei at the fire, the width of a question no one wanted answered.

She knew the looks. She had endured them, in the tribe, during the last years before she left. The sidelong measuring, the drawing-back, and the quiet closing-over of a camp that has decided a person is wrong. Last time the looks had come first, then the whispering, the caution around her. Until finally she had decided to leave for good.

She would have gone that season, if not for the boy. He was the one child who still sat inside the gap, eight or nine winters old and too young yet to have done the arithmetic. He sat as near the fire as the heat would let him. One gray morning, before the camp was up, he crouched over the last of the night's coals with his hands cupped around one and blew. It woke. Mei, lying awake across the fire, felt it: a thread of qi, thinner even than the old woman's had been, a child's thread, in its breath running into the ember. He did not know he did it, but he grinned at the flame.

"It likes me," he said, to no one.

Mei had heard that once before, in the rain, from an old woman with wet hair.

Later she asked whose he was. An old man at the next fire told her, in the tone of a thing everyone knew. "His grandmother was hers," he said, and lifted his chin at the hearth. "The one she buried first."

The old woman had never said. She had said none of it, about any of them. She had loved a fire instead of a line she might have to keep burying, and in all those years Mei never once heard her call the boy's mother her own. 

Mei taught him, quietly, as if it were only the fire. She did not teach him her own way, which had never been worth passing on. She taught him the old woman's: how little was enough, when to breathe on a coal, and when to leave it be. "Not so hard," she heard herself say, the first time he pushed too much into an ember and smothered it. "Listen to it first." She had to stop a moment after that before she could go on. She taught him the keeping as well, the one craft she had never needed: how to choose a coal, how to pack it in dry moss inside a horn of bark so it would live a whole day's walk and wake to a breath at the far end. He would never call fire out of nothing. But he could keep one, as the old woman had all her life, and that had been enough for her. He never knew what Mei was. To him she was only the woman who kept the fire, teaching him to keep it after her.

She did not have long. He was growing, and she meant to be gone before he was tall enough to look at her as the others did.

The evening he woke a coal out of gray ash by himself, she stopped waiting. One night the fire was banked and the lake people were breathing in the dark. She packed a coal in dry moss inside a horn of bark and set it by the boy's sleeping hand. Then she filled a skin with water and walked out of the ring of them while they slept. No argument. No goodbye to spoil. She chose the hour and the direction herself. 

Out in the dark, with the lake going silver behind her and the cold coming up off it, she understood what she had just taught herself to do. She would be doing it for the rest of a life she was beginning to suspect had no end. This was the pattern now. Find the warmth. Take what it gave. Go before it turned.

This is when I stopped asking their names.

Not that night, not all at once. But somewhere in the leavings that followed. There were so many of them by then, each one built the same as this. It was simple. A name is a handle you fit to a person so you can carry them with you. I was already carrying too many, and not one of them fit a thing I was allowed to keep. So I let the names go by. I learned faces instead: hands, habits, and the small ways one person is unlike the next. I told myself that would be enough to remember them by.

It was not. But it would be a long time before I learned how fast a face goes to weather without a name to hold it in place.
