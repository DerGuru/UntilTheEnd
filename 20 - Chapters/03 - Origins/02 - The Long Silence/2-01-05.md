The old woman kept me because I was useful and because, I think, she liked the company of someone who did not already know all her stories. She taught me the fire. Not how to make it. Anyone can make fire. She taught me to carry it.

A coal, chosen right and packed in the right moss inside a horn of bark, will live a whole day's walk in the dark of itself and wake to a breath at the far end. She had carried the same fire, she told me, since she was a girl. Not the same flame, but the same fire, mothered from coal to coal across her whole life. When I understood what she meant I felt something I had no name for. She was handing me a thing older than she was and asking nothing back for it.

I stayed longer than I had stayed anywhere. Long enough to see the seasons come round to the same low water twice, then a third time, then past my counting. And she got old in front of me.

It happened slowly enough that each change looked like nothing. Her hands took longer at the wood. She began to sit while she worked, then to work less, then to sit and let me work while she told me how. A cough came in a cold season and did not leave with it. Her voice went to paper. One spring she did not get up, and she watched the fire from where she lay, and I kept it for her so she would not have to see it go out.

I sat with her the night she died. There was nothing to be done and we both knew it, so I did the only thing there was, which was to be there and keep the fire high enough for her to see. Near the end she was not troubled. She had kept a fire her whole life and was passing it on, and that seemed to be enough. When her breathing stopped I did not catch the exact moment. The fire was loud and she was quiet, and somewhere in there the quiet had become the whole of her.

I buried her as she had shown me her people buried their own. Then I went down to the lake to wash the digging off my hands.

The water was still that morning. I had not looked at myself in a long time, since there is rarely a reason to. My face came up out of the gray water, and it was the same face. The same face that had walked out of the hills however many years before, thin and young and glad. Hers had gone from strong to bent to gone in the time it took mine to do nothing at all.

I knelt there a while. What I felt was not grief, or not only grief. It was colder than grief and lower down, and it did not have a shape yet. I looked at my young face in the water and understood, for the first time and without any drama about it, that I would do this again. That I would keep doing it. The faces around me would age and bend and go into the ground, and mine would keep walking out of the hills, thin and young and glad, for as long as there were hills.
