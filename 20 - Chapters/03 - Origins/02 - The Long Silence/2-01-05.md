For a while after that, the world was the best thing that had ever happened to her, and she ate her way across it.

Not from hunger. Her body had stopped needing much, though she did not yet know how far that went. She walked east, because the morning came from there and she liked walking into it. When the land bent, she bent with it. That was the whole of her method for years, and for a long time it was enough.

She ate things to find out what they were. A white root that snapped like green wood and tasted of pepper and cold earth. The pale inside of a bird's egg, still warm from the nest. A blue berry that numbed her tongue to the root, so that for half a day she talked to herself just to feel the words come out crooked, and laughed alone in an empty valley at the sound of them. She learned which mushrooms sat quiet in the belly and which turned the night into a long argument with the dark. She had time to be wrong. She could eat the wrong thing, be sick against a rock until the stars moved, then wake with the taste still in her teeth and her body already deciding the matter was settled.

She came over a headland one morning and found the sea. She had no word for it. Her People had lived and died hundreds or thousands of miles from any coast. None of the People had ever seen the land simply stop, and go to moving water all the way to the edge of the sky. She stood on the height a long time. Then she went down to it. She walked in to her knees with her clothes on, and the cold of it climbed her like a living thing. A wave she had not thought to respect knocked her flat, rolled her, and spat her back onto the sand with her mouth full of salt. She sat up coughing. She was laughing before she had the breath for it. She stayed there. She learned what the water did with the moon, tasted the strange animals it left in its pockets when it drew back, and slept where its sound could reach her. When she left she took a white spiral shell that a creature had finished with. She carried it for a long time. I could not tell you now what became of it, which is its own small grief.

Nothing the world did to her seemed to stick. She climbed higher than sense allowed and hung there with her fingers burning, because the burning was interesting. The fall did not frighten her. She stayed in a snowmelt river until her legs stopped answering, walked out on stumps she could not feel, and sat in the sun while they came back to her as pain. Cuts closed. Cold let go. Her body took the whole world as a suggestion and declined it.

Once, it went further than that. I want to be careful telling you this, because she was not careful living it.

She was crossing a scree slope above a valley she wanted to see the bottom of, and the ground she trusted was resting on nothing. It went out from under her all at once, a hundred small stones giving way together, and she went with it, arms out, mouth already open around a sound she never finished making.

And the world stopped.

The stones fell with her, and then they did not. They hung around her in the gray light, each one throwing its own small shadow, closer than any stone should hang to a falling body. The dust hung too. She could see every grain of it, suspended, a slow brown haze that should have been falling and wasn't. A bird had been calling somewhere above. The call was not there anymore. Not faded. Just gone. Her own breath was not there either. She could feel her chest caught mid-motion, neither rising nor falling. No wind. No slide. No grind of stone on stone. Only the held weight of everything at once, and the girl inside it, suspended and unhurt, waiting, not knowing what for.

Then the bird's cry continued. A stone came down where a stone should come down, and the world had sound in it again, all at once, roaring back in like a held breath let go. Her shoulder found rock. Her hip found rock. Then she was sliding, tumbling, the slope taking her down in pieces, and there was nothing left to do but try to keep the worst of it from her skull. Her arms went up and over her head, forearms crossed, fingers laced at the back of her neck. It was the only shape she had time to choose. The fall undid it as fast as she made it. Every tumble threw her arms wide again, and every time she dragged them back. She tried to become a ball, knees to chest, spine curved around nothing, and held that shape for exactly as long as it took her back to find something with an edge. Then her spine straightened on its own, a flinch she had no say in. After that she was open again: arms out, legs out, nothing left to protect and no strength left to try.

Stones found her everywhere after that. Her skull. Her ribs, twice, then a third time, none of the hits waiting for the last one to finish. Her shins. The soft of one forearm, hard enough to bend it wrong. She stopped trying to shape herself into anything. There was no shape small enough to stop what was happening to her.

She came to rest on a narrow plateau, bent into angles a body does not choose for itself, breathing in the dust she had raised on the way down, with the bird still calling above her as though it had never stopped. One stone was still coming, larger than the rest, unhurried, the last piece of the mountain to reach her. She watched it fall toward her face with a strange, detached patience, as though the death belonged to someone else.

Then it hit, and everything went black.

When she came to it, she could just lay there, while her body put itself back together, bone by bone, the pain arriving in waves that never quite finished before the next one started. She screamed through most of it until her energy ran out, loud enough to empty her lungs, and did not care who heard. No one came. I could not tell you, now, whether that was because there was no one for miles to hear her, or because the sounds she made were animal enough to keep anyone within earshot from coming any closer. More than once the pain put her under entirely, a mercy that never lasted. She would surface again to find nothing had changed: the same waves, the same waiting, and the healing that had gone on without her. She felt the exact moment a rib knit. A deep, private click, felt nowhere but in the bone itself. It hurt the entire time, steady, without letting up. When she could stand, she looked back up the slope and could not find the place she had started her slide. Too high. Far too high. Nothing should have walked away from it, and yet, she just had.

There was no good word for what she had just survived, so she reached for the one she had. Lucky. Not that she couldn't break. She had just proven she could, thoroughly, in more places than she could count. Maybe only that breaking did not stay on her, not for long. How long had it sayed? It was a thin thing to hang all that pain on, and some part of her knew it even then. But she had nothing better, and the pain was already gone enough that she stopped asking. She turned the thought over once, found no more use in it than that, and set it down. Then she went to find the bottom of the valley, because she had come all that way to see it.

I know what it was, now. She did not, and would not for a very long time. I have decided not to hold that against her. When you have never once been allowed to die, the fact of not dying looks exactly like luck. It goes on looking like luck for as long as you need it to.
