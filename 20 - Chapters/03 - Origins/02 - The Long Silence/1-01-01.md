The girl who took the People did not keep them long. I will say that now, so you are not left waiting for it. She won them in an afternoon, with her hands. She held them a while. Then she walked away, and I have spent a long time since trying to feel what she felt when she did, and came back with almost nothing.

These were the People who cast her out. The same fires, the same faces gone older, the same circle that had once watched a child driven into the trees to die and then turned back to their food. Years later she walked out of those trees, killed the man who ruled them, and sat down in his place, and not one of them could stop her, and not one of them could ever send her away again. That was the thing she had wanted her whole short life, though she had never had the words to say it plainly. To be somewhere no one could make her leave. She got it. It was the loneliest thing that had ever happened to her, and it was only the first time it would happen.

The blood came off eventually. It always did. Mei sat in the dead man's place by the fire and worked it out of the creases of her knuckles while the People pretended not to watch. They were all afraid of her now. She had counted on it. The same fear that had once driven her out into the trees now made her impossible to be driven out at all. What she had not counted on was Koh.

Koh was still alive. Grown now, a young woman, two summers older than Mei as she had always been, still long in the limb, still restless in the hands. When they were small Koh had been the brave one, the loud one, the one who climbed every tree, waded every current, and had never once been afraid of a thing she hadn't tried. She was afraid now. She sat among the women with a small child of her own leaning against her side, watching the flames, and did not come near. When their eyes met across the fire, it was Koh who looked away. Koh, who had never in her life looked away from anything, could not hold Mei's gaze for the length of a breath.

There was a debt between them neither would ever say aloud. When they were children, the big man had taken Koh by the arm, high up, where he liked to leave his marks, and Mei had done the thing she had spent her whole childhood hiding. She had thrown him off his feet without touching him, in front of the whole People. She had saved Koh. But the People had seen what Mei was and driven her out for it, into the dark to die — and Koh had said nothing. Someone had pulled Koh away, and Koh had gone, looking back over her shoulder, and that look was all Mei carried of her for a lifetime. Mei did not blame her. Koh had been a child. There had been nothing a child could do. She had told herself that on many many nights alone in the dark, and had almost come to believe it.

That was what Mei knew, sitting at that fire: that she had loved Koh, and saved her. She had been thrown away for it, and come back too strong to be thrown away again. What she did not know — what she could not have known, and what I have had all the time there is to understand — was that this was the whole of her, laid down complete before she was grown. She would love, and it would cost her, and she would go a little harder into the world, and love again, and pay again. A hundred thousand years of it. And every turn of that wheel began here, with one girl looking back over her shoulder as she was pulled away, and another girl learning, without knowing she was learning anything, that it is safer never to need saving, and safer still never to save. Koh started all of it. Not on purpose. Neither of us understood. But if you want to know where the woman came from that you are writing down, she came from a bruise on a girl's arm, and a friend who looked away.

So she stayed. She told herself the People needed her, and in a way, they did. She told herself she had nowhere else to be, and she didn't. The thing she did not tell herself, the thing she did not know at the time, was smaller and worse: that as long as Koh drew breath, there was one person left in the world who had known her before. Before the trees. Before the warm was a weapon. Before she was whatever she was now and would become even more so. She sat in the warmest seat at the warmest fire she had ever owned, ruling the people who had thrown her away, a stone's throw from the only friend she had ever had, and none of it reached her. They moved around her. They did not touch her. Nothing got in. But she stayed — because leaving meant no one anywhere remembered the girl, and she was not ready, yet, to be no one's.
