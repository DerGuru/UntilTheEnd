After the red country there were more fires than I could give you in a year of nights. Here are some of them, in the order they come back to me.

A fire in a hide shelter, the first winter after the red country. A child there took a fever, the bad kind, and her mother sat up with her three nights running. On the third night the mother looked across the fire at Mei's hands. Mei had told them nothing about herself. It did not matter. On the third night a mother looks at any pair of hands like that. Mei kept hers in her lap until morning. The child lived, as it happened. Mei never learned whether her hands would have helped. She made sure of that.

A fire of driftwood under a white cliff. The women there mended nets in the evenings and sang while they worked, a long song with a joke in the third verse. Every night when they reached it they laughed, and every night Mei laughed a beat after them. One evening the youngest of them leaned over with her netting needle still in her hand.

"You don't get it," she said, delighted, as if she had caught Mei out at a game. "Do you want me to tell you?"

"No," Mei said.

The girl waited for the rest. Then she shrugged and went back to her net. When the third verse came round, Mei laughed in the right place, a beat late. She stayed until the herring stopped running. From the cliff path she looked back once. They were still singing.

A fire in a hide tent on the open tundra. A woman there had twins and too much other work for two arms. Mei carried one of them on her hip all winter. It was a fierce, fat thing that slept against her with a fist wound in her hair and screamed if she tried to unwind it. By spring she could not have said which one it had been.

A fire in a valley of larches, where an old man played a flute cut from a crane's wing bone. He played the same tune every night, a small falling thing with a catch in the middle, and he taught it to her because she was the only one who never asked him to play something else. She stayed three winters. She learned it well enough to play it back to him, badly, and he laughed at her until he coughed. When she went, she hummed it on the road for days. Then for a season. Somewhere on the road she stopped humming it. She did not notice when.

A fire of dung on a plain with no trees. An old woman kept it. The first night Mei watched her feed it for a long while, feeling for a thread in those hands. There was nothing there but hands.

A fire where the people kept a half-tame wolf on a thong. It laid its head in her lap the first night, and every night after. She did not give it a name.

A fire in a wet wood. Good work. Bad smoke. A man who might have been kind, if she had stayed to find out.

A fire where a girl asked her name. Mei gave her one. It was not hers.

A fire by a river. A fever. Three dead, or four. She helped bury them.

A fire on a salt flat. Wind.

A fire in snow.

A fire.

Alone, between them, she made her own fires small. A coal's worth, no more, banked low and out by morning. She had been taught how little was enough. She had it confused with wisdom. Some nights she made none at all. The cold came in, and she let it.

Once, kneeling at a still pool to drink, she saw her face come up out of the water. It was the same face. She put her mouth to it and drank.
