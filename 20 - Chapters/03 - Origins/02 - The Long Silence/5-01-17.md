For a long time after, she went back to the garden. Not to live there. She could not live in that house with Lin Feiyun gone out of it, and the first time she came back the roof had already begun to sag where no one had mended it. But every few years her walking would bend, without her quite deciding it, toward that valley. She would come over the ridge, down to the beds, find them half wild, and put them right. A season of it. Pulling what had choked the good plants. Turning the soil. Setting Lin Feiyun's plants where Lin Feiyun had liked them. It was the one thing she still did for love and not for use. As long as she did it, some part of her was still the woman who had helped make it.

Lin Feiyun lay at the edge of it, where the good soil began. When the day's work was done, Mei sat by the grave with her hands in her lap and told her what was up, what the frost had taken, and where the gourds had come back on their own. 

She could hear her when she worked it. Not a voice from anywhere, just the memory, sharp as a splinter under a nail: Lin Feiyun on her heels in the dirt, a gourd seed held up between finger and thumb. *Every seed has its own depth.* Mei set each one as she had been shown and firmed the soil with her flat hand, never a fist. When the crows came down on the new rows, she was near enough to throw a stone. The memory was warm, and she held onto it, because it was the nearest thing to warmth she had left. Sometimes she hummed while she worked. It was the falling tune with the catch in the middle, and she got the catch wrong on purpose, as Lin Feiyun always had. Those visits kept Lin Feiyun a little alive, and kept the girl who had loved her a little alive too.

The village watched her come and go. At first people brought their small hurts to the garden wall, and she saw to them over it. The woman from the walnut tree kept the grave tidy while Mei was away. The first spring Mei came back, she found her there on her knees, pulling the weeds off it.

"I keep it for her," the woman said, without getting up.

"I know."

"You'll be back?"

Mei looked at the beds. "Some spring."

"Then I'll keep it till some spring." She never asked where Mei went in the years between.

Some other spring there was a second grave beside the first, and the weeds stood on both. A grown man with a farmer's shoulders came down the path to see who was working the old healer's ground. He looked Mei over without a flicker of knowing her. She had last seen him halfway up a walnut tree.

"This is the old healer's ground," he said.

"I know." Mei went on weeding.

"Who are you to her?"

She thought of a dozen answers. "I helped her with it."

He looked at the beds, then at her. "That must have been a long time ago."

"It was."

He watched her a moment longer than he needed to, then went back up the path. Mei told herself afterward that it was his look that kept her away the next spring, and the one after. It was the old rule, after all: go before they notice. It was even partly true.

So the visits came further apart. She stopped noticing when the season was right for them. When she did go she worked faster, stayed less, and did not hum. The house had gone down into a long hump of thatch and nettles. She did not look inside it. She let the crows take the seed. She stopped telling the grave anything. One time she caught herself tending the beds as she would clear any overgrown path, not for Lin Feiyun, only to have it done. She did not think much of that at the time.

A stretch of years went by and one evening, a whole range and a river away from that valley, she bought a gourd from a woman at a ford and stood turning it in her hands, pale and ridged, looking for soft spots. It came to her then that she had not been back in a long while. 

She noticed the thought arrive. She noticed it ask her to turn around. She waited to feel the pull toward the garden, as she had once stood on a pass and waited to feel the pull of leaving. Nothing came. Only the evening, the road going on, and a flat mild readiness to keep walking it. So she did. That night she cooked the gourd and ate it, and afterward she could not have said what it tasted of. She never saw the garden again.

Somewhere on that road the tune went too. She did not notice when.

I have wanted, from here, to reach back and turn that girl around. To make her walk the miles back, kneel in the weeds, and set the beds right one more time, not for the garden but for herself. She would not feel the size of what she left on that road for a very long time, and by the time she felt it, there was no road left to find.
