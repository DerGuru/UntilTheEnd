After her mother, the last of the ones who had known her began to go, and there had never been many to begin with.

There was a hunter, old now, his hip long since failed, who had thrown a stone at her the day they drove her into the trees. He had been young then, strong, and certain the People were right to be rid of the thing she was. He was not certain of much anymore. Some nights he watched her across the fire from the cold edge of it, where his bad leg left him, with a look on his face he never once found the words for.

She felt him going in the deep of a winter night. She could feel all of them go by then, the small warmths guttering out one after another across the camp, and mostly she let them. His she went to. She could not have told you why. She crossed the sleeping camp in the dark and sat down in his shelter, where no one else had cared to sit. He opened his eyes and was not afraid of her, because a man that close to the edge has nothing left that fear can be about.

His mouth worked. Whatever he had carried in his face all those years, it did not come.

"Cold," he said.

"I know," she said.

"The stone." A long pause, while he found the breath. "Was wrong."

"I know," she said again.

It was not an apology. Or it was the only kind of apology a man like that had, in a world like that. She sat with him until the last of the warmth went out of him. It was nearer to being forgiven than anything else the People ever gave her, and she took it, because you take what there is.

The rest went as he did, over the years, taken by the snow, or the fever, or only the weight of their own time. A woman who had been a small girl at the edge of that clearing. A man who had stood in the ring the day she was cast out. Each death carried off another pair of eyes that had ever seen her as anything but the thing at the fire, until the camp was full of strangers who happened to know her name, and there was only Koh.

There was a flat rock above the river where the People stopped in the warm season, and it had been theirs once, hers and Koh's, when they were small. The place where Koh said the things she did not say at the fire. The place where Mei had first shown her the warm, pressing her hands to the stone and pushing the heat into it until Koh could feel it if she held still. Their place. The one part of the world that had ever belonged to the two of them and no one else.

Mei went there the last summer they camped near it. Koh was already on the rock, an old woman now, her feet no longer reaching the water she had once dived into without looking first. She did not startle when Mei sat down beside her. She was perhaps the only living person who would not have.

For a while neither of them said anything. The river went by below, as it always had, indifferent to which of them had aged. Then Koh said, without turning her head, "You should have let him have me."

Mei did not answer.

"That day. You should have let him break my arm, and been a normal girl, and grown old here, and buried me." Koh's voice was flat, worn smooth, the voice of someone who has carried a thing so long it has lost its edges. "I've thought about it every year of my life. You saved me, and they threw you in the trees for it, and I let them. I was a coward. I've been a coward about it for sixty years." She finally looked over. Old face. The same eyes. "And now you're back, and you haven't changed, and I get to look at exactly what it cost you, every day, until I die."

Mei thought about all the things she had told herself in the dark. That Koh had been a child. That there had been nothing a child could do. She had come, over a lifetime, to almost believe them, and sitting on that rock she found that almost was precisely as far as she had ever gotten. She did not tell Koh it was all right. It was not all right. She did not tell Koh she forgave her; she was not certain she did. She said the only true thing she had. "You're the last one who knew me."

Koh nodded slowly, as though that were an answer, which it was. They sat until the light went. It was the last real conversation Mei would have with another human being for longer than the People had existed, and she did not know that, and Koh did, as the old come to know things.

I have had a great deal of time to decide whether I forgave her. I would like to tell you I did, on that rock, in the last of the light, as people do in stories. I didn't. I forgave her a long while afterward, when she was dust and it cost me nothing, which is the only kind of forgiveness I have ever been much good at. She was right about all of it. She should have let him have her. Neither of us would have wanted that, and she was still right, and both of those things have stayed true for a hundred thousand years.
