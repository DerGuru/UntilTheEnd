After enough of those leavings, she made a bargain with herself, though there was no one else to make it with.

The terms were simple. She could not change the ending. Every fire she sat at, she would leave, or bury. That was fixed. But she could change how much of herself she carried to the fire in the first place. If she gave less, she would lose less. If she stayed a season instead of ten winters, if she learned no names, slept a little apart, and never let a child ride on her shoulders, then when the looks came, as they always came, the leaving would cost her less. A smaller life, bought at a smaller price.

So she traded down. She stayed weeks where she would once have stayed years. She helped with her hands and kept her history behind her teeth. When someone leaned toward her, wanting a friend, a daughter, or a wife, she felt the lean start and stepped back from it before it could land. And it worked. That is the part that frightens me to tell you now, because it worked. The leavings stopped costing what they had cost. She walked away from good people with her chest quiet where it used to ache.

There was a settlement in a high cold valley: decent people, a hard place, and a season of good work. She had kept her distance the whole time and learned no one's name on purpose. When the snow came on and it was time to go, a woman she had worked beside every day of that season pressed a round of hard bread into her hands. She stood in the wind, looking sorry to see her go.

"You could stay the winter," the woman said. "No shame in it. The pass will still be there in spring."

"I'll go," Mei said.

The woman nodded, as though she had known that answer before she offered the other one. "Then eat it on the climb. Don't save it. Bread like that goes to stone." It was a kindness. It was the kind of thing you say to someone you would have liked to keep. Mei took the bread, and walked out over the pass. And somewhere on the climb she noticed she was waiting for the pull, the small tearing that leaving had always cost her, the thing she had spent a whole bargain trying to make smaller.

It did not come. She felt almost nothing. A thread's worth of tug, thin and brief. Then only the clean cold air, the open road, and her own steady breath.

She was pleased. She stood on the pass with the bread going hard in her hand and felt, of all things, proud. She had finally learned to do the thing well. She had made the wound so small she could barely feel it close.

It did not occur to her to ask what else in her might have gone quiet to make that possible. Why the bread meant nothing. Why the woman's face was already blurring, though she had seen it that morning. She had set out to feel less pain, and she had succeeded. It would be a long time, a very long time, before she understood that pain had not been the only thing she had turned down. That evening she noted only the success, like a snare finally set right, and walked on into the dark, well pleased with her own good sense.
