The first people after the lake lived in birch woods along a fast brown river. Mei smelled their smoke a morning before she reached them. Her heart went quick and foolish at it. She washed in the river before she went in. She combed her hair out with her fingers and practiced, under her breath, the greeting the lake people had used, in case it carried this far. Then she walked in, hoping harder than she would have admitted to be let stay. She stayed six winters, which she told herself was short. An old man there had lost a daughter about the age Mei looked. She learned that from the others; he never said so. He saved her the marrow from his share. He mended a tear in her cloak without asking. In the evenings he talked at her as if she had always been there to talk at. His name was Kesh. She learned it the first night and used it every day, because she had not yet learned not to.

"You eat like a bird," he told her once. "A bird with somewhere to be."

"I'm not going anywhere," Mei said, and nearly believed it.

One spring his grandson climbed her like a tree and settled on her shoulders, heels drumming at her collarbones. She carried him halfway to the river before she remembered the lake, and another child on her shoulders, grown into a woman. She set him down on the bank. He howled. Kesh looked at her across the water and did not ask.

The looks came in their own time, as they always came. Kesh's were among the last. She went at night. At the first rise she turned to look back. She turned again at the second, and again at the third, until the birches had closed over the last glow of their fire. She walked two days with the leaving lodged under her ribs like a swallowed stone.

Somewhere in those two days she made a bargain with herself, though there was no one else to make it with. She could not change the ending. Every fire she sat at, she would leave, or bury. But she could choose how much of herself she brought to it in the first place.

The next fire was on a cold coast, among people who lived on seals, shellfish, and whatever the storms threw up. She smelled their smoke too, heavy with salt, drifting down the shore toward her. Her heart lifted at it a little. She did not stop to wash. She stayed a summer and the winter after it. She worked. She answered when spoken to, and kept her answers short enough to end the conversation. When they asked her name, she shrugged, and after a while they stopped asking.

A young hunter took to leaving things at her shelter: a good flint, a sealskin pouch, and a string of shells with holes bored through them. She knew what the shells meant. She left them where they lay. 

He waited for more. There was no more. When the thaw came she went. She looked back once, from the headland, at their smoke going up thin over the shelters, and something in her chest pulled tight. It eased by noon. She noticed how quickly, and was glad of it.

And it worked. That is the part that still frightens me, with this fire between us, because it worked.

Some fires later she crossed a pass where the snow never fully went and came down into a valley high enough that summer came late to it and left early. She arrived at the tail end of one. From the pass she had read their smoke for what she needed: how many fires, how many hands, and whether there would be work. She came down to it at an even pace, and her heart kept the same one. Stone shelters roofed with turf. Smoke bent flat by the wind. On the slope above, a stand of wild grass had gone to seed, and every pair of hands in the valley was up there beating the seed into baskets before the cold came back. Nobody asked where she had come from. They asked if she could work. She could.

She spent a season on that slope and kept herself out of reach. She slept at the cold end of the shelter, by the door-hide, where the draft came in. She ate her share and let the stories that went with it pass over her. The woman who worked the row beside her said her own name on the first morning, and Mei let it go by. On the third day a small boy held his arms up to her, the plain demand of a child who wants to be higher than he is. She set him back among the baskets and went on beating seed. He stood looking at her a while, puzzled. Then he went off to find someone else to climb.

The woman kept a place for her at the evening fire, close in, where the heat was best. Every evening Mei took a place at the edge instead. The woman never remarked on it. She only went on keeping the place. Mei felt the lean in that, and stepped back from it each time before it could land.

The snow came, and with it the time to go. The woman met her at the foot of the pass and pressed a round of hard bread into her hands. She stood in the wind, looking sorry to see her go.

"You could stay the winter," the woman said. "No shame in it. The pass will still be there in spring."

Mei said nothing.

The woman nodded, as though she had known that answer before she offered the other one. "Then eat it on the climb. Don't save it. Bread like that goes to stone."

Mei took the bread and walked up into the pass. She did not think to look back. Somewhere on the climb she noticed she was waiting for the pull, the small tearing that leaving had always cost her, the thing she had spent a whole bargain trying to make smaller.

It did not come, or nearly not. A thread's worth of tug, thin and brief. Then only the clean cold air, the open road, and her own steady breath.

She stood on the pass with the wind in her face and felt, of all things, proud. She had finally learned to do the thing well. She had made the wound so small she could barely feel it close.

She forgot the bread on the climb. Four days down the far side she found it at the bottom of her bag, gone to stone, as the woman had said it would. She broke a corner off against a rock and chewed it a long time. It tasted of very little. For no reason she could have named, she tried to see the face of the woman who had baked it. She had the hands. She had the wind, and the voice saying *don't save it*. Above that there was only a blur. The name she had never kept at all.

She tossed the rest into the snow and walked on, well pleased with her own good sense.
