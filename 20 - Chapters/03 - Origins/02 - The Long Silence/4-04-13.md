The illness came in Lin Feiyun's last winter, and it came quietly, as the bad ones do: a cough that did not break, then a tiredness she could not sleep off. She knew what it was before Mei did. She had spent her life at other people's sickbeds. She was not afraid of it, or if she was, she folded the fear small and kept it out of the way, as she kept everything.

Here is the part I have understood least kindly about myself, and I have had a long time to try.

Mei knew things by then. A hundred sickbeds behind her, herbs from ends of the earth Lin Feiyun had never walked. There were things she could have tried. She did not try them. She told Lin Feiyun, and told herself, that the illness was past medicine. And that was even true. But under the true thing was another thing she would not look at. To save her would be to keep her. To keep her would be to do this again in twenty years, or forty. And some part of the girl that had grown very tired had already begun, without asking her leave, to let Lin Feiyun go.

And there was a thing under even that, though she would not have found the words for it then, and I am in no hurry to find them now. A season before, she had stopped a spirit beast with the flat of her hand. She had set her palm against two hundred pounds of charging death, and it simply stopped. There is no palm to set against a winter. None against an old woman's lungs going slowly to water, or against the plain arithmetic of a body that has done its years. The one strength she had was good for exactly the wrong thing. She could break anything that came at Lin Feiyun from the outside. She could not lift a finger against the thing that came from within.

So Mei did the other thing instead, the only thing she has ever really been able to do for the dying. She stayed. She kept Lin Feiyun warm. She brought her water she could barely take and broth she could not. When Lin Feiyun could no longer get out to the garden, Mei described it to her instead: what was up, what the frost had taken, where the gourd vine had finally given out, and what she would set in come spring. The seedlings shallow, always shallow, because she could not have set them any other way now if she tried. She covered her when she shivered. She kept the fire high enough for her to see. She sat through the nights, because the nights were when the fear came for people, and she had long since stopped needing sleep. Whatever those last days had in them to give, she gave. It was not much. It was what there was.

Lin Feiyun went out with the winter, near morning. Mei was awake for it, but Lin Feiyun left as she had lived, without fuss, sliding under so quietly that the fact of it arrived after she was already gone. One breath was the last one. Mei only knew which by the ones that did not come after.

She buried her at the edge of the garden, in the good soil they had argued over. Then she did a thing she had not done in a very long time. She stayed. Weeks. She kept the fire. She tended the beds. She told herself she was letting the ground settle, letting the winter pass, and being sensible about the roads. The truth was that she did not want to walk out of the last place that still smelled like Lin Feiyun.

But one morning she banked the fire, shouldered her water, and went. The garden would keep a while without her. She did not look back at it from the ridge, and told herself that was because there was nothing new to see.

She did not notice, walking down out of that valley, that something in her had gone still and would not start again. You would think a thing like that would announce itself. It does not. I have gone back over that morning more times than you would believe, looking for the moment it happened, and I never find it. It happens quietly, some hour when no one is watching the water, and by the time you see it the surface is already hard enough to stand on. She walked down out of the valley lighter than she had climbed up into it, and she thought that was grief lifting. It was not grief lifting. It was grief being the last thing to go.
