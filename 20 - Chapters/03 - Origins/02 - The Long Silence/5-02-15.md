There was a man she traveled with the better part of a year, and he is the one I am most ashamed of. I have kept him for the end of this part on purpose. She would not have called him a friend; she had given that word up on the roads, along with the names and most of the rest. But he was the nearest thing to one she had let walk beside her since Lin Feiyun, and if you want to understand what she had become, you have to sit with him a while first. So sit with him.

He was a peddler. He went town to town with a mule and a load of small useful things: needles, salt, cook-pots, thread, the little a hard country could not make for itself. He traded them for the little it could. He had been doing it so long that the road was the only home he named without flinching. They fell in together as travelers did, on a stretch of country where a lone walker was a gift to bandits and two were a second thought. Having fallen in, they simply did not fall back out.

He talked. God, he talked. He had that habit of narrating the road to himself under his breath, giving the hills names as they passed them: Big Ugly, The Other One, That Bastard There, a whole low country of insults that asked nothing of her but filled the silence anyway. He named the mule something different every week and pretended not to remember the old one. He named the weather. Once, on a gray morning with rain coming, he squinted at the sky and called it a real Auntie Mek of a day. When she did not ask, he told her anyway. Auntie Mek was a woman from his boyhood village who had arrived exactly when you did not want her and stayed exactly too long. By the end of the story Mei had caught herself almost smiling, and he had caught her at it.

"There," he said, pointing at her face like he'd flushed a bird. "Knew you had one in you."

She did not tell him he was the first person in a lifetime to come looking for it. She let him think it was his own doing. It cost her nothing, it pleased him, and those were the terms she kept the world on now.

That was how the year went. He gave, and asked nothing back that she could not spare. He shared his fire and half his pot without keeping count. He told her about places she had walked through when they were still forest. He did not know that was what he was doing, and she did not tell him. He had lost people, a wife and a child, as ordinary men lose them, to a fever in a bad winter a long way back. He told her about them once, plainly, on a night when the fire was low, not asking her to carry it, only setting it down where another person could see it. She listened. She was good at listening. It was the one thing the numbness left her fit for.

Once, and only once, he came near the thing she was. They had been on the road most of a year by then, through a summer and into the cold. He looked at her across the fire one night, head tipped, and said she wore well. That was all. You wear well. Most would have let it go. He was not most; he noticed things, it was his trade. She watched him decide whether to ask, watched him decide not to, and watched him choose, instead, to pour her tea. It was the same mercy Lin Feiyun had shown her, forty lifetimes and one whole heart ago. She took the tea. She felt the old warmth try to move in her, the ghost of what she would have felt once. She felt how little of it actually came, and she was not even sad about the littleness. That was the part that should have frightened her. It did not.

She kept him safe without ever letting him see it. A washed-out ford she steered them wide of a day before they reached it, for a reason she gave him that was not the reason. A fever town they went around. A pair of men on the road one dusk who meant them harm, then, for no cause any of them could have named, thought better of it and let them pass. He never knew. He thought he traveled a lucky road. He had, after a fashion. He had fallen in with the one thing on it that nothing could touch, and it had decided, out of a reflex it no longer felt, to spend a little of that on him.

The cold took him in the end, as it took men his age on hard roads. A cough that settled in and would not lift, then a heaviness, then a morning he could not get up to load the mule. She knew what it was before he did. She had learned it in Lin Feiyun's house, learned it well enough to name the day. She could have tried the things she had learned since, herbs from ends of the earth he had never walked. She did not. She told herself it was past medicine, and it was. She left it at that, and did not look at what sat under the leaving.

She stayed, because staying with the dying was the one habit that had outlived everything else in her. She got him into a roadside shelter out of the rain. She kept him warm. She kept the mule fed, because he asked her to, fretting about the animal even then, his last worry in the world saved for the thing that would outlast him. Near the end he was mostly not there. But once he surfaced, and found her face, and said, "You'll sell him on. Don't let them work him to death. He's lazy, but he's honest." She said she would. He seemed to think that was a fine last thing to arrange, and maybe it was. Then he went back under. A while later he stopped, and she did not catch the exact moment, because she never could.

He died in a roadside shelter in the rain. She closed his eyes. She built him a cairn out of what stones the field gave her, because it seemed owed. And then she sat down to feel it.

I want to be exact about this, because it is the hinge the rest of my life turns on. She knew the grief was supposed to come. She had felt it over the old woman by the lake, over Lin Feiyun, over more people than a long life has days. She had felt it coming a hundred times before it broke, the pressure of it building behind the eyes. So she sat by the man's cairn in the wet and opened the place it came from.

Nothing came. Not a lessened grief. Not a buried one. An absence, clean and complete, like reaching for a color at the edge of the sky and finding the sky had none there.

She sat with the nothing a while. And here is the thing I have never been able to soften, in all the time since: the nothing did not trouble her. A wiser woman, a woman still whole, would have been frightened to feel nothing over a man who had shared his fire with her for a year and asked her, dying, to be kind to his mule. Mei only noted the emptiness, like a tool worn smooth, with a distant, practical interest and no alarm at all.

It did not bother her that it did not bother her. That is the whole of it. That is the sentence I would carve on the marker of the girl she used to be, if I could find where that girl is buried. She sold the mule on to a decent family. She told them it was lazy but honest, because he had asked her to. She felt nothing about that either. She left the man his cairn. She walked on. Her step was easy, and the ease was the worst thing about it. She did not think so at the time.
