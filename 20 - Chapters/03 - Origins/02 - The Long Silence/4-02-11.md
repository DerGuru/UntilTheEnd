Mei stayed with Lin Feiyun as she had let herself stay nowhere since the lake. Years, and she stopped pretending they were days.

I could give you those years as a list of what they did. It would sound like nothing, and it would be everything. They rose before the village did. Lin Feiyun would already be at the grinding stone when Mei came in from the cold, working a paste of something green, humming that tuneless hum, and she would push a bowl of millet across the table with her elbow without looking up. They ate. Then the day: the plot, the plants, and the people who came to the door.

Lin Feiyun kept a plot at the edge of the trees. She worked it herself, bent-backed and stubborn. Half the village would have done it for the woman who set their bones and pulled their children into the world. She grew her medicine there. She ground it into pastes and teas. Along the sunny edge she grew food: millet, beans, and a gourd she was unreasonably proud of. She was a healer first and a farmer second, but she would have told you the two were one trade. Things that grow. Things that mend. She had no patience for anyone who thought either was beneath the other.

They made the garden together the second spring, or the third. It was the nearest thing to a fight they ever had, and the nearest thing to a courtship too. Mei set seedlings as she did everything: quick, deep, and done. Lin Feiyun would come along behind her, dig every one up, and set it again a finger shallower. "You bury them like you're ashamed of them," she said. "Let them see the sky. They want to live. Stop helping them so hard." Mei thought it was nonsense, but the shallow ones grew better, and she never admitted it. She set them shallow when Lin Feiyun was watching and deep when she was not, out of pure spite. The spite was the warmest thing she had felt in longer than she could name.

The gourd came good the fourth autumn. Lin Feiyun had been threatening it for years, this one vine that never gave more than knuckles. Then one season it put out a thing the size of a child's head, pale green, ridged, and absurd. She carried it in from the plot like a midwife. She set it on the table between them. She looked at it, then at Mei. "Say it's a fine gourd," she said. Mei said it was a fine gourd. "It is a fine gourd," Lin Feiyun agreed, satisfied, as though Mei had needed convincing. They ate it through the winter. It was a perfectly ordinary gourd. I have remembered it longer than the village that grew it has been dust.

Lin Feiyun taught her the work without ever calling it teaching. Which leaf for a fever. Which root for a cough that would not break. Which mushroom fed you, and which one fed the ground with you: serrated at the edge to live, smooth to die, never a smooth one however sweet it smelled. How to set a bone so it healed straight, how to turn a breech child, and how to sit with someone who was not going to live, making the leaving smaller by not being afraid of it yourself. I have taught pieces of that trade to more people than I can count since, in more tongues than you will ever learn, and I never once told any of them where I learned it. Mei had killed more people than the village had ever held. In that house she learned how to keep them alive and how to let them go. The second was harder, and it was the one she would carry longest.

There was a winter the fever came through, the bad kind, and they did not sleep for a stretch of days Mei stopped counting. They worked side by side in one smoky house after another, Lin Feiyun's hands and Mei's hands. When it was over, when the village had lost three where it should have lost twenty, they walked home in the gray dawn too tired to speak. Lin Feiyun stopped in the road. She took Mei's face in both her cold hands, looked at her a moment, and said, "I'm glad the rain brought you." Then she let go and went inside. She slept a day and a night. She never said a thing like that again. She did not need to. Of all the words in all the tongues Mei has swallowed since, she kept those five.

The years did what years do, to everyone but Mei. Lin Feiyun's black hair went iron, then white. Her hands, so sure at the grinding stone, began to ache in the cold and to fumble the small work. And Mei did not change. She saw Lin Feiyun begin to see it, caught her once or twice looking across the fire with the particular still attention of a woman adding a sum and not liking the total. Lin Feiyun said nothing. Mei said nothing. It sat between them, the one unsaid thing in a house that had no others. Mei waited, with the old cold waiting she knew too well, for the evening it would have to be spoken, and for the leaving she was certain it would bring.

I have been fair to almost no one in this telling, least of all the girl I used to be. Let me be fair to her here. She knew what she was doing. She knew every one of those good years was a debt that would come due. She knew that the shallower she set her roots the less it would hurt to pull them, and she set them deep anyway, deep as the seedlings, burying herself in that small warm life as if she were ashamed of how much she wanted it. She did it with her eyes open. It was the bravest thing she ever did, and no one will ever know to call it that but me.
