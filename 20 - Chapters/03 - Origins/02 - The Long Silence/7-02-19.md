The talk found a man who collected useful things.

He was the kind the age kept producing, risen out of the wreck of that empire I had watched fall, a warlord with a real army and a larger idea of himself than the land could hold yet. He heard the story of a woman in a river valley who could not be killed. A man like that does not hear such a story as a wonder. He hears it as a tool he does not yet own.

His men came for me at the end of a summer. A dozen of them, mounted, armed, and careful. Their captain was courteous, which told me the lord had thought about it. He did not know if the story was true, but if it was, rough handling would be remembered by a thing that lived forever, and he was not a fool.

They could not have made me go. I want that understood. Not the whole dozen, not the whole army behind them, not the lord himself with every soldier he had. There was nothing on that field or any field that could move me an inch I did not choose to be moved. I knew it. Some part of them, looking at me, knew it too.

I went anyway.

Not from fear, since fear had nothing to hold on to. Not from force. I went because refusing would have meant I cared what happened next, and I did not. Going and staying weighed the same, which is to say they weighed nothing, and when two nothings sit in your hands you may as well let the man with the plan tip the scale. He wanted the woman who does not die. I had no reason to disappoint him and no reason to oblige him, and in the absence of reasons a person simply drifts toward whatever current is pulling.

The captain, satisfied I would come, put his hand on my shoulder to turn me toward the horses. Heavy. Certain. The hand of a man who has been told to fetch a thing and has decided I am the thing. He said a name, his lord's name, the name I was being brought to serve, and a direction. North. Toward the hills where the man was building whatever he was building.

I let the hand turn me. I walked north between their horses, toward a name and a war and the long red middle of my life.

That is where I will stop for tonight.

---

Xu Ran is still writing when he realizes she has stopped talking.

He has been racing her this last while, trying to get the hand and the name and the direction down before they left him, and the wanting is all through him now. He can feel that this goes somewhere worse than anything yet, and he wants it as he has never let himself want anything from her. North. A name. A war. He looks up with the next question already in his mouth.

The fire is almost out. She is watching him, not it. And whatever she finds in his face, the greed of it, maybe, the boy still in him who wants the story more than he wants her to rest, makes her do a thing he has not seen her do.

She stops on purpose.

"Xu Ran," she says. "You look tired." A pause. The last light moves on her face. "We'll go on tomorrow."

It is not true that he is tired. They both know it. He is nineteen kinds of awake, and she has just left a stranger's hand on her own shoulder at the edge of the worst of it. But he has learned, over these nights, what her mercies look like, and he has learned not to argue with them. He closes the notebook over his thumb to keep the place. The dark comes in where the firelight was.

Across the coals, the oldest thing he has ever known has stopped, one more time, a half-step short of the fire. She has covered him with the last of the evening, as she must once have covered someone she has not told him about yet.
