The talk found a man who collected useful things.

He was the kind the age kept producing: risen out of the wreck of one of those empires she had watched fall, a warlord with a real army and a bigger idea of himself than the land could hold yet. He heard the story of a woman in a river valley who could not be killed. A man like that does not hear such a story as a wonder. He hears it as a tool he does not yet own.

His men came for her at the end of a summer. A dozen of them, mounted, armed, and careful. Their captain was courteous, which told her the lord had thought about it. The lord did not know whether the story was true, but if it was, rough handling would be remembered by a thing that lived forever, and he was no fool.

They could not have made her go. I want that understood, because it matters for everything that comes after. Not the dozen, not the whole army behind them, not the lord himself with every soldier he had. There was nothing on that field or any field that could have moved her an inch she did not choose to be moved. She knew it. Some animal part of them, looking at her, knew it too.

She went anyway. Not from fear; fear had nothing left to hold. Not from force. She went because refusing would have meant she cared what happened next, and she did not. Going and staying weighed the same, which is to say they weighed nothing. And when two nothings sit in your hands, you may as well let the man with a plan tip the scale.

The captain, satisfied she would come, put his hand on her shoulder to turn her toward the horses. Heavy. Certain. The hand of a man who has been told to fetch a thing and has decided she is the thing. He said a name. His lord's name, the name she was being brought to serve. And a direction. North. Toward the hills where the man was building whatever he was building.

She let the hand turn her. She walked north between their horses, toward a name, a war, and the long red middle of her life.

That is where I will stop, for tonight.

Not because the story stops. It does not stop for a long time yet. The next part is the one I have told no one. It is the part where the girl I have been handing you, thin, young, and gone quiet, walking north between a stranger's horses, becomes a thing the world learned a name for, then tried very hard to forget. I can feel you wanting it. I saw you lean in at the word war. But I have carried that stranger's hand on my shoulder for longer than your language has existed, and I find I would like one night on this side of it before I pick her up again.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow I will tell you about Liè.
