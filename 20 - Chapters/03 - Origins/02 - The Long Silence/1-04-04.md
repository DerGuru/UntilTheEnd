Koh died two winters after that, which was a mercy, because two winters is not long. She went as the old go, slowly and then all at once, her breath getting shallow over a handful of days while the camp waited. Mei sat with her at the end, because sitting with the dying was already the one thing she could still do for people, though she did not yet know she would be doing it forever.

Koh was not afraid. She had never been afraid of anything she had tried, and she had, by then, tried nearly everything except this. Near the end she was mostly not there. But once she surfaced, and found Mei's face, and said, in the old bright scattered voice still buried somewhere in the wreck of the new one, "Good beetle." It made no sense to anyone else in the shelter. It made Mei's chest do a thing it had not done in longer than she could remember, because it was what Koh had always said when Mei showed her something small and strange and true. Good beetle. Bad mud. Then she went back under, and a while later she stopped, and Mei did not catch the exact moment, because she never could.

She buried Koh above the river, near the flat rock, in ground Koh had scrambled over as a child. Then she went back to the fire, to the best place, the warm seat among the people who had thrown her away. She looked around the circle at every face, and there was not one left who had ever seen her as a girl. Not one who knew the warm had a name before it had a weapon. Not one who would ever say good beetle. They were all, every one of them, people who had been born into a world where Mei was simply the thing at the fire that kept them alive and would kill them if they were careless. She was, to the last living soul who could see her, no longer a person. She had become a fact.

She stayed a while even then. That is the part I want you to understand, because it stayed true of every leaving that came after, all hundred thousand years of them. She did not go the morning after Koh died. She stayed a season, and another, keeping them fed, sitting in the cold seat at the warm fire, being their fact. She kept waiting to feel a reason to stay that was better than habit. None came. One gray morning she banked the fire, filled a skin with water, and walked out of the ring of them while they slept. It was the same door she had once been driven through, except this time she chose the hour. No one drove her. And no one, waking to find her gone, did anything but breathe easier.

She had wanted, her whole short life, to belong to people. She had gotten as close as she ever would: a place no one could take from her, among the only people who had ever been hers. And she had learned the thing that would run the rest of her life, though she put it in no words then and would put it in none for an age. That belonging and being feared cannot live in the same person. That she could have their safety or their company, and never both, and she had had a bellyful of safety.

So she left the last place that was ever home, and went looking for the thing she had just proved she could not have. She would look for a very long time.
