By the time she walked into Lin Feiyun's doorway, she had been good at her bargain for longer than she could have said. She had made herself thin and portable. She could sit at any hearth for a season, take no mark, and leave none. She had it confused with wisdom.

It was raining the day she stopped, the steady gray rain that settles in for days, and the door of a house at the edge of a village stood open, giving off a smell she had no name for. Something green being crushed. Something bitter under it. Something warm under that. She stopped in the road, caught by her own nose. A woman looked up from a grinding stone inside and saw a soaked stranger dripping in her road.

The woman said the thing that undid her, though it took Mei years to understand it had. She said, "You're letting the rain in. Come or go, but shut the door."

Mei came in. She shut the door. She meant to stay until the rain let up.

The woman did not ask where she was from or where she was going, the two questions everyone asked, the two questions Mei had a hundred smooth answers ready for. She looked at Mei's arms, said they were fresher than hers, and put a pestle in Mei's hand. So Mei ground herbs while the woman cut. The rain went on. The room filled with that smell. The woman hummed while she worked, tuneless and absent, the sound of a person who has forgotten anyone is listening. That was the whole of it. A dry room, a smell, a woman humming, and a task set into Mei's hands as though she had always been there to do it.

The rain let up on the third day. Mei did not go.

She told herself the usual things. A few more days. The roads are mud. The woman can use the hands. But the roads had been mud before and never held her. What held her was the smell of the room before the woman woke, the sure small movements of her hands, and the plain fact that she asked nothing but gave anyway. Mei had spent an age teaching herself to want less. And here, over a bowl of crushed leaves, she caught herself wanting to stay.

It frightened her more than the scree slope had. More than the snowmelt river, more than anything the world had ever done to her body, because her body had never once been in danger, and this was. She had a rule. The rule had carried her whole through more leavings than she could count. And she was standing in a stranger's warm dry room breaking it on purpose, for a smell, a hum, and a pair of unhurried hands.

The woman's name was Lin Feiyun.

I have carried it a very long time. Longer than the languages you speak, longer than the oldest thing you would call old. I let ten thousand names go by in the years I have been describing, and I will let ten thousand more go by after her. Hers I kept. You may wonder why this is the one name I hand you whole, when everyone else in this story reaches you as a hand, or a fire, or a gap where a face used to be. The reason is not a large one. She was the first person who was kind to me for no reason at all. She wanted nothing. She simply had more warmth than her small life needed, and she let the overflow fall on whoever was standing in her doorway. One wet afternoon that was me.
