For a long time after, she went back to the garden.

Not to live there. She could not live in that house with Lin Feiyun gone out of it. But every few years her walking would bend, without her quite deciding it, toward that valley. She would come over the ridge, down to the beds, find them half wild, and put them right. A season of it. Pulling what had choked the good plants. Turning the soil. Setting Lin Feiyun's plants where Lin Feiyun had liked them. It was the one thing she still did for love and not for use. As long as she did it, some part of her was still the woman who had helped make it.

She could hear her when she worked it. Not a voice from anywhere, just the memory, sharp as a splinter under a nail: Lin Feiyun telling her she set the seedlings too deep, always too deep, burying them as if she were ashamed of them. Mei would catch herself setting one shallower to spite a woman forty years in the ground. The spite was warm, and she held onto it, because it was the nearest thing to warmth she had left. Those visits kept Lin Feiyun a little alive, and kept the girl who had loved her a little alive too.

The visits came further apart. She stopped noticing when the season was right for them. When she did go she worked faster and stayed less. One time she caught herself tending the beds as she would clear any overgrown path, not for Lin Feiyun, only to have it done. She did not think much of that at the time.

Then a stretch of years went by. One evening, a whole range and a river away from that valley, it came to her that she had not been back in a long while. A season, at least. More. The beds would be wild now. Lin Feiyun's plants would be choking under the weeds they had pulled together.

She noticed the thought arrive. She noticed it ask her to turn around.

And it did not matter to her at all. She waited to feel the pull toward the garden, as she had once stood on a pass and waited to feel the pull of leaving. Nothing came. Only the evening, the road going on, and a flat mild readiness to keep walking it. So she did. She never saw the garden again.

That was the last of it, and she did not know to mark it. The part of her that had kept a garden stayed on that road behind her, and she walked on without it. I have wanted, from here, to reach back and turn that girl around. To make her walk the miles back, kneel in the weeds, and set the beds right one more time, not for the garden but for herself, for the last warm thing she owned. She was already past it. She would not feel the size of what she left on that road for a very long time, and by the time she felt it, there was no road left to find.
