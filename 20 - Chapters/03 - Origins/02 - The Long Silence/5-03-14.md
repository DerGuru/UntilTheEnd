There was a man I traveled with for a while in there. I will not pretend I remember him well. But I remember that he was good to me, and quick to laugh, and that in an earlier age I would have kept him as long as he let me. He fell ill on a road between two towns whose names I never learned, and I stayed with him, because staying with the dying was the one habit that had outlived everything else in me.

He died in a roadside shelter in the rain. I closed his eyes. I built him a cairn out of what stones the field gave me, because it seemed owed. And then I sat down to feel it.

I want to be exact about this, because it is the hinge of everything that came after. I knew the grief was supposed to come. I had felt it over the fire-keeper, over Lin Feiyun, over more people than a long life has days. I had smelled it coming a hundred times before it broke. So I sat by his cairn in the wet and opened the place it came from.

Nothing came. Not a lessened grief. Not a buried one. An absence, clean and complete, like reaching for a color at the edge of the sky and finding the sky had none there.

I sat with the nothing a while. And here is the part I have never been able to soften: the nothing did not trouble me. A wiser woman, a woman still whole, would have been terrified to feel nothing over a friend. I noted the emptiness like a tool worn smooth, with a distant practical interest and no alarm at all.

It did not bother me that it did not bother me. That is the sentence. That is where I crossed over, though I would not have said I had crossed anything. I got up when the rain eased. I left him his cairn. I walked on, and my step was easy, and the ease was the worst thing about it, and I did not think so at the time.
