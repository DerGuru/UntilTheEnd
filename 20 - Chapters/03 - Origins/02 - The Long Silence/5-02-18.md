The potters lived where a slow river cut through banks of gray clay, and the smoke of their firing pits hung over the village most days of the dry season. Mei read it from a ridge away: a dozen pits, a great deal of wood, and work for anyone who knew fire. She knew fire. They took her on to feed the pits through the nights. And within a month the old potters had stopped checking her work.

The girl was the youngest of a family that had been potting longer than anyone could remember. She was ten, perhaps, and every pot she made came out of the fire cracked, every single one. Her brothers had stopped teasing her about it, which was worse than the teasing was before. She sat at the edge of the firing ground each morning while the pits were opened. She watched her own pots come out in pieces and did not cry. There was a set to her jaw that Mei recognized from a long way back.

It was not the clay. The clay was good, and the girl had grown up at these pits. She packed her pots where her father packed his. She pulled her walls up thinner than his, and just as even to the eye. Her father had gone over every pot she made so often that he had given up looking for the fault. Mei watched the girl's hands for a week before she felt it.

It came off the girl's thumbs while she pressed the coils together, a thread of qi so slight that Mei almost missed it. It was not like the qi of the old woman by the lake. Hers had run hot and quick into the ember, as Mei's own did when she made fire. This one was heavier and slower, a damp weight that did not run anywhere. It settled. Wherever the girl's thumbs rested longest, the clay went still and firmed ahead of the rest. Mei laid her palm on one of the drying pots and could feel the patches under the smooth skin: settled here, soft there. Every crack she had seen come out of that pit ran along the seams between them.

Mei could have told her in a sentence. Nobody would have believed it, least of all the girl. Instead she sat down beside the girl's pit one night, when no one else was awake to see, with a lump of wet clay between them.

"Listen to it first," she said.

They were the old woman's words. Mei heard that, and this time she did not have to stop. She pressed the girl's thumb into the clay and held it there. "Under your thumb. Feel it go still?"

The girl frowned at her own hand for a long moment. "It feels like clay."

"It feels like a lot of things. You'll learn them."

"Can my father feel it?"

"No."

"Can you?"

Mei put a fresh lump of clay in the girl's hands. "Again."

So that season she taught the girl to hold it back: how to feel the clay settle under her thumb, how to lift the thumb before it set, and how to keep her hands moving so they never rested long in one place. The girl was stubborn and quick. She argued with everything, and she was right about some of it.

"My father doesn't think about his hands," she said, the third night.

"Your father's walls are as thick as his thumb. He doesn't need to."

"Then I'll make mine thicker."

"Then they'll be his pots."

The girl thought about that for a long time, scowling at the coals. The next morning her walls were as thin as ever. A few nights later she brought Mei a pot she had worked the other way round. She had let the thread out on purpose and spread it through the whole wall instead of pooling it under her thumbs, so that all of it settled at once. It cracked in the firing, and so did the next five. But Mei, who had spent most of her life learning to hold the warm back, had never once thought of spreading it.

In return, or because she had decided that anyone who watched her hands so closely ought to dirty her own, the girl sat Mei down at the coils. Mei's first pot slumped. Her second dried in her palms and split before it was a pot at all, because she had forgotten herself for a moment. The girl laughed so hard she got clay in her hair.

"Your hands are worse than mine," she said.

Mei made another. It was worse.

Mei found she was getting up in the gray before dawn to check the girl's pit before the girl did. One morning the girl came out early and caught her at it.

"You're checking my pit."

"I was passing."

"Nobody passes here. It's the end of the row."

Mei went on checking it. She found she minded which way the wind blew on firing nights, too. She did not look at any of that too closely.

The pot came out whole on the last firing of the season.

The girl did not shout. She knelt by the pit with the pot in both hands, turning it, looking for the crack that had always been there, and there was no crack. Then she carried it across the firing ground, past her father and her brothers, straight to Mei. She held it up.

"Say it's a fine pot," she said.

For a breath Mei was somewhere else, across a table from a gourd.

She looked at the pot. It was a fine pot. She could see every way it was fine: the even wall, the clean ring it would give if you flicked it, and the warm gray where the fire had come up slow.

"It's a fine pot," Mei said.

The girl kept looking at her. Something went out of her face, not all of it, only the part that had been waiting.

"You said it like the weather," she said.

Mei had no answer to that. The girl took the pot back against her chest and went to find her mother.

Her mother saw it from across the firing ground and shrieked. She ran. She snatched up the pot, held it over her head, and turned in a circle with it, shouting for the whole village to come see. The girl lit up, all of her, as a pit lights when the wind comes round.

Mei had used the same words. She stood by the pits a long while afterward and went back over them, looking for whatever the mother had put into them that she had not. She could not find it.

I still have the shape of that pot in my hands. I could draw you its rim in the ash of this fire, and the place where the gray went warm. I do not have the girl's face.

She left in the night, before the next firing. The girl was asleep by her family's fire with the pot beside her, one hand on it. This time Mei had nothing to set by a sleeping hand.
