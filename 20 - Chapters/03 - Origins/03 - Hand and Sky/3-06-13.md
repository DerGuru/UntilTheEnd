By evening the river was running clear again above the gravel bar. Below it, where the bodies had caught, it stayed brown for longer.

The lord's men took the far bank's surrender in the last of the light. They sat the prisoners down in rows along the bank and tied their thumbs behind them with bowstring. Nobody in either army spoke above a mutter. The captains who were left walked up and down the rows, counting.

Liè went back into the ford.

The water was warm around her shins where she had stood. The banks on both sides were scorched in half-circles, as though someone had pressed the rim of a great hot bowl into the grass. The dead lay in the shallows in rings. Each ring had a clear middle, where she had been, and grew thicker toward the edge, where the force had run out. The current moved them a little. They rocked against each other.

She smelled iron, then the burned turf of the banks. Under those, there was a third smell. It was sharp and faintly sweet, like the air after lightning has struck close by. She had never smelled it before that day. She would smell it on a great many fields afterward.

It was then, standing in the middle of the ford, that she noticed the quiet.

All that day there had been two armies' worth of hums pressing on her from every side, in the river and on both banks. She had stopped noticing it, as you stop noticing a load you have carried long enough. Now most of it was gone. The hums around her had gone out one circle at a time, and the weight had gone with them. What was left was thin and far off, up on the banks. Inside the rings there was nothing at all.

She felt light. She stood in the river and felt how light she was. She felt how easy it had been. Three days the two armies had stood in this water trying to finish each other. She had walked through it from bank to bank before the sun had moved a hand's width. It was done. Everything inside the rings was finished, completely, with nothing left over.

She laughed.

It came up from the bottom of her belly, loud, and it went on. The sound carried across the water to both banks. It was the sound people make at a good joke, and she had not made it in longer than she could remember. She stood in the ford among the rings of the dead with her head back, and she laughed until her eyes ran.

On the near bank, a soldier with a red hand on his shield heard her. He set the shield down carefully on the grass. Then he walked a few steps away from it and was sick.

Xu Ran's brush has stopped. It is still in his hand, a finger's width above the page, and a drop of ink is gathering at the tip of it.

Yun does not look at him. She looks at the fire. A log has burned through in the middle and sagged. The two ends of it are smoking.

The drop of ink falls. It lands on the page and spreads. He does not blot it.

For a long time neither of them moves. Somewhere below them an owl calls, once, and does not call again.

Then Xu Ran sets the brush down across the notebook. He leans over, takes a split stick from the pile beside him, and lays it on the fire, across the sagging log, where it will catch. He watches until it does.

He picks up the brush. He wipes the tip on the rim of the ink stone, and he writes.

Yun waits until the sound of the brush is steady. Then she goes on.
