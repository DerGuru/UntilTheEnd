He made her show him twice. She split a pear tree at ten paces, then the one beside it. He made her do the second one without splitting the first one any further, and she did.

"The gate," he said. "The earth behind it. Not the houses."

"Yes."

He put his hand on her shoulder. "Only the gate."

She walked out across the burnt fields at midmorning. The army stood behind her in its lines and watched. Nobody walked with her.

The first arrows came when she was a hundred paces out. Most of them fell short. At fifty paces they began to hit. One took her in the thigh. One went through the meat of her upper arm and hung there by its fletching. She pulled them out as she walked. A third came down at a steep angle and went into her cheek under the eye, then out through the side of her nose. She snapped the shaft, drew out the two halves, and kept walking. Her face ran with blood for a dozen paces. Then it stopped running.

The bridge over the moat had been pulled up in the first week. She went down the bank and into the water. It came up to her chest. It was thick with the summer's dead, soft and swollen. They turned in the water as she pushed through them. Something heavy came down from the gatehouse and broke her collarbone. She stood in the moat until it knit. Then she climbed the far bank and stopped ten paces from the gate.

The men on the gatehouse were shouting. Somebody up there had begun to pray. She could hear him over the others, the same few words again and again.

Liè settled into the turn. She let her breath go out.

The gate went in. The force went through the oak into the plug of earth and rubble behind it. The plug went too. It went the whole length of the passage at once and came out the far end into the street.

Behind it, in the street inside the gate, the city had stacked its best men forty deep. They had been waiting four months for the day the plug came down. It came down on them.

Splinters as long as a man's arm went through shields and the men behind them. Stones the size of heads went through the men. A spearman in the front rank was there, then he was a smear across the three men behind him. The heat came after, and it was worse. Where it passed, beards went up in a breath. The bronze of the helmets glowed dull red. The men inside the helmets were alive for a moment longer, and they knew it.

The roofs beyond the gate street did not catch. She had kept it to the gate, the earth, and the men. She had kept it to the stone.

Liè walked into the passage. It was hot underfoot and slick. At the far end she walked through what was left of the forty deep, and on the other side of it the street opened into the city.

The city was on its knees. Four months of the lord's patience had made it thin. People knelt in the street because there was nothing else to do, and some of them were too weak to get up again afterward. A woman held up a child for Liè to see, as though that would help. Liè walked past her.

I have told you this before, in fewer words: a siege that lasted four months until I walked through the gate. You wrote it down then. You can find it in your old pages, if you look, on a night colder than this one. It is true. It leaves out what the gate was made of by the time I walked through it.

The lord rode in at noon with the boy and his clay behind him. He took the granaries that afternoon, and the people the day after. The king in the east never came.

He found Liè standing in the gate street. He looked at the street for a while, at what was spread across it, and at the roofs beyond it, which had not burned.

Then he put his hand on her shoulder.

"That," he said, "is what I meant by the stone."
