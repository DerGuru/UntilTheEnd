She did not count the days. I can't count them for you now, though I've tried, sitting here with your pine smoke in my hair. Light came, then dark, then light again. Each time, a little more of the mountain came apart.

By the third day there was no tree left standing within a long bowshot of the cave. By the fifth the stream that ran past his doorstep had boiled dry twice and found itself a new bed. The cliff above the cave mouth had split in so many places that it hung in slabs, held up by nothing she could see.

At night he sat on his doorstep, and she came at him in the dark. He did not light the fire again. He did not need light to know where she was, any more than she did. Whenever she drove him back against the side of the cave mouth, he would wipe the wet off the rock with the flat of his hand afterward, as a man wipes down a table. She never learned why.

I have sat with him since, two or three times over the long years. Neither of us has ever spoken of those days. Your fire smells of pine. His always smelled of wet stone.

Somewhere in the days his words changed. On the first day he had argued with her. Now he asked.

"What do you want?" he said, on the third night or the fourth. He was holding her up by the throat at the time, at arm's length, while her heels kicked against the doorstep.

She had been asked that once before, in a courtyard, by a young man with a farmer's shoulders. She gave the old man the same answer.

"Nothing."

He nodded. Then he waited.

The young man in the courtyard had filled that silence at once, with everything he wanted. The old man left it empty. They hung there together in it, she by the throat, he by the arm, until she broke his wrist and dropped.

"Who pointed you?" he asked, on another night.

"The lord."

"Which one?"

She opened her mouth. Nothing came. She had stood under four hands, or five, or six. She could feel the weight of every one of them on her shoulder, where it had sat. She could not have told him which was which.

"Hm," he said. Then he broke her knee.

The rain came and went. Once, for most of a day, snow fell on them. It melted where they fought, then fell again. He commented on it. He commented on the weather every day, briefly, as a farmer does. Rain tomorrow. Cold tonight. Clear by noon. He was right every time.

Once, while her ribs were knitting, he told her about the path below his door. A few people found it, he said. One in a lifetime, perhaps. Mostly young. Mostly alone.

"They come up to ask me how to climb higher," he said. He looked down at her in the mud. "You only came to go through me. It makes a change."

Near the end, though she did not know it was near the end, he asked her one more thing.

"What did you like?" he said. "Before."

They were both on the ground at the time. He was sitting against the rock with his legs out in front of him. She was lying on her back in the mud below his doorstep, waiting for her hip to set. She looked up into the mist and went looking for an answer, because he had asked.

It was a long way down. Somewhere at the bottom of it there was something. A smell of smoke and fat. An old woman's hands held flat over a fire. A clay pot that rang when you flicked the rim. She could see them, a little, as you see stones at the bottom of deep water. She could not get down to them. There was too much on top.

"I don't know," she said.

It was the first thing she had said to him that was more than one word.

"That's a start," he said.

It was after that night that she noticed it. Her strikes were coming slower. The warm still answered when she called it, but later and thinner, as a well answers late in a dry summer. When he broke her, she mended more slowly than she had on the first day. She noted it as she noted everything. Then she noted something else.

On the drill ground she had thrust at nothing from noon until dawn while an old soldier sat gasping on a bucket. The last time she could remember feeling anything like this, she had been a child in a cave, small and hungry. The warm had been new then.

Across the doorstep the old man sat with his back against the wet rock and his eyes closed. His breathing was slow and loud. He was tired.

So was she. It was the first new thing that had happened to her in longer than she could remember.
