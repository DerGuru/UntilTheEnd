She went up alone. The lord offered her a hundred men, out of habit. She did not take them, because there was nothing a hundred men could do up there except die in her way.

The path went up through pine forest, then through stunted pine, then through no trees at all, only rock, and a mist that lay on the rock without lifting. Water ran everywhere. It ran down the stone in sheets and dripped from every overhang. By the time she reached the cave her clothes were soaked through.

The cave was a dark mouth in a cliff, with a flat stone in front of it like a doorstep. An old man sat on the stone with a small fire beside him and a clay kettle on the fire. He was lean and long in the face, with white hair tied back. His robe had gone green at the hem from the damp. He did not get up. He watched her come up the last of the path, and his face did something adjacent to a smile.

Up close, his warm was like standing at the foot of a cliff in the dark. She could not find the top of it.

"You're wet," he said. "Everyone is, up here. Sit down. The tea's nearly ready."

She did not sit.

"Your lord's men took home most of my good leaves," he said. "I'm afraid this is the rough stuff." He lifted the kettle off the fire and poured a cup. "You've come to remove me."

"Yes."

"I thought so. They were very polite, but they looked like men who would be sending someone." He set the cup on the stone between them. "That's yours, if you want it."

Liè settled into the stance. She lifted her back heel the width of a finger and struck.

He moved his hand. It was not fast. It was early. Her fist met his palm. She felt the bones of his hand go, then his wrist, then his forearm to the elbow. He looked at the arm. It hung bent in places where an arm has no joints. Then it straightened, a joint at a time, with small wet sounds, and he flexed his fingers.

"Ow," he said mildly.

He hit her once, with the heel of the other hand, in the middle of the chest. Her breastbone folded in. She went backward off the doorstep, down the slope through the mist, and fetched up against a boulder twenty paces below with her ribs driven into her lungs. She lay there breathing blood while the ribs pulled themselves out of her lungs and went back where they belonged. It took longer than she was used to.

I have been hit since by things that brought down mountains. Nothing has ever hit me as hard as that old man's palm. You can stop rubbing your own chest. It's only a story.

When she came back up, he had refilled his own cup.

"That's not you," he said. "Whatever that was. It's very good, I'll grant you. But it isn't you."

She turned from ten paces. He leaned aside, as an old man leans out of a draught. Behind him the cliff split open from the mouth of the cave to the height of three men. A shelf of rock slid down across the path.

"You have no reason of your own to be here," he said, picking grit out of his hair.

She went in close. She put her thumb into his left eye to the second knuckle. He took her forearm in both hands and twisted it off at the elbow with a wet, tearing sound.

She stepped back and looked at the stump. He looked at it too, with his one eye. The other was already filling back into its socket.

"Sorry about that," he said. "You'll grow another. I've had to."

Her arm grew back over the rest of the morning while she fought with the other one. First came the bone, wet and pale. Then the meat came over it, and then the skin. She did not stop to watch.

At noon she turned all the way round. The pines below the cave went flat in a ring twenty-five paces wide. The mist blew out of the ring and stayed out for an hour. His kettle went over the edge of the slope. He stood in the middle of the ring with his robe smoking, his face and hands blistered. The blisters were already going smooth again. He watched the kettle bounce away down the rocks until he could not see it.

"That," he said, "was a good kettle."

He let her come at him again. "It's pointless," he said, while she did. "Stop."

All afternoon she looked for the door. She went for his throat, and it closed behind her hand. She went for the backs of his knees, and he stood up anyway. She went for the base of his skull, the place the drill master had shown her for when everything else failed, and it held. Every door she found in him closed again before she was through it. For the first time since the drill ground, the fastest way led nowhere.

He hit her only when he had to. When he did, it was enough. By evening she had been broken in more places than in all the years since the cart.

At dusk he sat down on his doorstep. The cup he had poured for her was still there, where he had set it in the morning. Rain had got into it.

"What's your name?" he asked.

"Liè."

"That's what they call you down there. I asked what your name is."

"Liè."

He looked at her for a long time across the cup. Then he nodded, as if he had been told something more than a name.

"Liè, then," he said. "Sit down a moment, Liè. You're bleeding on my step."
