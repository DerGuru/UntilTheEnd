The hands changed. I won't give you all of them. The boy who stood on the base of the post grew tall enough not to need it. His son needed it again for a while, then didn't. After that I lose count. The fire's gone low. Put something on it. I'll want the light for this part.

The house on the hill had become a palace. The wall of old dressed stone still stood at the heart of it, around the courtyard, but new walls ran out from it in every direction, and the town below had grown up the hill to meet them. The post in the courtyard was the third post on that spot. The dark ring at its foot was the same ring.

Liè stood in the courtyard. She had stood there, on and off, for longer than anyone in the palace had been alive.

She was seldom needed now. When she was, she walked out to wherever the trouble was and stood where it could see her. Most trouble ended there. Armies that had marched for a month to meet the lord's would see her on the far side of a valley and start for home. She remembered a time when she had needed to fight. She did not remember missing it.

The last of the hands had grown up around her. As a small boy he had hidden behind her legs from his tutors, and she had let him, as she let the flies walk on the post. As a man he touched her shoulder without looking at her, often in the middle of saying something to someone else. He rested his hand there as he might have rested it on the back of a familiar chair. He had learned the reasons. He could explain a crime from the beginning and in order, as his fathers had. He had never needed to learn why anyone bothered.

In his time the lord's lands reached the northern mountains. He wanted those too. He sent an army north to see what was in them, and he went along himself, for the air. Liè went because she went with everything now.

On the fourth day of the march they came down into a valley where nothing grew. The trees stood bare in a wide ring, gray to the root. The grass between them had gone to dust. In the next village an old woman told the soldiers that a young man who wanted something from the sky had lived there once. He had taken it out of everything around him, she said, then gone on up into the mountains to look for more. The soldiers made the sign against bad luck and marched faster. Liè walked through the middle of the ring. Under her feet the ground gave back no hum at all.

On the ninth day in the mountains she felt it.

It came from the north, from higher up, a long way off. At first she took it for the mountain. Some places had a warm of their own, thick and full. She had stood on mountains like that before. This was not a mountain. It was one thing, in one place. It was larger than every hum in the army behind her put together, and it was very old. It felt less like a man than like a place where a man had been sitting for a long time.

She went to the lord's tent and told him. It was what she did with every warm she found.

He sent two men up with his terms. They came back down the next evening. An old man lived in a cave up there, they said. He had given them tea. He had told them, politely, that he did not own anything the lord could want.

"Did he kneel?" the lord asked.

The two men looked at each other.

"He poured the tea, lord," one of them said. "He was sitting down the whole time."

In the morning the lord explained it to Liè, from the beginning and in order, as his fathers had. A power stood in his mountains that did not kneel to him. It had been asked. It had poured tea. He finished his explanation and reached for his bread. With the other hand, still half turned toward his breakfast, he found her shoulder.

"Remove it," he said.
