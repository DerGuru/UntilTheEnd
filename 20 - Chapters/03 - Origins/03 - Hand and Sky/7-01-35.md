The city sat on a river plain among low hills, with its back to a line of blue mountains. She came down into it one afternoon in late summer, a long time after the pass. I can't tell you how long. The numbers blur at that distance. Twenty thousand people lived there, perhaps thirty. The walls were clay and timber, faced at the bottom with river stone, and high enough to stop a cart but not much more.

She walked through the market because it was in her way. It smelled of turmeric, cured leather, and a dark fermented bean paste that the women sold from big clay jars with wooden lids. One of them held out a horn spoon of it as she went by. She tasted it. It tasted of salt, smoke, and something under both that she had never tasted before. The woman laughed at her face.

"Nobody makes it but us," the woman said. "Nobody else can."

Children were playing in the square by the well. They had drawn a grid on the flagstones in chalk and were throwing pebbles into it, hopping, arguing about where the pebbles had landed. She stopped to watch them. She had seen that game before. She had seen it on the plains under the lord, in the pass village, and in the street below the old woman's roof. Every place she had seen it believed it had made it up.

At the center of the city stood a temple with a curved roof. The roof had been patched with copper over the years, sheet by sheet, and the copper had gone green. In the late light it shone a color she could see for a long time afterward with her eyes shut.

She left by the north gate and climbed a low hill outside the walls to watch the sun go down. She had taken to hills by then. From the top she could see the whole city laid out below: the walls, the market smoke, and the green roof at the middle of it all.

North of the city, beyond the hill, the fields ran out toward the mountains. In one of them there was a climber.

She had felt him as soon as she came out of the gate. He sat alone in a stubble field a little way off the road, cross-legged, his face to the mountains. His warm stood in him high and thin. It was going up as she watched, a step, then another.

She knew how long it had. She had felt enough of them by then.

The sun went down behind her. The light went the color of the copper on the temple roof. In the field below, the climber's warm went up another step, and she felt it in her teeth.

She could go down. She knew how long it would take her to reach him: the hill, the road, the field. She would be there before the light changed. She knew what she would do when she got there. She would kneel, lay her hand on his chest, and lean. She knew what came after that, too. A burning grove. A ferry overturned. A town in a river bend. A summit opening under her like a mouth, and a man looking at her face the whole time.

She put her hands behind her back. She held one wrist in the other hand, hard, as an old man holds a dog on a short rope.

She watched.

His warm went up the last step at dusk. She felt it break loose of him and go out over the fields in a ring, all at once, without a sound. The sound came after. The walls cracked first. The clay split along lines nobody had known were in it, every one of them at the same moment. The timber went next. The temple roof bent inward, slowly, folded down the middle, and fell in on itself with a noise she felt through the soles of her feet. The whole city made a long low sound as it went down, like a great animal pressed flat under a hand.

The market caught fire. The turmeric burned with a yellow flame, very bright, almost beautiful. The smell came up the hill to her on the evening wind.

The children were in the square. I remember the smoke. Not the faces. Your brush has stopped. Don't write that down. No. Write it down.

Below her, in the field, the climber stood up. He looked at his hands. Then he walked away north, toward the mountains, without looking back at the city.

She did not move. She stood on the hill with her hands behind her back until the fires burned down. The turmeric smelled like summer.

Three days later she walked down through the ruins. The north gate was a heap of clay. The market was ash. In the courtyard of the temple, the copper roof lay where it had fallen, warped into a long buckled sheet. Its green had burned black. She stood over it for a moment.

Then she kept walking.
