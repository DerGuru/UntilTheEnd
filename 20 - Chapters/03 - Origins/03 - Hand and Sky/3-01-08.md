The eastern king's city sat on a rise above the plain, with the river looped around three sides of it. It was the largest place Liè had seen since the empire whose road she had walked in on. Its walls were stone at the foot and rammed earth above, so thick that a cart could have been driven along the top. Inside them, the traders said, there were granaries enough to feed the whole plain through two bad years.

The lord wanted the granaries. He did not want them burned.

When the army came over the last rise, the fields around the city were already on fire. The townspeople had set the harvest alight themselves, everything from the river to the foot of the hills, so that the lord's men would find nothing to eat. The smoke lay over the plain for nine days. For the rest of that summer everything in the camp tasted of burnt grain.

On the first morning the lord walked Liè down to the edge of the moat and showed her the gate. It was a deep gate, a passage through the wall as long as a house. The townspeople had filled it. They had packed it with earth and rubble from end to end, so that the gate itself was only a lid on a plug of stone.

"You'd get through that," the lord said. "Eventually. With your hands, while they dropped rocks on you, and my men stood out here waiting for you to finish." He looked along the wall at the roofs behind it. "Or you'd burn it. Then I'd own a very large hole full of ash, and no granaries."

He took his hand off her shoulder.

"We'll wait," he said.

The siege was mostly waiting. The waiting was mostly flies. The men diced for one another's rations, then for one another's boots, then for nothing at all. In the second month the flux went through the camp. Men squatted in rows along the latrine trench with gray faces, and forty of them died of it, more than had died at the first town. In the mornings the stones at the foot of the wall were dark with the river's damp. By midsummer the moat stank. The defenders threw their dead into it at night rather than give up ground to bury them. The heat did the rest. The smell came over the camp on the evening wind, sweet and thick.

The drill master did not get the flux. Every evening he went down to the edge of the moat and shouted at the men on the wall. He told them what he thought of their mothers, their walls, and their king. They shouted back. On good evenings it was the best entertainment in the camp.

Liè stood at the edge of the camp after dark, facing the city. She could feel it from there. Thousands of people were packed behind the wall, and their hum pressed out through the stone. Over the weeks it changed. It did not grow quieter. It grew thinner, in patches, as cloth wears thin at the elbows. Here and there a hum went out. She felt the city starve one hearth at a time.

She went on with the figures at night. She had a hundred of them by then. Most were the drill master's stances with something altered: a heel, a breath, or the angle of a wrist. Each one sent the warm into her body somewhere new. Her shins could break a spear shaft by then. Her forearms were dense as seasoned oak.

In the third month, in a pear orchard the townspeople had not managed to burn, she found one that went the other way.

It began as a turn. She had been working the low stance with her hips swung through instead of set, the spear coming round with them. Halfway through the turn, the warm did not sink. It moved with her. She felt it swing out along her arms and keep going past her hands, past the end of the spear, as water keeps going when the bucket stops.

Three paces in front of her, the bark of a pear tree split open from the roots to the height of her head.

Liè stood still. She looked at the tree. The edges of the split were smoking a little.

She did it again. Nothing happened. She did it a hundred times before it happened a second time. After that it came more often. She learned that it came with the turn, not the thrust, and only when her breath went out on it. She learned that it came easier when she stopped trying to push it. By the end of that week she could split bark at five paces. By the end of the month she could split a trunk at ten.

She noted, too, that the force arrived before the point did. A man ten paces off would be broken before her spear was anywhere near him. She noted it as she noted everything then, as a fact about how the world was put together.

I can still do it. I could split that pine behind you from here, if you'd like to see. No, sit down. I'm only telling you.

One morning near the end of the fourth month, the lord's boy came out to the orchard to fetch her. He stopped at the edge of it with his board of clay held against his chest. Every tree in the first two rows stood split from the roots up, each trunk open at the height of a man's throat. A few of them were still smoking.

The boy ran back for the lord.

The lord came out slowly, with his hands behind his back. He walked down the first row, then the second. He put his fingers into one of the splits and took them out again, fast, because the wood was still hot.

He looked at the trees. Then he looked across the moat at the gate.
