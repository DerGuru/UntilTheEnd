That first winter in the pass, Jìng did not sleep at all. She had a stone hut above the warm pool, with a goat-hair curtain for a door, and she spent the long nights outside it on a flat shelf of rock, working.

She started with what she knew. The drill ground had taught her a handful of shapes, a long time ago, by accident. One was the low stance, weight back, heel lifted. Another was the turn, with the hips swung through and the breath going out on it. Each of them opened a door. Each of them let the warm out further and faster than it wanted to come. She had spent centuries walking through those doors, one after another, until there was nothing left on the far side to walk into.

She needed the opposite.

So she turned them round. She stood in the low stance with the heel down flat instead of lifted, and the warm in her settled, a little, as water settles when you stop stirring it. She swung the turn the other way, with the breath coming in on it instead of going out. The warm came in with the breath and lay down. She tried a hundred shapes in the dark that did nothing at all. She tried a dozen that did the wrong thing, fast, and had to stand very still afterward until the rock under her stopped humming.

By midwinter she had nine that worked. By the thaw she had seventeen. Each one asked the warm to go a little slower than it wanted. None of them forced it. Laid end to end, they took about as long as it takes a pot of water to come to the boil.

She showed them to him in the goat field, in the gray before dawn, with the frost still on the stones.

He watched her go through them once. He said nothing, which was unusual for him. Then he stood up and did them, badly, out of order. She stopped him at the third one and moved his foot. She stopped him at the fifth and pressed his shoulder down. At the end of the morning he could do them in order. He stood in the field with steam coming off him and counted on his fingers.

"Seventeen," he said.

"Yes."

"Why seventeen?"

"That's how many there are."

He thought about that seriously, as he thought about everything, and decided it was a good answer.

He did them every dawn after that, in the goat field, before he took the goats up. His warm steadied. It still grew, because it was young and his. But it grew as a tree grows, a ring a year, and not as a fire grows when the wind gets into it. She could feel the difference from her hut without getting up.

The years went by in the pass. He grew a beard. He married the headman's younger daughter, who was afraid of Jìng and brought her cheese anyway. Every few years a child in the village turned out to have a spark in it, and he brought the child to the goat field at dawn. He taught them the seventeen. He was a better teacher than she was. He explained things. He made jokes about the goats. When a child flared, he said "Not so hard. Listen to it first," exactly as she had said it to him, then explained what it meant, which she never had.

Every spring, when the caravan came up out of the city on its way to the sand, she went down to the pool to meet it.

The old woman came every year. Every year she was slower. Her hip was worse, then her other hip, then her eyes. The year came when she no longer rode at the front but in a litter between two donkeys, with the boy from the sandstorm riding the gray donkey ahead of her. He had grown up into a narrow, quiet man with her squint. He held his hand out at arm's length to the sky at dusk, as she had taught him.

"He's not bad," the old woman said, from the litter. "Not as good as you. You never got lost." She considered that. "You never knew where you were, either. I don't know how you managed it."

Jìng sat by the litter that night until the old woman fell asleep. She held one of her hands. It was very light, all knuckles, like a bird's foot.

The next spring the caravan came up the pass without a litter. The narrow quiet man rode at the front on a new donkey. When he saw her waiting by the pool, he got down and came over to her with something in his hand.

"In the winter," he said. "On the roof, in her chair. My mother found her in the morning." He held out his hand. "She said to give you this. She said, 'Give it to the girl, she'll only get lost without it.'"

It was the cord. Plaited goat hair, dark with handling, with its uneven knots. Jìng took it. It weighed almost nothing.

She did not cry. She noticed that she did not. She stood by the warm pool with the cord in her hand while the caravan men climbed into the water up to their chins, as they always did, groaning, as they always did.

I had that cord for a long time. Longer than you'd believe. I lost it in a river in the end, crossing on a bad night. I went back for it the next day, and the river had moved. Hold your hand out, toward the fire. No, flat. There. That's how she held hers.

That night Jìng climbed up to the shelf of rock above her hut. The Nail was where it always was. She held her hand out at arm's length, flat, and laid the edge of it along the black line of the mountains.

Four fingers.

She ran the cord through her fingers until she came to the first knot. Then she sat on the rock with it in her lap until the sky went gray. Below, the boy from the pass, who was not a boy anymore, came out into the goat field and began the first of the seventeen.
