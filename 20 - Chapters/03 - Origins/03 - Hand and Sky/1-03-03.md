The lord kept them waiting until the shadow of the post had moved across three flagstones. The captain stood at ease beside her the whole time and did not shift his weight once. Mei stood because she had been brought there to stand. Men came and went around the edges of the courtyard with sacks and water jars. Every one of them found a reason to look at her on the way past.

When the lord came, he came from the drill ground, not from the house. He was younger than she had expected, younger than his captain by twenty years. He had a farmer's shoulders and a face weathered early. A boy trotted at his heel with a board of wet clay and a stylus. The lord stopped in front of her and looked her over without hurry, from her bare feet to her face.

"Did she come willingly?" he asked.

"She came, lord," the captain said.

"That isn't what I asked."

"It's the best I can give you."

The lord nodded, as though that were an answer worth having. The boy pressed a mark into the clay.

"I'm told you stood up from under a cart," the lord said to her.

"Yes."

"I won't ask you to lie down under another one. But I'd like to see something." He lifted two fingers without looking round, and a soldier came off the wall with a knife already in his hand. "May I?"

Mei held out her arm.

The soldier knew his work. He opened her forearm from wrist to elbow in one stroke, deep enough that the fat showed yellow before the blood came. Then the blood came. It ran off her fingers and spotted the flagstones beside the older stains. She watched the cut close. The lord watched it close. The soldier had stopped watching after the first moment and looked somewhere above her head.

The boy with the clay had forgotten to write. The lord touched him on the shoulder, and the stylus moved.

"Good," the lord said. "Now show me what you do."

He looked around the courtyard. Along its east side ran a stretch of the old wall, dressed stone, the carvings on it worn almost smooth. Two of his guards stood at their posts there, ten paces apart. He walked over between them and laid his palm flat on one block at the height of his chest.

"This one," he said, and stepped back.

Mei looked at the stone. She had not pushed the warm out of herself at anything in a long time. Once, long ago, she had lit fires with it for people who were cold. She reached for it now and found it where it had always been, low in her, banked, enormous. She let it go at the stone.

The stone did not break. It went white, then it went. The blocks on either side went with it, then the mortar between them, then the packed earth behind. A wash of heat crossed the courtyard and lifted the hair on every head in it. Chips of dressed stone sang off the flagstones and rattled away into the corners.

The guard on the left had turned his face away in time. His beard was smoking. He beat at it with the flat of his hand and stared at the hole in the wall. The guard on the right had not turned in time. He sat down hard with his hands over his face. Between his fingers the skin had gone the color of a boiled crayfish, and it was already lifting in blisters. Then he started to scream.

Where the block had been there was a hole a cart could have driven through. Through it Mei could see the hillside beyond, a goat on the hillside, and the goat looking back.

The lord walked over to the hole. He looked at it for a while. He looked at the screaming guard. Then he said to the captain, "Get him to the woman with the honey pots." The captain hauled the man up by his belt and took him.

The lord came back to Mei. He did not look angry. He looked like a man who had added up a column and found it longer than he expected.

"I pointed at one stone," he said.

"Yes."

"You gave me the wall." He glanced back at the hole. "Next time I'll want the stone. Only the stone."

Mei looked at the hole too. It had not crossed her mind that there was a difference.

"What do you want?" he asked her.

She thought about it. She thought about it honestly, because he had asked honestly, and at the bottom of the thinking there was nothing at all.

"Nothing," she said.

He took that in. Something in his face eased.

"Then we'll get on," he said. "I want a great deal. There'll be plenty to go round."

He put his hand on her shoulder. It was heavier than the captain's. It did not rest there. It settled, as a man's hand settles on the neck of a horse he has just paid for.

Some nights, with my back to the cold like this, I can still feel where it sat. The heel of it on the muscle. Two fingers on the bone. She did not shrug it off. Shrugging would have meant she minded.

He turned her with it, gently, until she faced the far end of the courtyard. Beyond an arch lay a strip of beaten earth below the north wall. A hoarse man was shouting at forty others with spears.

"Start there," the lord said. "He'll teach you where the stone ends."
