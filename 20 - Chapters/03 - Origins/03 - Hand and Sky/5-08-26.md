The market town sat at a ford on a slow brown river, where three roads met. It smelled of fish, dung, and roasting chestnuts. She came into it in late autumn with the crowd off the north road. She had come down out of the mountains on purpose. All the way south she had thought about the woman under the byre. She remembered the arm giving in her grip. She remembered the scream, which had been for her, not for the mud. If she was going to be among people, she would have to learn how.

The crowd pressed on her from every side, all those small hums pushing at once. She had forgotten that. She stood still at the edge of the market and let it press until she could bear it.

A boy came through the crowd fast, low, with his eyes on the purse of the woman ahead of her. He misjudged the gap. He ran into her arm.

She did not move. The boy did. He bounced off her as off a wall and sat down hard in the mud, holding his wrist. His mouth opened without any sound coming out of it. The wrist was broken. She could see it from where she stood. Around them the market went quiet in a widening ring. People looked at the boy, then at her, then at her arm.

She walked away before the quiet could turn into anything else. She walked until she found a yard behind a dyer's shed, with blue water running out from under the fence, where a man was shouting at five young men with sticks.

He was short, broad, and bandy-legged. He had a stick of his own, worn black where his hand held it. He also had a voice that carried over the fence, the dyer's shed, and probably the river. The young men were hitting each other with their sticks, slowly, by numbers. Every time one of them got hit, he made a face, staggered a step, and swore.

She watched them over the fence until the light went. She watched how they fell.

In the morning she came back and asked him to teach her.

He laughed. He laughed for some time, leaning on his stick. Then he looked at her again, at her shoulders, at her hands. He stopped laughing. "Widow?" he said.

She did not answer.

"I get widows," he said. "Men come back from the north road and find their brother's moved into the house. Can you pay?"

"I can carry water."

He looked at the two big jars by the gate, which had to be filled from the river every morning. Then he looked at his knee. "Gods know I can't," he said. "All right. You'll come when I shout."

"What will you shout?"

"What's your name?"

"I don't have one."

He took that without a blink. "Then I'll shout 'you,'" he said. "You'll know it's you because you'll be the only one not hitting anybody."

On the first day he put a stick in her hands and set her opposite the smallest of the young men, a farmer's son with ears that stood out from his head. "Go slow," he told the boy. "She's new."

The boy swung, slowly, at her shin. She let it hit. The stick broke in half.

Everyone looked at the stick. The boy looked at his hands.

"Rotten," the teacher said, after a moment. He threw the half he had picked up over the fence. "Fetch another. You. Again."

It went on like that. She was a terrible student. The young men liked her for it, because she was worse than any of them had ever been. When they hit her, she did not move. When they swung at her, she was somewhere else before the stick arrived, and then stood there, waiting, while they tried to work out where she had gone. When the teacher told her to fall, she fell. She did it a long breath after the blow had landed, all at once, like a tree.

"No," he said. "No. You fall like a sack somebody forgot to drop. The stick hits, then you go. Not the stick hits, you think about your mother, then you go."

"Like this?"

"Gods, no. Again."

He taught her to breathe. She did not breathe hard, he said, and everybody breathed hard. "Look at him," he said, and pointed his stick at the farmer's son, who was bent over with his hands on his knees, blowing like a bellows. "That's a man who's been fighting. You look like a woman who's been sitting on a wall." She tried it. She panted like a dog in summer, and the young men laughed until two of them had to sit down.

She carried his water every morning. In the evenings his wife fed all of them from a pot of lentils on the yard fire. The young men talked about girls, horses, and each other's sisters. They laughed and threw lentils. She sat with her back against the fence and listened. She did not know she was listening until the teacher's wife put a bowl in her hands and said, "You're smiling at them."

She stopped. The wife laughed at her and went back to the pot.

Some evenings they talked about the east. A salt trader had told the teacher's wife that out past the hills there were men who went off alone into the woods and came back wrong, or did not come back at all. The woods around them died, the trader said. They died in rings.

"Hill men," the teacher said. "They eat roots and think they're gods."

She said nothing. She remembered a ring of gray grass on a plain.

By midwinter she could fall. By the thaw she could take a blow on the forearm, let the arm drop, and hiss through her teeth at the right moment, no later. She learned to let her shoulders slump after a long bout. She learned to be beaten by a hair, so that the man who beat her walked away pleased with himself and nobody wondered why.

That was the hardest part. Losing by a long way was easy. Losing by a hair meant watching every blow come in and choosing which of them to take. She had never in her life had to choose not to.

At the spring fair the teacher put her in the ring against the farmer's son, in front of half the town. The boy had grown over the winter. He came at her hard. She held him off for as long as a woman her size reasonably could. Then she let a blow slip through her guard onto her temple. She went down at once, the moment the stick touched her. She lay in the mud with her ears ringing and her chest heaving. Her lip was split. She had let it split.

The crowd cheered the boy. He stood over her, flushed, and held out his hand to pull her up.

The teacher was leaning on the fence with his stick. He nodded to her, once.

"Better," he said. "Still late."

I've been losing ever since. I'm very good at it now. You've seen me do it, more than once, and you've never said anything.

She left the town in early summer, when the jars by the gate were full, and took the east road. The teacher was shouting at a new widow over the fence and did not look round.
