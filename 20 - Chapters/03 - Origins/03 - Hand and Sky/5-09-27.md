The valley was a good one. It ran east between two long ridges thick with oak and chestnut. A stream came down the middle, with a village of charcoal burners at the lower end. The air there tasted of smoke and leaf mould. In the woods there were deer and boar. There were other things too, with a little warm in them: a white stag that the burners left offerings for, a kind of long-bodied cat that nobody had ever caught, and an old bear with a hum in it like a hive.

She had followed the talk east all summer, from village to village, through hill country she did not know. Every village had a story about a wood that had died. Every story was set a little further east. In the autumn she came over the northern ridge above the charcoal valley and felt something out on the east road, a long way off, coming this way. She sat down on a shoulder of rock above the trees to wait. From there she could see the whole valley and the smoke from the burners' mounds going up in the still mornings. Nobody came up there. From the rock, the valley looked like something laid out on a table.

The young man came in on the east road a few days later. His warm was bright and quick, piled up too high, like a fire built in a hurry with green wood.

He was polite to the burners. He was young and good-looking. He paid for his bread with meat, because he hunted every day and caught a great deal. He asked them about the white stag. They told him it was not to be touched. He nodded and went into the woods.

She watched him from her rock.

He killed the stag on the first day. She felt its warm go out, then felt his climb. He had eaten its heart where it fell. On the second day he found the long-bodied cat that nobody had ever caught, and on the third he found two more. On the fourth day he killed the bear. That took him a whole morning. When it was done, his warm stood up in him so high and so thin that she could feel it shake.

The burners said nothing. He was polite, and he paid in meat.

On the fifth day the trees around his clearing began to die. It began with the leaves. They went brown in a ring, then gray, then fell all at once, as though a frost had come for that patch of wood and no other. The ring grew a little every hour. Inside it the birds went quiet. The burners came up the stream to look. They stood at the edge of the gray, muttering, then went back down.

On the sixth day they came up again with axes and spears, about twenty of them. They meant to tell him to go. By then the ring was five hundred paces wide, and everything in it was gray. They stood at the edge of it and looked across the dead ground at the young man sitting cross-legged in the middle. They did not go in. After a while they went back to the village and began to load their carts.

By then there was nothing left to object with.

She watched him every day from her rock. She watched him work in the evenings, when he got up from his sitting and moved through his forms in the dead clearing. She knew them. The low stance, the weight back, the heel lifted the width of a finger. The turn, with the breath going out on it. He did them badly, too fast, pushing.

Somebody had taught him that.

On the seventh day, at dusk, his warm began to climb and did not stop.

She felt it from the rock as a pressure behind her eyes, then as a hum in her teeth. Everything in the valley that still had a hum went quiet under it. The last birds left the trees all at once and went north in a long black scarf. In the dead clearing the young man stood up. He spread his arms. The light around him went strange, too clear, as if the air had been wiped.

She stood up on her rock.

She could go down. She knew she could. She knew how it would go, too, if she did: the turn, at a run, from twenty paces, and whatever was in the middle of the clearing would stop being in the middle of anything. She took a step toward the edge.

Nobody was standing behind her. No hand rested on her shoulder. She had not had one there in a long time, and she found she was waiting for it.

She stood on the edge of the rock and did not go down.

The clearing went white. The sound came after, a long low crack that she felt in her feet. The dead trees around the ring fell outward all together, flat, their tops pointing away from the middle. Where they had stood, gray dust lifted and drifted. Under it the ground had gone smooth and dark, like a glazed pot. She had crossed ground like that once, on the plain, without slowing.

The young man was rising out of the middle of it. He went up through the dust without hurry, his arms still spread, and then higher, past the line of the ridges. For a moment she could see him against the last of the light. Then she could not.

He did not look down.

In the morning the burners' carts went out by the east road in a slow line. Nobody looked up at her rock. In the dead clearing a little gray dust still moved when the wind crossed it.

I've spoken of him before, in passing, to make a point about somebody else. He deserved better than that, or worse. Here is where I was standing.

It was the first time she had watched from a hill with nobody pointing, nobody stopping her, and all of it in reach.

It was not the last.
