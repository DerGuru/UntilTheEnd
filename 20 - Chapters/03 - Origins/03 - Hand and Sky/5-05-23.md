The hills ran out in the end, as hills do. Beyond the last of them lay a plain so wide that the grass went silver when the wind crossed it, then green again behind the wind. She came down onto it in a summer some years after the farmers. I can't tell you how many. It was long enough that the hills had stopped being a place she kept to and had become a place she happened to be.

There were herds on the plain. Small dun horses with a dark stripe down the spine. Wild cattle with horns as wide as a man's outstretched arms. Things that ate grass, and things that ate those. They drifted out of her path as she came and closed again behind her. Nothing on the plain had any warm in it. There was only the hum of all that life, thin and wide, laid over the grass like heat.

On the fourth day she crossed a ring of dead ground, two hundred paces across. The grass in it had gone gray to the roots. The herds grazed up to its edge and no further. In the middle the earth was smooth and dark, like a glazed pot, warm under her feet in the morning cold. Somebody had sat there once. She walked across it without slowing.

On the ninth day she saw the runner.

It stood alone on a low rise, grazing. At first she took it for a deer, from its size and its color. Then it lifted its head, and she saw that it stood on six legs. The front pair were short, tucked close under the chest. The other four were long and fine, built for running. It was dun along the back, pale underneath, with a dark stripe down each flank. When it shifted its weight, it moved as lightly as a bird on a branch.

She had not seen one in a very long time. She could not have told you when.

It saw her. It did not run. It watched her come with both ears turned toward her, chewing.

There was no other runner on the rise. There was none on the grass beyond it, as far as she could feel. She walked a whole day in a wide ring around the rise to be sure, out to where the plain met the sky. She felt for them as she had once felt for the warm in conquered towns. Horses. Cattle. Wolves, far off to the east. A great many birds. No runners anywhere.

When she came back, it had lain down in the grass with its long legs folded under it.

She had nothing to do. That was all it was. She had walked for years with nothing to do, and the nothing had grown loud enough to hear over the wind. The runner raised its head as she came up the rise. It was beautiful. She could see that it was. She could not feel it.

She killed it with one blow to the neck.

The bones gave with a soft, dry sound, lighter than she expected. They were hollow, like a bird's. She knelt and broke one of the long legs open with her thumbs, because she wanted to know what was inside. The marrow was thin and pale. It smelled faintly sweet, lighter than the marrow of anything she had broken before.

For three breaths, perhaps, it was enough. The sound of the bone. The smell. Something had happened, and she had made it happen.

Then the wind came back over the grass. So did the nothing.

She sat beside the runner until the flies found it. She had walked a whole day to make sure it was the last. She had known before she lifted her hand.

I've killed a great many things since, for less reason. Most of them I've lost. That one I've kept, because I made certain first.

In the morning there were vultures on the rise. She left the runner to them and walked north, toward the mountains.
