Xu Ran has the fire going before dark tonight. He built it high, with more wood than one evening needs, and now he sits with the notebook open on his knee. The ink is ground. A fresh brush lies across the page. 

Yun watches the flames climb. She lets him wait, and he lets her, which by now is a kind of courtesy between them.

"I said I would tell you about Liè," she says at last.

The brush dips.

"And I will." She leans back from the heat. "But first there was a hand, and a girl who let it turn her."

---

The river town sat where a brown river out of the hills ran into a green one out of the marshes. It was a fishing town with ambitions. It had a chest-high wall on the landward side that kept out nothing but goats. It had a market along the bank. At the end of every summer it had a threshing floor, beaten flat and shared by every household, where the whole town turned out to flail its grain before the rains came.

Mei had come for a season's work in the year the cart went over her. She had finished that season. Then she had stayed for the next one, because staying took no more effort than going, and she had none to spare for either. Nobody asked her to leave. Nobody asked her to stay.

They still watched her. The women at the drying racks went quiet when she passed, then louder than before once she was by. The children dared each other to touch her sleeve. One boy had done it in spring and run off shrieking with joy. By midsummer the dare was worth nothing, because every child in town had done it twice already.

On the last day of threshing she stood in the line with the others and swung her flail from first light. The chaff rose and hung gold in the air. Men stopped to cough, drink, and wipe their faces, then took up their flails again. Mei did not stop. The others had long since given up remarking on it.

The net-maker's widow worked the flail beside her. She talked to everyone, including her goats. And now she approached Mei.

"You'll stay for the eels," the widow said between strokes. It was not a question. After threshing the town ate eels for three days and complained about them for even longer.

"Maybe," Mei said.

"Maybe," the widow told her flail. "She says maybe."

Mei heard the horses before the line did. Hooves on the river road, a good many of them, at a walk. The flails slowed one by one down the row as the others heard it too. Riders were nothing new in the river town. Traders came mounted, and so did the men from downriver who took a share of the fish each autumn. These were neither.

There were twelve. They rode in pairs, spears upright, the bronze heads red in the low sun. Each man held his horse on a short rein. They came through the gap in the goat wall, along the bank, and past the drying racks until the threshing floor lay in front of them. Then they stopped all together, as if one string had been pulled.

Their captain rode at the front on a gray mare that had been fine once. He was older than his men. His beard was cut close and gone gray at the edges. The top of one ear was notched where a blade had taken a bite out of it long ago. He looked across the floor once, found her and got down from his horse.

Nobody in the line said anything. The headman's wife had never once in two summers spoken to Mei directly. She looked at her now, then at the captain, then at Mei again. Mei watched her decide that whatever this was, it was not the town's business.

The captain came across the threshing floor through the chaff. He stopped at a careful distance. It was farther than most men stood from a stranger, and nearer than anyone in that town had stood to her since the cart.

"You're the one who stood up," he said. "From under the wheel."

Mei leaned on her flail. "Yes."

He looked at her hands, then at her face, as though one of them might tell him what the other would not. Neither did. He nodded anyway.

"My lord would like you to come to him," he said. "He asks. He doesn't order. I was told to make that plain, so I'm making it plain."

Mei said nothing.

"He'll feed you," the captain went on. "House you. Pay you in whatever you like."

"I don't like anything."

He considered that. "Then he'll pay you in that," he said, "and you'll both come out even."

One of the riders laughed and turned it into a cough. The captain did not look round.

Mei looked past him at the other eleven riders. Every spear stayed exactly where it had been. She looked at the threshing floor, at the half-beaten sheaves, and at the headman's wife, who had found something urgent to do with a basket. She thought about the next sheaf. She thought about the road, north or south or wherever it went. Neither thought weighed more than the other.

She leaned the flail against the post of the drying rack. She had nothing to fetch.

The captain saw that she would come before she took a step. He moved in beside her and set his hand on her shoulder. It was not a hard grip. It did not need to be. It was the hand of a man who had been sent to collect something and found it where he was told it would be.

I can't give you his face. I've tried, but it won't come. I can give you his hand, though. I can tell you how broad it was across the back, where the calluses sat, and how much it weighed. He said a name then, his lord's name, the name she was being taken to serve. I can't give you that one either.

Then he said a direction. "North."

The hand turned her toward the horses. She let it.

From the top of the drying rack, the boy who had first touched her sleeve watched her go. Behind her, before the horses had cleared the goat wall, somebody started up the flails again.
