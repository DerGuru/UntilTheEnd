She came back down the valley twenty years later, or thirty. It was autumn. The road from the pass was busier than it had been, and the carts on it carried stone.

The city was still there. The survivors had done what survivors do. They had stacked the broken sandstone into new walls, lower and uglier than the old ones. They had turned the cracks in the blocks to the inside, where nobody would have to look at them. The towers had not been rebuilt. The roofs were flat and bare. There was washing on some of them, and on one a goat. Nobody had planted new gardens.

Where the libraries had stood there were empty lots, swept clean, with weeds coming up between the flagstones. Nobody had replaced them. You cannot copy a scroll that is ash.

By the east gate, where the library of the two old men had been, there was a yard. A wall had been built around it of the same broken gold stone, chest-high, with a wooden gate. Over the wall she could see children in rows. There were perhaps twenty of them, of all ages, standing on the old library floor in the late sun. They were going through a set of forms, slowly, together. A woman walked up and down the rows.

She stopped outside the gate and watched.

The woman was in her thirties, perhaps, thin, with her hair tied back hard. She walked with her hands clasped behind her. When she unclasped them to move a child's arm, there was a scar across the palm of her right hand, old and shiny, as if she had once held on to something that was burning.

Beside the gate, set into the new wall at the height of a child's eyes, there was a small niche with a wooden lattice over it. Behind the lattice, on a bed of clean sand, lay a blackened wooden rod with a curl of burned paper still clinging to it.

A mother came up the street with a boy of about seven and stopped at the gate. The woman came to meet them. She had a board under her arm, painted white, with names written down it in columns. She looked at the boy for a long moment, then laid her scarred hand flat on top of his head and closed her eyes.

"Yes," she said. She opened her eyes and wrote his name at the bottom of the board. Then she crouched down in front of him.

"Here is how it goes," she said. "Nobody sits alone. Nobody sits within ninety li of a wall. Every spark is written down. And when the teacher says stop, you stop." She waited. "Say it back."

The boy said it back. He got the ninety wrong, and she made him say it again until it was right.

Then she stood up and saw the woman watching over the gate.

"Have you a child for us?"

"No."

The teacher looked at her, at her dusty clothes and the bare hands on the top of the gate. "Have you a spark yourself?" she said. "Anyone with a spark is to be written down. It's the rule now. The council voted it."

"No," she said.

She had learned to lose, a long time before, in a yard behind a dyer's shed. She had been losing ever since. The teacher looked at her a moment longer, then nodded and turned back to the rows. The boy had joined the end of the back one. He was copying the child beside him, badly, too fast.

"Not so hard," the teacher told him. "Slowly. Listen to it first."

She stood at the gate until the light went off the yard. Then she walked on, out through the north gate, and did not look back at the board with the names on it.

I thought it was a school. It was a school. What came out of it, and out of yards like it all over that country, is a long story. It starts well. I'll tell you another night. The sky's going gray at the edges; we haven't got the dark for it.

The path up the mountain was slippery. It always was.

The heap of rock where the old cave had been was grown over now, with birches standing up out of it as thick as her arm. A little to the right of it, someone had dug a new cave into the cliff. In front of the new cave mouth lay the old doorstep, in its two pieces, set side by side in a bed of gravel. On it sat the old man with a kettle on a small fire beside him.

He lifted two fingers. She lifted hers.

"Liè," he said.

She sat down on the doorstep beside him. He poured her tea. It was the rough stuff. She drank it.

"How do you like my cave?" he said.

She looked at it. Water was running down the rock at the mouth, and the floor inside shone wet in the firelight.

"Damp," she said.

"Yes." He considered it. "They always are."

She stayed a long time. She did not count the days. She did not tell him about the city, or the yard, or the board with the names. He did not ask. He talked about the stream, which had moved again. He talked about a pair of crows that had nested in the birches and fought all spring. He talked about the kettle, which was the third since she had known him, and the lid, which was still the first.

On the first evening he looked up at the sky over the peaks.

"Clear by tonight," he said.

It was. The stars came out over the mountains, one after another, more than she could have counted. She found the Nail. It was where it always was, over the black line of the north. She did not hold up her hand. She knew how far up it was.

They sat on the doorstep under it, the old man and the old woman, and talked about nothing until it was morning.

The fire is down to a bed of red. One stick has burned through in the middle and lies in two halves, glowing at the break. In the north, over the line of the trees, the sky is beginning to pale. Most of the stars are gone. One is left.

Xu Ran has stopped writing. He sits with the notebook open on his knee and the brush across it. He does not move.

Yun looks up at the last star for a while.

Then she lifts her arm. She holds it straight out at the sky with the fingers together and the hand flat, the palm turned outward. She lays the edge of her hand along the black line of the trees.

Xu Ran watches her.

"What are you doing?"

"Measuring."

He looks at the star, then at her hand. After a moment he puts the brush down and lifts his own arm. He points at the star with one finger.

"Not like that," Yun says. "You're not pointing at it."

She leans over. She takes his wrist, lightly, and turns his hand until the palm faces the sky. She straightens his elbow with a little push. Then she lets go.

He looks along his arm. He counts, under his breath.

"Three."

"Good," she says.

Above them the last star fades into the morning. Neither of them lowers a hand.
