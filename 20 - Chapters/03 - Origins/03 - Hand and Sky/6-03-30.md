On the way home the road went over the mountains before it went down to the sand. The pass was a long climb through black rock, old and glassy, that the herders said had once run down the slopes like water. Hot springs smoked in the gullies. Near the top, where the road leveled out, there was a village of herders: stone huts, goat pens, and a pool of warm green water. The caravan men lay in it up to their chins and would not get out.

It was the fifth crossing. She knew the village by then. She knew the headman's wife, who sold them cheese, and the headman's dog, which bit. She knew the springs ran hot. This time they ran hotter.

"It's the holy man," the headman's wife said, when the old woman mentioned it. She pointed up the slope above the village, where a cave mouth showed dark against the rock. "Sits up there. Has done for twelve years. Never comes down. We take him bread." She frowned up at the cave. "The goats won't graze up there this summer. And the water's gone cloudy."

She had felt him as soon as the caravan came up into the pass. A warm, high above, piled up so tall and thin that it trembled. She had felt that before, on a rock above a valley of oaks. She knew how long it had taken there.

Not long now.

She sat by the warm pool that evening and thought about the ring. She could see it, laid over the slope, as clearly as if someone had drawn it in chalk: the cave at the middle, and the whole village inside the edge. The goat pens. The huts. The caravan's donkeys hobbled in the stony field. The old woman asleep on her pack with her bad leg out straight.

She had stood on a rock once and done nothing. She remembered how the dead trees had fallen, all pointing the same way.

When the camp was asleep, she went up.

The slope was warm under her bare feet. The scrub on it had died in a wide circle round the cave, gray and brittle. The holy man sat in the cave mouth with his legs crossed and his hands on his knees. He was older than the young man in the valley. His head was shaved. He was very thin. His face in the starlight was calm and wet with tears. Around him the air shook as it does over a fire.

She stood in front of him.

"Stop," she said.

He did not open his eyes. He smiled, a little, at something far away.

His warm went up another step. She felt it in her teeth. Below them, in the village, a dog began to bark and would not stop.

She had never stopped anything. She had only ever broken things, burned them, and pushed them down. So she did what she knew. She knelt in front of him and laid her palm flat on his chest, over the breastbone, as she had once laid it on a lord's chest in a burning room. She did not push her warm into him to burn him. She pushed it down against his, against the direction it was going, as a man leans on a door against the wind.

His eyes opened.

His warm pushed back. It was very strong. It was stronger than anything she had felt since a cave in the northern mountains, and it was all going one way, up, through her hand. She leaned on it. The ground under her knees began to shake. Stones came loose above the cave and rattled down past them. The holy man's mouth opened. A sound came out of it that was not a scream yet.

She leaned harder.

Something gave. It gave in him, not in her. His warm stopped going up. For a moment it stood still between her hand and his heart, crushed between them, with nowhere to go.

Then it went sideways.

It went out of him through the rock to the left of the cave, all at once, with a crack she felt in her whole body. A stand of old pines on the slope below went up in one sheet of fire. The flames were white at the roots. The heat of it rolled back up the slope over them. Below the pines there was a goat fold with a hut beside it. The fire took both in the same breath.

The holy man fell back against the wall of his cave. His warm was gone, as a hearth is gone when someone kicks it apart and stamps on the coals. He sat there in the light of the burning pines, a thin old man with a shaved head, and wept like a child.

She stood up. The slope was still shaking. Stones were still rolling past her, one after another, bouncing down into the fire. Below, people were shouting. The dog was still barking. Every part of the mountain was moving except her.

There was a young man on the path below the cave. She had not felt him come. He had run up from the village with no shoes on, and he had stopped where the gray scrub began, with the firelight on one side of his face. He was looking at her. He had a warm in him. It was bright, quick, and very young. It was climbing, a little, from fright.

He did not look at the fire or the old man weeping in the cave. He looked at her, standing in the middle of all that shaking, as still as a post.

There had been a herder in the hut by the fold. She found him in the morning. That is the first one I can tell you about. There were others.

The caravan left the next day. The headman's wife did not sell them cheese. The old woman sat on her donkey at the head of the line and watched the loader come down from the burnt slope with soot on her hands. She did not ask. She held out a waterskin.

Behind them, as they went down toward the sand, the young man with no shoes stood at the edge of the village and watched them go.
