The line in front of her had a hole in it now, the width of one man. The men on either side of the hole were dragging their shields across to close it. Mei stepped into it before they could.

Her spear was too long for the space. She let it fall. She settled into the stance with her back heel lifted the width of a finger, felt the warm sink into her forearms, and struck the shield on her left with her fist.

The wicker went in. She felt both bones of the forearm behind it go under her knuckles, at the same instant as the wicker. The shield folded back into the man's face. He went down with his arm bent where an arm does not bend.

The man on her right swung an axe at her head. He was slow and close, with his mouth open. The bronze bit into her scalp above the ear. She let it. She caught his jaw in her hand, because that was faster than finding another spear. She closed her grip, set her feet, and pulled. The jaw came away. He stood for a moment with the rest of his face, making a sound that had no mouth left to come out of. Then the axe slipped out of his hand, and he went down after it.

Something went into her from the second rank. She looked down. A spear had come in under her ribs on the left and out through her back. A young man with wide eyes held the other end. He was already letting go. She did not give him the time. She took the shaft in both hands and pulled herself along it, hand over hand, until her face was a hand's width from his. The shaft stood out an arm's length behind her.

He said something. It might have been a name. She put her thumb into the hollow of his throat and pushed until something gave. He stopped saying it.

She drew the spear back out of herself and dropped it.

The lord's men had come up into the gap behind her. When she turned, the nearest of them stepped back from her before he could stop himself. The man beside him did the same. In front of her the town's line was coming apart. Behind her, her own men were backing away.

"Forward!" the drill master roared, with what was left of his voice. "Doors, you dogs! Doors!"

They went forward.

The townsmen broke. They ran for the gate across the plank bridge over the ditch. The men on the wall began to swing the gate shut before half of their own were through.

Mei reached it as the gap narrowed to the width of a body. She put her shoulder to the edge of the oak and stopped it. On the far side a dozen men threw their weight against it, then twenty. She felt the copper facing bite into her shoulder. She set her feet in the stance. The warm sank. The gate went back the other way.

The lord's men poured past her into the town.

Inside the wall it was a tangle of lanes, the thatch hanging low enough to touch. The fighting broke apart into a hundred small fights. Mei walked through them. A man came at her out of a doorway with a bronze knife, and she put him back through the doorway. Two came at her together from behind a cart, and she broke the cart over them.

The boy with the chipped tooth ran past her into a side lane, shouting, his red-hand shield up. A woman on the roof above heaved a quern stone over the eave. It took him on the crown of the head. He went down without a sound. The stone rolled off him into the gutter.

When Mei came to that lane he was lying on his back with his mouth open. She knew him by the tooth.

She stepped over him.

Your brush has stopped. Leave it a moment. I asked you to write the tooth down because somebody ought to know him, lying there, and feel something about it. I knew it would not be her.

By the middle of the afternoon the fighting was done. The men of the town lay in the lanes or knelt in the square with their hands on their heads. The town's women had taken the children into the houses and barred the doors.

The lord rode in through the gate with the boy and his clay trotting behind. He walked his horse into the square and looked around it. Then he leaned down from the saddle and put his hand on Mei's shoulder.

"All of it," he said.

He had pointed at a single stone once. Now he did not point at all.

She went to the nearest house and laid her palm on the thatch of its eave. It was old thatch, dry after a summer without rain. She let the warm go into it. It caught at once. She walked on to the next house and did the same. Behind her the fire ran along the eaves from roof to roof faster than she walked.

The doors came open. People ran out of them into the lanes, where the lord's men were waiting. Some stayed inside, and the roofs came down on them. A child ran out of one doorway with its hair alight. A soldier knocked it flat and beat the flames out with his cloak. Another soldier, two lanes over, did not bother.

Mei kept the same pace through all of it. As the afternoon went on, the hum of the town thinned around her. She noticed it as she noticed the heat, as a fact about the air.

By morning there was nothing inside the wall but ash and the stubs of mud-brick walls, still too hot to walk on. The lord rode once around the outside of it, slowly. When he came back to the gate where she stood, he held out his hand. The boy put the old tablet into it. The lord found the town's row, the two summers of marks, and the empty place where this summer's mark should have been.

"Mark it paid," he said.
