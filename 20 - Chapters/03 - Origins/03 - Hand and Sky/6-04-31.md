He caught up with them at the first well, two nights out on the sand. He came in out of the dark at a stumbling run, with a waterskin over his shoulder and his feet wrapped in strips of goat hide. He sat down at the edge of the firelight as if his legs had been cut.

He was perhaps eighteen. His nose had been broken once and set a little crooked. He looked at the loaders, the donkeys, and the old woman on her saddle. Then he found the one he wanted, and looked at nothing else.

"I want to learn what you did," he said.

She did not answer him. She was mending a pack strap. She went on mending it.

"On the mountain," he said. "I felt him every day. Since I was small. I thought it was the mountain. Then I felt you go up." He swallowed. "Then I felt you put him out."

"No," she said.

He nodded, as if that was what he had expected. Then he took the goat hide off his feet, which were bleeding. He lay down by the fire and went to sleep.

"Well," said the old woman. "He's keen."

He walked behind the caravan all the way across the sand. Nobody asked him to. The old woman made him wear a pair of sandals off a man who had died of fever on the last crossing, and they gave him water when he ran short, because he always ran short. In the heat of the day he lay as near to the loader's awning as the other loaders would let him. At night he walked beside her and said nothing for hours, then asked something.

"How did you know where he was going to break?"

She said nothing.

"Why did it go into the trees and not into you?"

She said nothing.

"Can you feel mine?"

She could. She had felt it since the slope. It was quick, bright, and young, a fire laid of good dry wood. Since that night on the mountain it had not settled. It went up a little every day, as if something in him had heard a door open. He did not know it was happening. She could feel it in the dark beside her, climbing.

She thought about the holy man in his cave, twelve years on the rock, and the village bringing him bread. She thought about the ring.

On the ninth night she stopped walking. The caravan went on past them, bells thin in the dark. He stopped too.

"Sit down," she said.

He sat on the sand, at once, with his legs crossed. She sat in front of him.

"Find it," she said.

He closed his eyes. She felt him reach for it. She felt it come up in him, fast, eager, all at once, flaring, as a fire flares when someone throws on oil. His face went red. His breath came quick.

"No," she said. "Not so hard. Listen to it first."

He opened his eyes. "Listen to it?"

"It knows what it is. You don't yet." She held out her hand, palm down, a little above his. "Let it come up by itself. Slowly. If it wants to go faster than you can follow, let it go down again."

He tried. It took him all night. It flared again and again. She said "No" every time, until he was soaked with sweat in the cold and the caravan was a long way ahead. Near dawn it came up slowly, a little way. It sat in his hands like a coal under ash. He looked down at his hands with his mouth open.

Those weren't my words. I had them from an old woman by a lake, a long time before, when it was my own fire that wouldn't sit still. I've given them away so often since that I sometimes forget whose they were.

They caught up with the caravan at noon. The old woman was awake under her awning. She looked at the two of them coming in over the dunes, gray with sand, and lifted an eyebrow.

"He's learning," she said. It was not a question.

"He's going to learn," the loader said, "whether I teach him or not."

That night, at the well, he sat across the fire from her with his knees drawn up. He had been quiet since dawn. He kept looking at his hands.

"What do I call you?" he said.

"Nothing."

"You must have a name."

"I don't."

"Everybody has a name."

"I had one," she said. "Nobody uses it now."

He frowned at her across the fire. He was a serious young man. He thought about everything as if it would be on a test. She watched him think about her.

"On the mountain," he said slowly. "When it all went. The rocks were falling. The trees were burning, and he was screaming. The whole slope was moving. I could feel it in my feet." He looked at her. "You weren't moving. You were just standing there. Everything was shaking except you."

He thought some more.

"Jìng," he said.

Still.

She did not say yes. She did not say no. She looked into the fire. Across it, the old woman pulled her blanket up round her shoulders and snorted.

"Better than 'girl,'" she said, and went to sleep.

The young man said it again in the morning, when he wanted water, as if he had always said it. The loaders heard him and began to say it too. By the steppe town, nobody on the caravan called her anything else.

Jìng let them. It was easier than not. That was what she told herself, crossing the last of the sand with the boy from the pass walking beside her, asking questions she mostly did not answer.

On the way back over the mountains in the spring, she did not go on with the caravan to the city. She stood in the herders' village by the warm green pool and watched the donkeys go down the far side of the pass, the old woman at the head of them, sitting sideways on her gray donkey with the white nose.

The old woman did not look back. She lifted one hand over her shoulder, and dropped it.

"Next crossing," Jìng said to nobody. Then she turned round. The boy from the pass was standing behind her, waiting to be told what to do.
