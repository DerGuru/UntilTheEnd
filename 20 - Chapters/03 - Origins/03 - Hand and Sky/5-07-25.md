The cold came in slowly. It took her feet first, then her hands, and the ice took the warmth from her back as fast as her body could make more. Her body went on making more. She lay and let it.

Above her the gap of sky went from white to blue to dark. Stars came out in it, a narrow handful. The old man sat on the lip with the blanket around him. Sometimes he talked. He said the wind was going round to the north. He said there would be snow by the second night. Once, toward morning, he hummed something under his breath, a tune with only four notes in it, over and over, until he lost it and stopped.

The glacier moved. She could feel it more than hear it: a creak from deep in the ice, then a long silence, then another creak somewhere else. Each time, the walls of her crack eased a little closer. By the second night they touched her shoulders. The snow came, as he had said. It sifted down into the crack and lay on her face. She did not brush it off.

On the third day the ice began to press.

It came on her chest first, gently, like a hand laid on a sleeper. Then it did not stop. Her ribs bent. She heard one go, then another, small and dull, like sticks broken under a blanket. Her body mended them. The ice broke them again. It was very slow. She had wanted it slow.

This, then, she thought.

She stopped pushing back. She had not known she was pushing until she stopped. The warm went quiet in her, all the way down. She let the ice have her chest, her shoulders, her hips. Above her the old man had stopped humming. The glacier stopped creaking. A drop of meltwater gathered on the ice beside her face and hung there, swelling. She waited for it to fall.

It was very quiet. It was quieter than it had been on the ledge under the snow, long before, the first time. She thought that this was what she had come for. She thought it clearly.

Then the thing came up out of the bottom of her.

She knew it this time. It had no words, and it came up through all the cold as if the cold were nothing. Her hands were clawing at the ice. Her mouth was open. Every part of her that had lain down was standing up inside her at once, screaming, and the scream had only one thing in it.

The world stopped.

The drop of water hung by her face and did not fall. The snow stood in the air above her, each flake where it was. The ice did not press. It did not ease. Her heart had stopped halfway through a beat and was held there, a fist that would not open.

It went on longer than under the snow. Long enough to see the stars in the gap above her and to see that none of them were moving. Long enough to find the old man's face at the lip of the crack. His breath stood out from his mouth in a white cloud, as still as everything else.

His eyes were not still. They were on her.

A line of cold ran through the ice beside her head, a hand's width to the left.

Then it went on.

The ice cracked along that line with a sound like a tree splitting. The wall on her left side fell away into the dark. The pressure went with it, all at once. She was lying on her back in a crack twice as wide as the one she had climbed into, with snow coming down on her face and water running under her.

She lay there for a while. Her ribs mended. Her hands stopped clawing at nothing. The thing that had come up out of her went back down to wherever it kept itself, and it did not look at her on the way.

When she climbed out, the old man was sitting where he had been. He looked down into the new crack. It ran away from them across the glacier, fresh and blue, wider than a man is tall.

"A little to the left," he said.

She sat down in the snow beside him. Her clothes were wet through. She was not cold. She was very tired.

"You held," he said, after a while. "Not long." He looked at her. "What pulled you back?"

"I don't know."

"Hm," he said. He did not ask again.

They stayed on the glacier that night and the next. He made a small fire on a flat rock at the edge of the ice and boiled snow in his kettle. Neither of them said anything about the crack.

On the third night she woke to a sound she had heard once in her life before. It was a crack, flat and huge, from below them this time. Then came a long roar, rising, as if the whole valley were drawing breath.

In the gray before dawn they walked down to the snout. The new crack ran all the way there from where she had lain. It went straight through the old tongue of ice that had held the milk-colored lake. The tongue was gone. So was the lake. Where it had been, there was a bowl of gray mud with stones sticking out of it, steaming a little in the cold.

Below, the valley was gray from wall to wall. The barley terraces were gone. So was the village. She felt for the hum of it. She found a few threads, thin and scattered, a long way down.

The old man looked at it for a long time. Then he tied his kettle to his belt.

"Come on," he said. "There'll be someone in the mud."

They went down. They dug all that day and into the night. He dug with his hands, slowly, as he had made his fire with a flint. She dug faster. They found a woman alive under the wreck of a byre, two children in a tree, and an old man who had ridden a door a long way down the valley. She pulled the woman out by the arm and broke it doing so. The woman screamed at her, not at the mud. The rest they found too. They laid them in rows on the slope above the mud, where the people from the next valley would see them when they came.

It wasn't bad luck. I know that now. It was a bill, and the village paid it.

When the people from the next valley came over the pass, the old man and the woman were already walking away up the far slope. At the top he stopped. He turned. He lifted his hand with the first and second fingers raised, held together.

She lifted hers. It came up slowly. Her fingers found the shape without being shown.

He nodded, as if she had answered a question. Then he went south toward his mountain, and she went the other way.
