The drill ground was a strip of beaten earth below the north wall, long enough for forty men to stand in two lines and wide enough for them to fall down in. The man who ran it had lost two fingers off his left hand and most of his voice to a lifetime of shouting. What was left of the voice came out like gravel poured from a sack. He had held a spear for the empire whose road she had walked in on. He had held it on the day that empire stopped needing spears.

He did not ask what she was. He put a spear in her hands and moved them apart on the shaft.

"Not like that. Like this. Weight back. Point low." He tapped her with the butt of his own spear: throat, armpit, groin, and the back of the knee. "Those are the doors. Everything else is wall. You don't knock on walls."

The first post she struck exploded. So did the spear. He looked at the splinters for a while, then handed her another spear. She broke three more before noon. After noon he stopped handing her spears and handed her a stick.

"Lighter," he said. "Lighter. You're not angry at it. You don't even know it. Lighter."

By the end of the first month she could put the point of a stick into a knot the size of a thumbnail and leave the post standing. By the end of the second she could do it with a spear. The other recruits had stopped laughing at the stick some time before that.

She did not tire. He found that out by testing it, as he tested everything. One evening when the others went to eat, he kept her back. He kept her until the moon came up, then until it went down. Toward dawn he sat on an upturned bucket with his chest heaving and watched her thrust at nothing, over and over, at the same pace she had kept since noon.

"You don't sweat," he said.

"No."

"Do it anyway, damn you. You're making the others look bad."

In the second month a recruit beside her in the line swung his butt end too wide and split her cheek to the bone. The drill master saw it open. He saw it close. He looked at her face for a long moment. Then he told the recruit to mind his butt end. He told her to tighten her guard. He went on down the line.

It was in the third month that she noticed it.

There was a stance he made them hold until their legs shook: feet wide, weight back, and the spear drawn in low against the hip. The others hated it. Mei held it because she was told to. One morning, holding it, she felt the warm move. It did not flare, as it did when she pushed it out. It sank. It ran down her arms and stayed there, packed into the meat of her forearms. Her grip closed on the shaft, and the wood creaked.

She tried the other stances. Nothing. She tried the thrust, the parry, and the turn. Nothing. She went back to the low stance, and the warm sank into her arms again, as water runs into a ditch once the sluice is up.

She wanted to know why.

It surprised her. She stood in the line with the shaft creaking in her hands and turned the wanting over. It had been a long time since she had wanted to know anything.

That night she went out past the latrines to where the hill broke into a scree of old building stone. She settled into the stance and felt the warm sink. Then she began to change it. She shifted her weight forward. Nothing. She set her feet wider. She turned her hips. She held her breath, then let it out. Most of it did nothing. Some of it did a little. One small change, the back heel lifted the width of a finger, sent the warm down into her hands so hard that her fingers ached.

She picked a block of dressed stone out of the scree, set it against the bank, settled into the stance, and struck it with her fist.

The stone held. Her knuckles did not. She felt three of them go, small dull cracks like green sticks breaking underfoot. She looked at her hand. Then she held it still and watched it mend. The bones slid back into line under the skin. The split across her knuckles closed. When it was done she flexed her fingers, settled into the stance, and struck the same place again.

By the tenth blow something had changed. The knuckles still broke, but later. By the twentieth they did not break at all. By the hundredth the stone did.

She did it every night after that. She did not hurry it. She would break a bone, wait, heal, and break it again. She watched the healing as closely as she had once watched clay in a kiln.

Ten thousand years later a sect would charge silver for this and call it body tempering. I can see you writing the word down. She never had a word for it. She had a stone, a fist, and all the nights there were.

She stopped going back to the barracks somewhere in that winter. It was not a decision. One night she worked until the sky went gray. The next night she did the same. On the third she understood that she had not slept since the first frost, and that she did not miss it.

The drill master found the stone in early spring. He came out past the latrines with his water skin one morning and stopped. The block was split down the middle. Its face was brown with old blood, layer on layer, a winter's worth. He looked at it for a long time. Then he looked at her hands, which were clean.

"Cheaper than posts," he said at last, and went to piss.

The lord came down to the drill ground on the first warm day, with the boy and his clay at his heel. Twenty posts stood in a row along the north wall, each as thick as a man. The lord walked along them. He laid his palm on the eleventh and stepped back.

Mei settled into the stance. She lifted her back heel the width of a finger. She struck from three paces with the spear, as the drill master had taught her, and the eleventh post burst at the height of a man's throat. The tenth and the twelfth stood where they were. A splinter as long as her forearm turned over once in the air and came down at the lord's feet.

He looked down at it. Then he put his hand on her shoulder.

"Good," he said.
