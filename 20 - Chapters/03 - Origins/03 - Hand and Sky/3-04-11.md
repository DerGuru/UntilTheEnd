There were other towns after the eastern king's city. I won't give you all of them. They run together in me now, one wall into the next. The stars have turned a long way since we sat down, so I'll give you only the village, because it was small.

The lord called her into the courtyard at the start of a campaign season. His hair had gone gray at the temples by then, and his farmer's shoulders had thickened. He had a cousin, he told her, his father's brother's son, who had a better claim to the hills than he did and had been saying so all over the south. The cousin had run out of friends. He had run to a village at the edge of the marshes, where his mother's people lived, and they had hidden him.

The lord explained the claim. He explained it carefully, in order, from his grandfather down. A swallow had built under the eave of the gatehouse, and Liè watched it go in and out.

"The cousin," the lord said at last, "and the village that hid him."

He put his hand on her shoulder. It was heavy and certain, as it always was.

The campaign went south and stayed south for a long time. Liè went with it.

The village was twelve houses and a well at the edge of the marsh. It had no wall. It had goats, reeds, and a headman who came out to meet the soldiers with his hands held open, to show that there was nothing in them.

The cousin was in a grain pit under the headman's floor. Two soldiers dragged him out by the arms. He was a soft man with his family's shoulders. He looked at Liè and knew what she was. He began to explain his claim.

She broke his neck while he was still explaining it. It took one hand. His head went round further than a head goes, and the explanation stopped in the middle of a word.

Then the village.

The headman was the first. He was still holding out his open hands. She put her fist through his chest. His hands closed on her arm, then opened again. An old man came at her with a reed knife, and she took it from him with the fingers still on it. A woman threw a pot. Two young men ran for the marsh. Liè turned once. The reeds where they ran went flat for thirty paces, then began to burn.

The children were in the headman's house. The fire took that house with the others.

It took less time than the cousin's claim had taken to explain. The soldiers watched from the track. None of them had been needed.

The campaign stayed in the south for three more summers. When it came home, the town below the lord's wall had been finished a long time. The raw thatch had weathered gray. There were children in the streets who had not been born when she left, and somebody had told them what she was.

In the courtyard the post still stood in its dark ring. The boy with the clay was there too. He was a man now, bearded, thickening at the waist, and carrying his board of clay under his arm. He stood at the heel of a young man with soft hands and the lord's jaw.

The young man came across the flagstones to her. He stopped where his father used to stop. He lifted his hand. It hung for a moment above her shoulder, as though he were not sure it would be allowed. Then it came down.

It was lighter. Liè noticed that. She noticed it as she would have noticed a change in the wind, and she did not ask why.

"The cousin?" the young man said. His voice was his father's, pitched higher.

"Yes."

"And the village?"

"Yes."

He nodded, as he had seen it done. The steward pressed a mark into the clay.

I can't tell you when the first one died. I've tried, on nights like this, with the frost settling on the blankets. Somewhere in those three summers a rider must have come south, and somebody must have told her. I can tell you the hand got lighter.

The young man turned her with it toward the drill ground. A man she did not know was shouting at the line there, in a voice that had not yet worn down to gravel.

She let the hand turn her.
