She came back over the pass a long time later. The road was the same road, though the black rock had worn smoother under more feet. Near the top she turned off it and climbed the old peak.

The summit was a bowl now. The rock of its sides had gone from black to gray, and moss had come up in the cracks on the north face. At the bottom there was water, rain and snowmelt, green at the edges, perhaps a third of the way to the rim. A hawk sat on a rock above it. There was nothing else there.

She did not go down to the water. She stood on the rim for as long as it took the hawk to lose interest in her. Then she went back down to the road.

I've stood on that rim with you since. It was full by then, and there were temples on the far shore. You wrote down the temples. You didn't know what you were writing.

In the herders' village the warm pool still smoked. A woman she did not know sold her cheese. At dawn, as she was leaving, she passed the goat field. A handful of young people stood in two rows in the frost, breathing in on the turns, going slowly through a set of forms. An old man walked up and down between them, moving a foot here and a shoulder there.

She did not stop. She did not count the forms.

The city of golden stone was where it had always been, at the head of its valley under the mountains. She came over the last rise at evening, and it glowed, as it always had. The gardens were still on the roofs. There were more of them than before. The fig trees had become orchards in great rows of pots, and the vines had crept from roof to roof over little bridges of poles, so that a child could have walked across half the city without touching the street.

She went in by the river gate. She found the street, though it had changed. The tall thin house was still there, leaning on its neighbor. A different family lived in it. There were fig trees on its roof. She stood in the street below, looking up at them, until a man came to the door and asked if she was lost.

"No," she said.

She went to a library because the door was open. Inside it was cool and dim. It smelled of cedar and old ink, so strongly that it caught in the back of her throat. The smell stayed with her long after. Shelves went up into the dark. Scrolls lay on them in their thousands, each with a little tag of colored cloth. Two old men sat at a table under a window, copying, their brushes moving together. One of them looked up, smiled at her, and went back to his work. She stayed until the light at the window had gone.

The climber was fourteen li from the walls.

She had felt him from the pass. Everyone in the city knew about him. He was a young man, twenty-two or twenty-three, the son of a family that had a house with three roof gardens. He had gone out to a cave in the hills to the east, to be away from people. They spoke of that with pride. They took him bread and oil. Scholars from the libraries went out to sit with him and came back excited. He was going to do something, everyone said. Nobody was sure what. He was going to bring something back.

His name was in every mouth in the city. I had it for three years. I don't have it now.

She found a hill between the city and the cave. She sat down on it.

She watched him for three years. She watched from the hill in summer and from a ledge out of the wind in winter. She watched him as she had once watched the sky from the front of a caravan: closely, every night, until she could have told you where everything was without looking. He was brilliant. She could feel it. His warm was large, quick, and full of light. It pulled at everything around him: the trees, the beasts in the hills, and the little hums of the shepherds who came too near his cave. He was certain. She had never felt anything so certain.

Some of his forms were slow. She knew the slow ones.

He was careful, too. He had gone out to the cave so that the city would be safe. He knew how far it was. He had worked it out with the scholars, on a scroll.

Fourteen li. It was not far enough. She could have told him.

Sometimes, in those three years, she went down into the city. She walked the streets in the evening, when the stone was gold. She bought bread. She sat on the steps of the library where the two old men worked, until one of them died and the other went on alone. Children ran between the planters on the roofs above her. Old women sat on benches under the vines, drinking tea, watching the sun go down behind the mountains.

In the third spring his warm began to climb.

She felt it go up a step, then settle. Then another step, a few days later. Each one was higher than any she had felt in a person since the summit above the pass.

On the last evening she went down into the city one more time. She stood in the street under the tall thin house and looked up at the fig trees. The man who lived there came to the door again. He was older now. He did not ask if she was lost. He nodded to her, as people nod to someone they have seen in the street for years, and went back in.

She walked out by the east gate at dusk and climbed her hill. Behind her, the lamps were coming out on the roofs, one by one. Ahead of her, fourteen li away in the dark, a warm she had watched for three years was standing up.

She sat down. She did not go to the cave.
