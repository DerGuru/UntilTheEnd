She walked back to the palace. It took her nine days.

On the way she thought about the child's weight. It had weighed almost nothing, like the boy's hand at the ford. She thought about that the whole way, without getting anywhere with it.

The lord came out into the courtyard to meet her, smiling. He had heard already. The rider who had brought him the news had been very quick.

"Well done," he said, and lifted his hand.

It came down on her shoulder, light, a hand on the back of a chair.

This time she did not turn.

She reached up and took his hand off her shoulder. She held it by the wrist and closed her fingers. The small bones of his hand went together under her grip with a sound like dry reeds crushed in a fist. He began to scream.

She put her other palm flat against his chest, over the heart, and let the warm go into him. For a moment he was a man with his mouth open. Then his clothes were burning. His skin under them was burning, and the scream stopped, because there was nothing left in him to make it. He fell back against the post. The post caught.

The guards on the wall had their spears half lowered. She looked up at them. They laid the spears down on the wall walk, one after another, and went.

She let them go.

Then she walked through the house the line had built, room by room, and laid her hand on the beams. The servants ran ahead of her. She let them run. In the steward's room the shelves of tablets stood from the floor to the ceiling, every account the line had ever kept. The fire baked them hard. When the roof came down on them, every mark was still sharp.

By morning the house on the hill was ash. Only the old wall of dressed stone was left standing, around the courtyard, with its carvings set in sideways and upside down. In the middle of the courtyard the stub of the post was still smoking, in its dark ring.

There was only ash left.

Liè stood in it, on the flagstones where the first hand had found her.

Then she walked out through the gate.
