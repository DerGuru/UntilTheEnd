The captain offered her a horse at the first ford. It was the spare, a bay gelding with one white sock, led on a rope behind the last pair. Mei looked at it. The gelding looked at the river.

"I'll walk," she said.

"It's twenty days to the hills."

"Then I'll walk twenty days."

He did not argue. He set the pace to suit her, and his men disliked it in silence. By the third day they had stopped looking back to see whether she was keeping up. By the fifth they had stopped looking back at all. She walked between the last two horses with the dust of the others on her face. At evening the horses stood with their heads low, their flanks dark with sweat. She was the only one in the column who did not sit down.

The land changed by degrees. The road left the river and climbed through stubble fields into scrub, then through scrub into stony pasture where sheep scattered ahead of the horses. On the eighth day the road turned to stone. Somebody had laid it a long time ago, flat slabs fitted edge to edge. Weeds grew in every seam now. Here and there a slab had been pried up and carried off for some farmer's wall.

Mei knew roads like this one. She had watched them laid. She had watched the people who laid them grow fat on what the roads carried, then grow careless, then go. Now their stones were going too, one wall at a time.

The men cooked at night in two messes of six. On the first night nobody fed her. On the second the youngest of them, a boy whose first beard was coming in patchy along the jaw, filled a spare bowl with barley porridge. He put it on a stone at arm's length from her and backed away, as he might have done for a strange dog.

Mei ate it because it had been put in front of her.

"She eats," somebody muttered at the other fire.

"Everybody eats."

"I heard she doesn't."

"You heard she doesn't die. Nobody said anything about eating."

The boy brought her a bowl every night after that. He never came closer than the stone. He watched her eat with the frank attention boys give to dogfights and fires.

On the sixth night he came over. He waited until the others were asleep, or near enough. Then he crossed the space between the fires in a crouch and sat down a little way off, arms around his knees.

"Is it true?" he whispered.

Mei looked at him. He had a chipped front tooth. He kept touching it with his tongue.

"The cart," he said. "They said it went right over you. They said you stood up and your ribs went click."

She held out her hand, palm up.

He stared at it. It took him a while to understand what he was being offered. When he did, he took out his knife. It was a good knife, bronze, with a bone handle bound in cord. He held it over her palm for a long breath without moving.

"Go on," she said.

He drew the edge across the meat of her palm. It was a shallow cut, a boy's cut, made by someone who had never opened anything but fish. Blood welled along the line and ran into the creases. Then it stopped. The edges drew together, slow at first, then all at once, until there was only a pink seam. By the time he had wiped his blade on his thigh even the seam was gone.

He wiped the blade again. He wiped it a third time, though it was clean.

"Put it away," the captain said from the dark.

The boy flinched. The captain had not moved from his blanket. He lay on his back with his eyes open, looking at the stars.

"He asked," Mei said.

"I know. You let him." The captain did not turn his head. "Next man who puts a blade to her without my lord's word walks home. Barefoot. Go to sleep."

The boy went. He lay with his back to her for the rest of the night, and Mei could tell from his breathing that he did not sleep. She did. She still slept, in those days.

Write down the tooth. You have ink on your thumb already, so a little more won't hurt. I will need you to remember it later, and by then I won't want to say it twice.

The hills came up out of the haze on the fifteenth day, low and brown at first. They grew greener as the road closed on them. By the nineteenth day it was climbing in earnest. On the twentieth, at noon, it brought them over a shoulder of land, and the captain reined in so that she could see.

The lord's house stood on the highest of the hills, inside a wall of old dressed stone patched with new mud brick. The dressed stone had come from somewhere else. Some of the blocks still bore carvings, set in sideways or upside down by masons who had not cared what they said. Below the wall a town was going up. Every second roof was raw thatch. Every third man in the streets was carrying something heavy.

"He's building," the captain said. It was the most he had volunteered in twenty days.

They rode up through the half-built town. People stopped work to look at her. Somebody had told them already. The talk always ran ahead of her now.

Then came the gate. Beyond it lay a courtyard paved with slabs from the same road. In the middle stood a post as thick as a man's thigh. The flagstones around its foot were dark.

It had not rained in a month.
