The storm came on the twentieth day of the sand, in the afternoon, while the caravan slept. She felt it before anyone woke: a change in the air, a dryness that stood the hair up on her arms. Then the light went yellow. The old woman sat up under her awning, sniffed once, and began shouting.

They got the donkeys down and the packs round them in a ring just before it hit. After that there was no seeing anything. The sand came sideways, stinging, thick as smoke. The men lay face down in the lee of the packs with cloths over their heads. The donkeys groaned. The noise was everything: a roar that did not rise or fall, and under it the hiss of sand on hide.

She lay with the others, a cloth over her face for the look of it. She counted hums. Eleven men. Forty donkeys. The old woman, close by, cursing steadily into her sleeve. The boy.

The boy's hum was not in the ring.

She found it a long way out, small and frightened, moving the wrong way. He must have got up to tie a donkey and lost the ring in the first gust. In the storm a man could be lost five paces from his own fire.

She got up and went out into it.

The sand stripped the skin from her face as she walked. Her face grew it back. She did not need to see. She walked straight to the small frightened hum and found him crouched against a dune with his arms over his head, sobbing. She picked him up. He fought her for a moment, then clung. She walked back with him to the ring, through the roar, and laid him down beside the old woman.

"Found him," she said, through the cloth. "Luck."

The old woman looked at her through the slit of her own headcloth. She looked at the boy. Then she pulled him in against her side and said nothing at all.

The storm blew itself out by morning. They dug the packs out of the drifts and counted the donkeys. Two were gone. The well that should have been half a night ahead was gone too, under a new dune that had not been there the year before. The old woman sat on her donkey and ran her cord through her fingers twice, frowning. Then she looked up at the white, empty sky and swore.

"We'll find the next one," she said. "Tonight. Or we won't."

They found it on the second night, with the waterskins nearly flat and the donkeys too dry to bray. The old woman found it. She did it by the stars and by a smell on the wind that she said was thorn trees. She never told anybody how near it had been.

Three weeks later the sand ended in a long slope of gravel, then scrub, then the first thin fields in a valley under mountains. On the evening of the fortieth day the caravan came over a rise. The old woman reined in her donkey and turned it sideways so that the loader could see past her.

The city lay at the head of the valley, where a river came down out of the mountains. It was built of sandstone. In that light the stone was gold, a warm deep gold she had never seen in stone before. Walls, towers, steps, and roofs were all the same color. All of it glowed as if the light came from inside. On the roofs there were gardens. She could see the green of them even from the rise: fruit trees in great pots, vines on trellises, small figures moving between them.

"Well," the old woman said. "There it is."

"You live there."

"I was born there. My daughter lives there. I come back when my hip tells me to." She shifted on the donkey. "It's telling me now."

They came in through the river gate at dusk. The streets were narrow, steep, and full of people going home. Somewhere a man was playing a pipe. The air smelled of dust, bread, and something sweet she could not name. Every so often they passed a doorway that breathed out a different smell, cool and dry, like the inside of a chest.

"Library," the old woman said, when she saw her turn her head. "Cedar shelves, so the worms leave the scrolls alone. And old ink. The best smell in the world, my father used to say. He was a fool, but he was right about that."

Her daughter's house was tall and thin. It leaned on the house next to it. The roof had a garden on it, like the others. There were fig trees in pots as big as a man could get his arms round. A vine grew over a frame of poles, with a stone bench under it still warm from the sun. The daughter cried when she saw her mother. Two grandchildren hung off the old woman's good leg. Nobody asked who the loader was. A plate was put in front of her as if she had always been there.

She sat on the roof after the others had gone down. The city went dark around her. Lamps came out on the other roofs, one by one, until the whole slope was set with small lights. The wind came down off the mountains and hummed through the trellises. Somewhere below, the pipe player had started again.

She found the Nail. She held her hand out at arm's length, flat, and laid the edge of it along the black line of the mountains.

Three fingers and a half.

The old woman came up the stairs late, slowly, one step at a time, her hand on the wall. She sat down on the bench with a groan and looked out over the lights with her.

"I go back in the spring," she said. "Same road. Same donkey, if she lasts." She did not look round. "You could come."

She did not answer at once. It was a long time since anyone had asked her where she would be in the spring. It was longer still since she had known.

"Yes," she said.

I went back across that sand with her six times. I can tell you every well on her cord. I could not tell you her name if you held my hand in this fire. Don't look like that. It's only the truth.
