It was autumn when Jìng came back over the pass. The goats were down from the high pasture, and smoke from the village fires lay along the slope in the still evening. She felt the village a long way off, every small hum she knew. Then she felt him.

She stopped on the path.

She had been gone a season. When she left, his warm had been a tree that put on a ring a year. Now it stood in him tall, straight, and very quiet, as high as she had ever felt a warm stand without shaking. It had not grown like a tree. It had gone up as if a whole forest of years had been added to it while she was away. It should have taken him twenty years. It had taken him a summer.

She noted it. She went on down to the village and said nothing.

In the morning she went to the goat field before dawn. A dozen of them were there in two rows on the frosty grass, going through the seventeen: children, young men, and young women. The headman's grandson was among them, and his own two daughters. They were slow and careful, breathing in on the turns. She watched them go through the third form, then the fourth.

He stood at the side, watching them, with his arms folded. There was gray in his beard. He did not do the forms himself.

"You've stopped," she said, beside him.

"I don't need them now." He did not take his eyes off the rows. He reached out and corrected a girl's foot as she went past. "It's quiet. Since the summer. I can hear it, Jìng. You told me to listen. I'm listening."

"What does it say?"

He smiled. He had always been a serious man. It was not a smile she had seen on him before.

"That there's more," he said. "Up there." He looked at the peak above the pass, black against the paling sky. "I want to see what it is."

"Not so hard," she said.

"I'm not pushing. That's the thing. You'd feel it if I was." He turned to look at her. His face was full of something she had never been able to give anyone. "It's going by itself. I'm only going with it."

She could feel that he was telling the truth. That was the worst of it.

Seven days later he went up the mountain.

He did it properly. He went in the night, alone, without telling the village, so that nobody would follow. He went to the top of the old peak above the pass, as far from the huts as he could get. He sat down on the black rock of the summit with his face to the east to wait for the sun.

She followed him up. She knew how far the summit was from the village. She knew how far a wave went.

It was not far enough.

He heard her coming. He did not turn. "I thought you might," he said.

"Come down."

"Jìng." He did turn then. He was sitting with his legs crossed and his hands on his knees, as the holy man had sat, a long time before, in a cave below this same peak. His face was calm. "Watch," he said. "Please. Just this once. You've never watched anything. Watch."

His warm began to rise.

It did not shake. That was what she would remember. There was no tremble in it, no green wood, nothing piled up too fast. It rose as a tide comes in, smooth, level, and all at once. Everything she had taught him was in it. Every form. Every breath in on every turn. It rose without a sound. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever felt in a person, and it was going to take the village.

She knelt in front of him and laid her palm on his chest.

He looked down at her hand. Then he looked at her face. He did not try to stop her. His warm pushed up against her hand without his help, by itself, because it was going and he was going with it.

She leaned.

It was stronger than the holy man. It was stronger than anything she had ever leaned on, except once, in a cave in the north. It came up against her hand in one smooth unbroken push. She pushed back with everything she had. For a moment the two of them knelt there on the summit with all of it crushed between his heart and her hand.

His eyes were on hers the whole time.

Then it went down.

It went down through the rock under them, straight down, into the mountain. Long ago the mountain had been fire. The summit opened like a mouth. The black rock fell away under them into a bowl, and the bowl filled with light from underneath, red, then yellow, then white. She went down into it with him. She still had her hand on his chest.

The heat took her clothes, then her skin, then the flesh under her skin. Her body put it back. The heat took it again, faster. She could not see. She could feel her hand on his chest, and then she could not feel his chest. She could not feel her hand.

This was the end, then. She did not choose it. She had not come up the mountain to die. She was simply in it, all the way down, and there was nothing left of her that the heat had not reached.

The thing came up out of the bottom of her. It did not wait for her to let go first.

The world stopped.

The fire stood in the bowl, every tongue of it. A fountain of liquid rock hung in the air above her, its drops held apart like beads on a broken string. On the slope below, in the village, in the goat field, she could feel them: a hundred small hums, every one of them standing still, every one of them looking up at the peak. The sun was half over the eastern ridge. It stayed half over the ridge.

It went on and on. It went on longer than she had ever felt it, longer than on the glacier, longer than on the doorstep. Long enough to know that the stillness reached all the way down the mountain to every person looking up at it.

A line of cold ran through the fire around her, in the shape of her own body.

Then it went on.

The rock around her went black and hard in an instant, in a thin shell. The rest of the fire went on around the shell, roaring. She lay in the shell. Her body came back to her a piece at a time. When she could move, she broke the shell with her hands and climbed out of it, up the side of the bowl, over rock that was still red in the cracks.

He was not in the bowl. Nothing was in the bowl but fire.

She sat on the rim until the sun was all the way up. Below, the fire ran down the north side of the mountain, away from the pass, in a slow red river. It went into an empty gully a little to the left of the village and stopped there.

The stopping was what broke me. Not the climbing. I've said it before. I'm not sure I understood it then either. Give me your hand a moment. It's cold. There.

When she came down to the village, the people were standing in the goat field. They had been standing there since dawn. His wife was at the front of them, with her two daughters. She looked at Jìng: the soot, the bare skin, and the hands. She did not ask. She had always been afraid of Jìng. She was not afraid now. She was only looking.

Jìng could not find anything to say to her. She went past her, through the field, and up the far side of the pass. She did not stop.

Behind her, the next morning, a dozen of them went out into the goat field before dawn in two rows. Without him, they went through the seventeen, slowly, breathing in on the turns. She felt them do it from the ridge. She did not go back.

Nobody called her Jìng after that. The one who had was gone.

The fire has burned low again. Xu Ran has stopped writing some time ago. He sits with the brush in his hand and looks at Yun across the coals.

"The lake," he says. "With the temples."

"Yes."

He nods slowly. He looks down at the page. Then he looks up again.

"What was his name?"

Yun looks into the fire for a long time.

"I don't know," she says.

He dips the brush. He writes a line. In the middle of it, he leaves a space, about the width of a name, and goes on writing on the far side of it.
