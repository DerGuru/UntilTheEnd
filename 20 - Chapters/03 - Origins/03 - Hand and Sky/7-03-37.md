It began before dawn, while the stars were still out. She felt it first in her bones, a low hum, too low to hear, that she knew as she knew her own pulse. It climbed. It went up past anything a throat could make, into her teeth, behind her eyes, into the rock of the hill under her. On the slope below, a fox came out of its earth and stood in the open with its ears flat, shaking.

The sky in the east went gray, then pale. Fourteen li away, in the hills, the warm she had watched for three years stood all the way up.

It went out from the cave in a sphere.

She could not see it. She felt it come over the hills toward her, the line between before and after, moving faster than anything that size should move. It went through her. It was like being struck by a wave of warm water from every side at once. Then it was past her, going on toward the city, and on beyond the city, out across the valley to the mountains. It went ninety li before it was done.

The walls cracked first. From the hill she saw the cracks run through them, clean and straight, like lines ruled on a page. The walls did not fall. They held, shuddering, in their new shapes.

The libraries burned.

There was no fire to start it. The scrolls had soaked up the wave, every one of them, all those centuries of ink, until they could not hold any more. Then they burned from inside. The doorways of the libraries lit up, one after another, all across the city, gold, then white. I've been told that for a moment, before the paper went, every word on every scroll could be read, sharp and black, as the page darkened around it. I didn't see that. I was too far off. I've always believed it.

The gardens died in a breath. All across the roofs, the fruit trees curled inward. Their leaves went brown and dropped all at once in a soft rain onto the stone. The vines went black on their trellises. The trellises swayed in the wind of the wave, empty, then stood still.

The wave struck the mountains beyond the city and came back.

It came back weaker. But it came back, through the city again, then out, then back, rolling between the hills and the mountains like water in a tilted dish. Each time it passed, more of what had only cracked gave way. The tall towers went first. Then the houses that leaned on each other went down together.

The children ran. Most of them did. Some didn't.

I won't describe those. Restraint isn't mercy. It's triage.

Out in the hills, the young man rose out of his cave.

She saw him go up against the dawn, small and bright, his arms out. He went up above the hills. He went up above the smoke that had begun to climb from the city, and above the mountains. He did not hurry. For a moment he hung there in the clear early sky, shining.

He did not look down.

She stayed on the hill. The sun came up. The smoke from the city went up straight at first, then leaned over in the morning wind and lay along the valley. She could hear it now, a long way off: people calling names. A dog barking and barking. A bell somewhere that someone kept ringing and would not stop.

When the smoke thinned toward noon, she could see down into the nearest streets. A library stood at the edge of them, near the east gate, the one where the two old men had copied. Its roof was gone. Its walls stood, black inside. In the middle of what had been its floor, among the heaps of ash, there was a child.

She was five, perhaps six. She stood in the ash in a dress that had been white, holding a scroll. The scroll was burned almost to the rod. Only a little of it was left, curled and black at the edges. The child held it in both hands against her chest and cried.

She was not crying because she could read it. She was too young to read. She was crying because something very large had been taken out of the world, and some part of her knew it.

She could have stopped it. She knew that, standing on the hill. She had known it every day for three years. A hand on his chest in the cave, fourteen li out. A grove burned. A shepherd's hut. A man looking at her face the whole time.

She would have been part of it, then.

She had watched instead. It was easier. It was always easier.

She stayed on the hill until the smoke was gone.
