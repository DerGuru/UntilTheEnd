After the old woman died, Jìng did not cross the sand again. She stayed in the mountains. She had a hut in the pass and a man in the goat field who did the seventeen every dawn. Around them, further than she had ever cared to feel before, she began to feel for climbers.

There were more of them than she would have guessed. There always had been, perhaps. She had never listened.

The first after the holy man was a woman in a salt marsh to the east, three weeks' walk away. Jìng felt her on a still night as a thin tremble at the edge of everything, and she went. She got there in time. She knelt in the mud by the brine pans. She laid her hand on the woman's breastbone and leaned. When the warm went sideways, it went into the pans. They boiled dry in a breath, and the salt workers' huts beside them went up like straw.

The next was a boy on a river island, in a country of vines. When his warm went sideways, it took the bridge. The ferry that went out the next morning in its place was too full and turned over in the current.

The next was an old man in a quarry. That one went into the rock, and the rock held it. Nobody died. She sat in the quarry afterward for a whole day, waiting for something else to happen. Nothing did.

She began to keep count. She did not write it down. She remembered a boy with a clay tablet who had followed a man with a farmer's shoulders across a courtyard, a long time before, writing down what every town had cost. She had never thought about that boy. She thought about him now.

Then there was the town in the river bend.

She felt the climber there too late. She ran. She came in through the town gate at noon on the third day, with the whole sky over the town gone strange and too clear. He was in the market square, a young man standing on the lip of the well with his arms spread, and the people were standing round him in a ring, staring up. She went through them. She put her hand on his chest.

If she had let him go up, the wave would have gone out over the whole valley. Most of it was fields. She knew that. She knew it as she leaned.

The warm went sideways. It had nowhere to go but the town.

She did not stay to count that one. She walked out through the gap where the gate had been, past the ring of people, who were no longer standing, and turned north.

The path was slippery. It always was.

He was sitting on his doorstep with the kettle on a small fire beside him, as if he had not moved since she last came up the path. He lifted two fingers. She lifted hers. She did not stop at the edge of the ring this time. She came all the way up to the doorstep and sat down on it beside him, in the wet.

"Liè," he said.

He poured her tea. She held it and did not drink it. It was raining a little, fine rain that did not fall so much as hang in the air and settle on everything. Below them the pines went down into the mist.

"I've been stopping them," she said.

"I know. I feel it, sometimes. Like a door slamming somewhere down the valley." He drank. "How many?"

She told him. He nodded slowly.

"And now you want to stop," he said.

"I want you to do it."

He did not answer for a long time. Rain gathered on the rim of his cup and ran down.

"You're the only one who could," she said.

"I don't know that I could."

"You're the only one who could try."

He set the cup down on the doorstep, carefully, beside the crack that ran through it. He looked at her. He had a long face, and it was very still.

"All right," he said. "I hope you'll succeed."

He did not say anything else. He did not make her wait. He got up, and she knelt on the doorstep where she had been sitting, facing out over the pines. She pulled her hair forward over one shoulder to bare her neck. She heard him step up behind her onto the wet stone. She felt his warm rise, all of it, more of it than she had felt on the first day of the fight. He put it all into the edge of one hand.

It was very clean. It was the cleanest thing anyone had ever tried on her.

She let go of everything. She did not push back. The warm in her lay down. The rain hung in front of her face in the gray air. She watched it. It was very quiet. She thought: yes. She thought it without fear. She heard the small sound of his breath going in before the stroke.

Then the thing came up out of the bottom of her.

She had known it would. She had hoped, this time, that it would not. It came up screaming through all of her, as it had in the snow and the ice. Every part of her that had knelt down stood up.

The world stopped.

The rain stood in the air, every drop of it. The mist over the pines stood still in long gray folds. Behind her, she could feel his hand, a finger's width from her neck, held there. She could feel his breath held in him. She could feel his eyes on the back of her head. They were moving. Everything on the whole mountain had stopped except his eyes and hers.

It went on a long time. Longer than on the glacier. Long enough for her to count the drops on one strand of her hair.

A line ran through the stone under his foot, along the old crack in the doorstep.

Then it went on.

The doorstep split. His foot went out from under him on the wet stone, and his hand went past her ear by the width of a finger, into the rock of the cave mouth, which broke. The crack went up the cliff above them, fast, with a sound like ice on a lake. The cave roof came down. It came down behind them all at once, a long roar of rock and water. The dust of it rolled out over the doorstep, over the two of them, and down the slope into the pines.

When the dust cleared, the cave mouth was gone. There was a slope of broken rock where it had been, with water already finding its way out between the stones.

He was sitting on the ground beside the doorstep with his leg out in front of him, looking at it.

"I slipped," he said.

She said nothing. She was still kneeling. She could not yet make herself get up.

"It's slippery," he said. "It always is." He looked at his hand. It was bleeding where it had gone into the rock. The bleeding stopped as he watched it, and the skin closed. He looked up at the heap of stone that had been his cave. "I liked that cave."

After a while she sat down properly. He picked up his cup. It had stayed on the doorstep through all of it, on the half that had not moved, full of rain. He drank it.

They sat there for the rest of the day. They did not talk about what had happened. We never have, he and I, not once since. He talked about the weather, the stream, and where he might dig. She listened.

Toward evening he looked up at the sky, which hung low, gray, and heavy over the peaks.

"Clear by tonight," he said.

It rained all night. It was the first time she had known him to be wrong about the weather. Neither of them said anything about that either.
