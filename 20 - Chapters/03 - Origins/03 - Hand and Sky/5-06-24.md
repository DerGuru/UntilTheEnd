She had walked this path before. It climbed out of a pine valley into the mist, crossed the same stream twice, and went on up over wet black rock. Moss grew thick on the north side of every stone. Water ran everywhere, under the moss, over the rock, and down the back of her neck. Somewhere above, a long way off, there was a warm that felt less like a man than like a place.

The path was slippery. It always was.

The cliff over the cave had come down at some point. The slabs that had hung from it lay in a long tumbled heap below the cave mouth, a little to the left, with young birches already growing up between them. The doorstep was where it had been, mended into one piece with a crack running through it. On it sat the old man, with a kettle on a small fire beside him.

He saw her before she reached the edge of the old ring. The ground there was still pale, though grass had come back into it in tufts. He lifted his hand with two fingers raised, the first and the second.

She knew what it meant now. She could not have said how she knew. She stopped where she was. She did not lift her hand.

"Liè," he said.

"Nobody calls me that now."

"What do they call you?"

"Nothing."

He considered that, then nodded at the place across the fire from him. "Liè, then. Sit down. You're dripping on my ring."

She sat. He poured from the kettle into a cup and held it out. It was a new kettle, rounder than the old one, with a lid that did not quite fit it.

"I made another," he said. "The lid's the old one."

The tea was the rough stuff. She drank it. She had come a long way to ask him something, and now that she was here she found she was in no hurry. Rain moved over the pines below them in slow gray curtains. He watched it.

"Clear by tonight," he said.

After a while he said, "One came up the path in the spring. Young. He wanted me to show him how to climb." He drank. "I sent him home. He went up somewhere else instead. I felt it in the summer, a long way east."

He did not say what he had felt. She did not ask.

She waited until the cup was empty. Then she set it down on the doorstep between them.

"How do you die?" she said.

He did not look surprised. He went on looking at the rain for a while, as if the question had come up with the weather and would go when it went.

"I don't know," he said. "I've looked."

"Where?"

"Fire, first. Everyone tries fire first." He turned his cup in his hands. "Then water. I lay at the bottom of a lake in the east for a while. Forty years, about. The fish got used to me." He drank. "I came up because I was bored. That's as near as I got."

"Falling."

"You know about falling."

She did. She said nothing.

"Ice," she said after a while.

He thought about it. "I haven't tried ice," he said. "There's a glacier two days north of here. It moves, slowly. It grinds the mountain under it into flour." He looked at her over his cup. "It's very slow."

"I have time."

He made a sound that was nearly a laugh. Then he was quiet for a long time, and so was she. The rain thinned. A patch of sunlight came and went on the far slope.

When the rain had stopped, he got up. He went into his cave and came out with his old blanket over his shoulders. The kettle hung from his belt on a cord.

"I'll show you," he said. "It's cold up there. Not that it matters to either of us."

They went north. The glacier lay in a high valley between two peaks. It was gray at the edges with the stone it carried and blue in the cracks. Below its snout, where the ice ended in a broken wall, meltwater had gathered into a lake the color of milk. A tongue of old ice held the lake in. Far below that, down the valley, smoke rose from a village. She could see the green of its barley terraces against the stone. She could feel the hum of the people, small and many, like bees in a wall.

She noted the village as she noted everything. Then she turned and walked up onto the ice.

The crevasses ran across the glacier in long blue lines. She chose one near the middle, where the ice was deepest. At the top it was wide enough for a man to fall into. Below, it narrowed to a dark seam she could not see the bottom of. Cold came up out of it like breath from a cellar.

She climbed down into it. The walls were smooth, wet, and very blue. She went down until the crack was too narrow to go further. Then she wedged herself into it on her back, with the ice close on both sides. Far above her, the gap of sky was white.

The old man's face appeared at the edge of it. He sat down on the lip of the crevasse with the blanket around him and his legs hanging over.

"I'll wait," he said. "Somebody should."

She closed her eyes.
