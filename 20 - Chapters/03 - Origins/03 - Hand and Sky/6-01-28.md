After the charcoal valley she went south, out of the woods, until the trees gave out. She wanted country with nothing in it that would burn. At the edge of the steppe there were trading towns that sent caravans out across the sand.

Her caravan left from one of them in the cool of an autumn evening, with forty donkeys, eleven men, a boy, and an old woman. She had hired on to load and unload. She could lift a full pack as easily as an empty one. She had learned not to. She lifted them like the other loaders did, with a grunt, a heave, and a hand at the small of her back afterward. Nobody looked at her twice.

The old woman rode at the front on a gray donkey with a white nose. She was small and dark, with a bad hip that made her sit sideways. She slept through the heat of the day under an awning, snoring, and woke at sunset as other people wake at dawn. She was the reason the caravan went where it meant to go. On the steppe that mattered less. Further on, it mattered a great deal.

For the first weeks she paid the loader no attention. Then, one night on the steppe, she found her awake by the dead fire, looking up.

"Can't sleep?" the old woman said.

"I don't, much."

"No more do I." She lowered herself onto a saddle with a hiss, her bad leg out in front of her. "What are you looking at?"

"Nothing."

"Nobody looks at nothing that hard." The old woman followed her gaze. "That's the Bow. Those three, and the bright one at the end. In winter it's over there. In summer it's down behind the hills, and you have to do without it."

She showed her four more that night. The Sisters, a little smudge of six or seven, depending on your eyes. The Hunter. The Ladle. A dim star low in the north that she called the Nail.

"Everything else goes round," the old woman said. "The Nail stays put. The whole sky hangs on it." She squinted at it. "More or less."

The next night she showed her how to measure. She held her arm straight out at the sky with her fingers together, the hand flat, and laid the edge of her hand along the dark line of the hills.

"Four fingers," she said. "The Nail's four fingers up, here. When it's three, you've gone too far south. When it's five, you've gone north. Go on. Hold yours up."

She held up her hand. The old woman leaned over and took her wrist. Her fingers were dry and light. She turned the hand until the palm faced the stars. She straightened the elbow with a little push.

"Not like that. You're not pointing at it. You're measuring it." She let go. "There. How many?"

She looked along her own arm at the sky. She counted. The Nail sat just above the edge of her fourth finger.

"Four," she said.

"Good girl."

Nobody had called her that in a very long time. The last person who had might have been her own mother. She let it go by.

You know the star the sky turns on. Look up, past the edge of the branches. No, further. There. It wasn't that one then. The Nail was dimmer, a little off to the side. The sky has moved since. Everyone tells me it moves slowly. I've never found it slow.

After that the old woman taught her every night. She taught her the risings: which star came up over the edge of the world at which season, and where. She taught her to read the wind on the grass, then on the sand, in ripples you could feel with your bare feet in the dark. She taught her to lay a straight line across the night by keeping one star over the left ear of a donkey and walking until it slid. She had a cord of plaited goat hair with knots tied in it at uneven intervals. Each knot was a well, or a turn, or a place to rest. She ran it through her fingers as she rode.

She learned fast. She remembered everything she was told. The old woman found that out on the second night and was delighted with it.

She also had no sense of direction at all. She found that out on the third night and was more delighted still.

"You've got eyes like a hawk," the old woman said, "and you'd walk into the sea looking for the mountains. How have you lived this long?"

"Badly."

The old woman laughed until her hip hurt.

On the steppe they traveled by day, as everyone did. When the grass gave out and the sand began, they turned it round. They slept under awnings in the white heat, with the donkeys lying in the thin shade of the packs. They walked all night by the stars. The sand was cool underfoot after dark. It squeaked. The donkeys' bells sounded thin and very far away, though they were only a few paces off.

On the twelfth night of the sand the old woman fell asleep on her donkey. She had done it before. Usually the boy woke her. This time he did not, because he was asleep on his own donkey too.

The loader walked at the front. She looked up. She found the Nail, the Bow, and the Sisters low in the east where they ought to be. She laid one star over the left ear of the old woman's donkey. She held her hand out at arm's length, flat, and measured.

Four fingers.

She walked on, and the caravan walked behind her. Nobody knew. She kept the star over the donkey's ear until the sky went gray. When the old woman woke at dawn, blinking, the first well on her cord was in sight across the sand, a low ring of stones with a thorn tree beside it.

The old woman looked at the well. She looked at the loader walking beside her donkey. She ran her cord through her fingers until she came to the knot.

"Hm," she said. Then she went back to sleep.
