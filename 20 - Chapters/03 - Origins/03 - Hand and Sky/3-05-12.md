The war began in the son's time. It was not the kind of war that begins on a particular day. A town in the west stopped paying, then another. Then a dozen of them stopped together, under a man who had studied the lord's tablets and understood them. The son sent Liè west. She burned two of the towns. After that the rest learned to empty before she came and fill again behind her, as the tide fills a footprint.

The war went on. It went on for longer than she paid attention to it.

When the hills called her back, it was spring. The courtyard was as it had always been: the flagstones, the post, and the dark ring at its foot. The steward was waiting by the post. He was old now. His beard had gone white, his back had bent, and he still carried the board of clay under his arm, as though he would not know where to put his arm without it. Beside him stood a boy of nine or ten in a cloak that had been cut for a man.

The steward bent and said something in the boy's ear. The boy climbed onto the stone base of the post. Standing on it, he was nearly as tall as Liè's shoulder.

He lifted his hand. It did not reach. The steward took the boy's wrist, gently, and set the small hand where it was supposed to go.

It was hot and a little damp. It weighed almost nothing.

"The ford," the steward murmured.

"The ford," the boy said.

He was nine, I think. I'm guessing from the hand. I have nothing else of him. Out here, with the cold coming up out of the ground into my legs, I can still feel how damp it was.

The ford lay twelve days to the west, where the plains river spread out wide and shallow over gravel. Two armies had met there. Neither would give up the crossing, and neither could take it, so for three days they had fought in the water. By the time Liè came over the last rise, the river below the ford ran red as far as she could see. Bodies had drifted down onto a gravel bar and stuck there. There were more crows on the bar than she could count.

She went down through the lord's camp without stopping and walked into the river.

The water came to her knees, then to her thighs. The fighting was in the middle of the ford, where the current was slowest. It was a churned mass of men up to their waists in the river, shoving with shields, stabbing low, holding each other under. Nobody there could tell any longer which way he was facing. The red hands on her own side's shields were the only thing that told one army from the other, and half of those shields had floated away.

They saw her coming. Some of them knew what she was. A man with a red hand on his shield shouted her name and tried to get out of her way. A man without one shouted it and came at her.

His spear went in under her breastbone. Another went into her back from somewhere behind. A third man got his arms around her waist and tried to drag her under. She let him, because it was easier to stand still. More came. In the space of a few breaths she stood at the middle of a knot of men, every one of them trying to kill her, with three shafts standing out of her body and the water at her chest.

She had done the turn a thousand times by then, in a pear orchard, on a hillside, and at the gates of towns. She had always done it toward something. She had never done it all the way round.

She did it now.

The air went hard. For an instant the surface of the river around her went flat as beaten bronze, all the way out to twenty-five paces in every direction. Then the water jumped. Every man inside that circle stopped at once. Their eardrums went first, then the small vessels of their faces. Blood ran out of their noses, their ears, and the corners of their eyes. The nearest of them were cooked inside their leather. The heat came off her in a single breath, and the river around her rose up in steam with a long hiss.

When the steam thinned, there was nobody standing within twenty-five paces of her. The man who had held her waist floated face down against her hip.

She pulled the three spears out of herself and let them drift away. Then she walked on, toward the far bank.

At every twenty paces she turned again. Each turn emptied a circle. She did not look at the shields of the men inside it, because it would have slowed her. Red hands went down with the rest. Behind her, on the near bank, the lord's captains were screaming at their own men to pull back out of the water. Most of them did.

Ahead of her the other army had stopped fighting. They stood in the shallows by the far bank and watched her come. She turned once more, at forty paces from them. The river between went flat, jumped, and steamed.

The first man on the far bank threw his spear into the water before she reached him. Then the man beside him did the same. Then a hundred did it together, and the sound of it was like a hard rain.

Nobody on that bank was holding a weapon when she came up out of the river.
