She heard the flails before she saw the valley. It was the second autumn of the walking, or the third, and she was on a ridge above country she did not know. The sound came up to her from below: many flails beating together, out of step, then in step for a while, then out again. She had not heard it in a long time.

Two villages sat in the valley, one on each side of a stream, each with its own threshing floor. Between them lay a field of barley stubble, pale in the low sun. The chaff from both floors hung over the valley and caught the light.

She sat down on a rock to listen. It was the first time in the walking that she had sat down for anything.

There were others after this one. The new stick has caught; watch the bark curl back from it. This is the first I remember, which by now is not quite the same as the first.

The flails on the near floor stopped. A little later the far ones stopped too. She heard voices, then more voices. Men came out of both villages and walked down toward the stubble, not hurrying, in the loose bunches of people who have known one another all their lives. They had brought their flails. Some had brought sickles. Near the stream, at the edge of the barley, stood a stone about as high as a man's hip. Both bunches were walking toward it.

She counted them out of habit. Thirty from the near village, a few more from the far one. Farmers, every one of them. Not one of them had any warm in him.

She had walked past wars that summer. Once she had passed within sight of two armies meeting at a ford and had not turned her head. She stood up and went down the hill toward a boundary stone.

You've stopped writing. If you want to know why, I can only give you the answer I gave the old man on his doorstep: I don't know. He would have told you it was a start.

The stone had been moved. She could see that before anyone said it. The earth at its foot was fresh. A pace to the north there was a hollow in the stubble the size of its base, dark and damp, with worms still in it.

The men stood on either side of it and told each other so. A gray-bearded man from the far village already had a split lip and was talking through it. A young man from the near village swung his flail in slow circles over his head, as boys do when they want to be seen holding one. On a cart at the edge of the field, a woman was shouting at her son to come home, now, this instant. Nobody looked at the cart.

She walked out of the stubble into the space between them. They saw a woman in dust-colored clothes. A few of them glanced at her. Nobody stopped talking.

"Stop," she said.

The gray-bearded man told her to get out of the way. He did not look at her while he said it.

For as long as she could remember, trouble had ended when it saw her. Armies had seen her on the far side of a valley and started for home. This trouble had never heard of her. It went on over her head, as it went on over the stone.

The young man brought his flail down. He meant it for the gray-bearded man's shoulder. It caught the side of his head instead. The old man sat down in the stubble with a hand over his ear. Both sides moved at once.

She knew two things. She could push, and she could burn. She had never done either one softly. Nobody had ever pointed her at anything that needed it.

She set her feet. She began the turn she had first found in a pear orchard, and she began it as slowly as she could.

The air turned with her. It went round the stone, then round the men, then round the whole corner of the field. Chaff came up off the stubble. Dust followed it, then the chaff from both threshing floors, gold at first, then brown. She let a little of the warm into the turn, less than she had ever let into anything. The heat climbed. The air climbed after it and carried the chaff up into a column.

The sun went brown, then went out. It was the middle of the afternoon, and in the valley it was dark.

Men coughed. Men shouted for each other by name. She could not see them, but she could feel their hums moving in the dark, away from one another. It was what she had wanted. She held the turn. She did not know how to make it smaller. Nobody had ever needed her to stop halfway.

The column lifted the first of them off his feet. She felt him go up. Then another went. Then she felt a dozen of them rising, then thirty, all of them as light as chaff.

She let go.

The wind did not stop when she did. It ran on for a while by itself, then came apart over the valley. Everything it had been holding came down. The chaff came down slowly. The rest came down all over the countryside: in the stubble, in the stream, on the roofs of both villages, and in fields beyond the ridge where nobody had been threshing at all.

They were farmers. None of them had ever learned how to land.

The dust took a long time to settle. When it had, the stone was still standing where the last man had put it. The gray-bearded man sat beside it with his hand over his ear. He had been sitting down. The wind had gone over him.

The others lay where they had come down. Most of them were moving. Some sat up and looked about them as if they had woken in the wrong bed. Some lay still. One lay across the stream with the water running over his back. She did not go to look.

The woman from the cart came down into the field, calling the same name over and over. She found her son in the stubble with one arm under him at a bad angle. He was crying. She knelt beside him and hit him twice on the shoulder that was not broken. Then she held his head against her.

Behind the cart a small girl stood with both hands on the wheel. She had watched all of it from there. Now she was telling anyone who would listen, which was nobody yet, what she had seen. The woman had stood in the middle of the field, she said. The dark had come out of her mouth and swallowed the men. Then it had spat some of them back.

You know what that became. Your sleeve's torn again, by the way. You told me that story yourself once, while I was mending mine.

Nobody stopped her when she walked out of the valley. People moved out of her path from a long way off. A man with a hoe stepped down into a ditch to let her by and stood there up to his shins in water until she had passed.

She climbed back up to the ridge where she had sat. The rock was still warm from the sun. Below her, on both threshing floors, nobody had picked up a flail.

For a long time after that, she stayed up on the hills.
