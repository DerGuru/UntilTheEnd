The lord marched the survivors north. There were not many: women, old men, and children who could walk. The ones who could not walk were carried by the ones who could, until the carrying grew too heavy. Then they were left by the road. The lord wanted hands. His town in the hills had more walls to build than men to build them.

Mei walked beside the column. On the first day she heard the word.

A woman said it to a child as Mei came near. She said it low and quick, then pulled the child behind her skirts. An old man said it into his beard. By the second day the whole column was saying it. The word ran ahead of her along the line, passed from mouth to mouth in a hiss. The line bent away from her before she reached it, as water bends around a stone.

The soldiers heard it too. There was a trader among the prisoners, a thin man with a squint who had bought and sold along the river for thirty years. He spoke the hill tongue well enough to cheat in it. On the third night one of the soldiers dragged him over to the fire.

"The word," the soldier said. "The one they keep saying. What is it?"

The trader looked across the fire at Mei before he answered.

"Liè," he said.

"I know what it sounds like. What does it mean?"

The trader thought about it. "A field fire," he said. "In high summer, in the barley, with the wind behind it, past any beating out. That's liè. You stand at the edge of your field and watch it, because there's nothing else left to do."

The soldier laughed. "Liè," he said, trying it. "Liè."

"Not so loud," the trader said. Then, remembering whose prisoner he was, he added, "Sir."

By the time they reached the hills every man in the army was saying it. They said it half as a joke, as men will joke about a storm that drowned someone they knew. They said it when she passed and when she didn't. The drill master said it once, tasted it, and spat.

Nobody brought her a bowl at night now. Mei did not notice for three days. On the fourth she noticed. She could not remember why anyone ever had.

The lord heard the word before they reached the hills. He found it useful. At the end of summer he sent riders south to the next town in the plains, the one upriver of the ash. They carried his message. The barley was due by the full moon, and if it did not come, he would send Liè.

The town paid before the full moon. It paid twice what it owed and sent along the headman's son with the barley, as a hostage nobody had asked for.

The lord said it to her himself on the day the barley came in. She was standing by the post in the courtyard. He came out with the boy at his heel, put his hand on her shoulder, and said it.

"Liè."

Mei turned her head.

It was a word somebody had handed her, so she held it.

You're trying it now, under your breath. I can see your lips move. Don't. It was never meant for a mouth that wasn't afraid of it.

Liè stood by the post until the shadow had crossed three flagstones. Nobody had told her to stand anywhere else.
