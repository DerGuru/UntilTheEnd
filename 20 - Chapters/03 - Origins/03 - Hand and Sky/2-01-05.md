The flies came early that summer. They found the courtyard before the lord did. By the time he came out with the boy and his clay, a dozen of them were walking up and down the post in the middle of the flagstones.

Mei had been standing by it since dawn. Nobody had told her to stand there. It was where the hand had left her the evening before, and she had seen no reason to be anywhere else.

The lord held out his hand without looking, and the boy put a tablet into it. It was an old one, dry and pale, its marks gone soft at the edges.

"A town in the plains," the lord said. "Four days south of the hills, where the river bends. Two summers ago they agreed to send me a fifth of their barley. Here's the mark. Here's last summer's." He ran his thumb along the rows. "This summer they sent it east, to a man who has started calling himself a king. I'd like them to understand that was a mistake."

He went on. He explained the river, the barley, the man in the east, and what the town had promised in front of witnesses. He explained it carefully, in order. A fly walked up the post to the height of a man's head and stopped there to clean its face.

"Do you follow?" the lord asked.

"Yes," Mei said.

The fly finished with its face and started on its wings.

The lord looked at her for a moment. Then he handed the tablet back to the boy. "It doesn't matter," he said. "I follow. That's enough for both of us."

He put his hand on her shoulder. "South."

They went down out of the hills with three hundred men. The captain rode at the head with the lord. The drill master marched with the spears and swore at them by the hour, steady as rain. Mei walked where she was put, near the front of the column, in the dust of the riders.

The boy with the chipped tooth marched two ranks behind her. He had a spear of his own now and a shield of stretched hide with a red hand painted on it, the lord's mark. On the first night he brought her a bowl of porridge without being asked, set it on a stone, and went back to his own fire. He grinned at her as he went. The tooth showed.

The hills gave out on the second day. After that there was only the plain. It ran flat to the edge of the sky in every direction, barley country, gold going brown in the heat. The road was a pale line through it. Far ahead a single tree stood in the middle of a field, and it took them half a day to reach it.

On the third day they saw the town's smoke. On the fourth morning they saw the town.

It sat in the bend of a slow brown river, ringed by a ditch and a mud-brick wall twice the height of a man. The gate was oak faced with copper gone green. Above the wall the thatched roofs crowded together, with smoke going up from a hundred hearths. Somebody inside had seen them coming. A horn was blowing, flat and long, over and over.

The gate opened. Men came out of it at a run and formed a line across the road in front of the ditch. There were two hundred of them, perhaps. They carried wicker shields, spears, and a few bronze axes. They had come out to fight in the open rather than wait behind their wall, which told Mei they had heard of the lord and not of her.

The drill master set her at the point of the line, where he would have put his best man. "Doors," he told her, as though she might have forgotten.

The lord rode up behind the line. He leaned down from the saddle and put his hand on her shoulder. He did not point. There was only one thing in front of them to point at.

The horn on the wall was still blowing when the lines closed.

The man in front of her was about the captain's age. He had shaved that morning. There was a nick under his jaw where the razor had caught, still pink. He came at her over the rim of his shield with his spear held high and overhand, which the drill master would have cuffed him for.

She went low. Her point went in under his chin, behind the edge of the wicker, and up. She felt it pass through the soft floor of his mouth. She felt it grate on bone and go on until it stopped inside his skull. His teeth came down on the shaft. For a breath they stood like that, joined by the spear, his eyes on hers.

Blood ran down the shaft. It reached her hands, then her wrists. It was warm. It was the warmest thing she had touched in a long time.

She waited.

She had killed one man before, long ago, in front of the people who had thrown her out. She remembered that it had been loud inside her. She stood with this man's weight on her spear and waited for the loud thing.

Nothing came.

I have lost ten thousand faces since. I still have that nick, pink under his jaw. It comes back on nights like this one, with the wind coming round the rock behind us, and I let it come.

She drew the spear out. It came free with a sound like a boot pulled out of mud, and the man folded down onto his shield. Mei stepped over him. She looked for the next door.
