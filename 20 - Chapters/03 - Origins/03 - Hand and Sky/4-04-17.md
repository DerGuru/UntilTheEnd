It was raining on the last day. He had said it would.

She came at him in the gray light before morning, as she had every day. She did not settle into a stance. She had tried every stance she knew on him, and the turn from every distance. All of it had closed over behind her like water. She stood at the foot of his doorstep in the rain and understood that the fastest way left was all of it at once.

She let go.

She let the warm go, every scrap of it that was banked in her. It was the heat she had carried up the mountain and the heat she had carried since she was a child in a cave. It went out of her into the ring around her and kept going.

The rain stopped where it fell. It turned to steam a man's height above the ground. The ring under it went dry, then white. The doorstep cracked across. The wet rock of the cliff hissed, went silent, and began to glow dull red, then orange.

The old man sat on his doorstep in the middle of it. He stayed where he was, with his hands on his knees. His robe caught and burned off him. His skin blistered, split, and healed, then blistered again. He watched her. He said nothing.

She burned until the light changed. Each time she thought there could be no more in her, there was more, from somewhere lower down. She burned past the place where she had ever stopped before. She burned past the place where she had thought the bottom was.

Then there was none.

She was lying on her back in a ring of white ash. She did not remember lying down. Above her the mist was coming back over the edge of the ring, slowly, and the first drops of rain were reaching the ground again.

She called the warm. It came, a little, as a coal answers breath, then sank back down. She lifted her arm. It lifted. She tried to close her hand into a fist, and it would only close halfway.

She lay there and let the rain land on her face. It was cold. She noticed that it was cold.

The old man got up off his doorstep. He was naked and pink as a newborn. He moved like a man three times his age, which was saying something. He came down into the ring, stepping carefully through the hot ash, and sat down beside her in it.

For a while he said nothing. The rain came down on both of them.

"Are you done?" he asked.

There was no judgement in it. He asked it as he had told her about the weather every morning, because it was a thing worth knowing.

She looked for the answer. It was right there. It had been there for a long time, under everything, and nobody had asked.

"Yes," she said.

She had been done for a long time. She had not known it until he asked.
