The lord kept the eastern king's city. He put his own men on its walls and his own keepers in its granaries. He had every soul inside it counted. The boy with the clay did the counting. He was not much of a boy anymore. His voice had broken over the summer, and it broke again now and then in the middle of a number, which made the soldiers laugh.

They brought the townspeople out into the market square a street at a time. Smiths went to one side, weavers to another, and the young men who could carry a spear to a third. The rest went to whichever side the lord's steward pointed at.

Liè stood at the edge of the square because she had been put there. She was not watching the sorting. She was looking at nothing in particular when she felt it.

It was very small. It came from somewhere behind her, past the square, in the lane where the bakeries were. A thread of the warm, thin as a hair, running hot.

She turned her head.

The bakeries had kept their fires going through the whole siege, on what the townspeople had been able to scrape together. One was still lit. In front of it a boy of perhaps twelve was pulling loaves out of the oven mouth with his bare hands. He laid each one on the board beside him, reached back in for the next, and did not shake his fingers afterward. There was soot ground into the creases of his knuckles. There were no blisters.

He felt her looking. He looked back. Then he went on taking out bread, a little faster.

Liè found the lord at the granary gate with his steward. She waited until he had finished speaking.

"There's a boy at the bakeries," she said. "He has fire in his hands."

The lord turned. "Like yours?"

"No. A hair of it."

He thought about that. "Are there others?"

She had not looked. She looked now. She let her attention go out across the city, past the press of hums in the square, into the lanes, the courtyards, and the sickrooms. There were others. A woman at the dye vats by the river had something cool and slow in her, like water in a cistern. An old man mending a wall in the potters' quarter had something heavy that settled when he touched the stone. There were five in all, in a city of thousands.

"Yes," she said.

"Find them," said the lord.

She found them before dark. She walked the soldiers to each door and stood in the street while they went in.

I told him about that boy as I would have told him it looked like rain. Write that down exactly as I said it. The wind has swung round, and the smoke is in your eyes now. Wipe them if you need to, then write it down.

The lord took the five north with him when the army went home. He gave them to the drill master. The drill master looked them over, the baker's boy, the dyer, the old wall-mender, and two girls who had not stopped crying since the city fell. He spat.

"What am I meant to do with these?"

"Whatever you did with her," the lord said.

The drill master looked at Liè for a long time. "Spare me," he said, and put spears in their hands.

They drilled in the line beside her that winter. They were frightened of her. They stood as far from her as the line allowed and watched her out of the sides of their eyes.

The baker's boy watched her more than the others did. On the third night she felt his warm behind her, at the top of the scree, where he thought the dark hid him. She did not turn round. He came back the next night, and the night after that. She let him stay. She showed him nothing. She worked through the figures as she always did, and he lay on his belly at the top of the scree, watching.

In early spring the drill master called the low stance, and the line went down into it, grumbling. The baker's boy went down into it with his back heel lifted the width of a finger.

The drill master stopped in front of him. He looked at the heel. He looked at the boy's forearms, where the cords stood out.

"Who taught you that?"

"Nobody," the boy said.

The drill master turned his head and looked down the line at Liè. Liè was looking at the posts.

That night the boy did not lie on the scree. He came down to the bottom of it, where she had worked the night before, and tried the turn. He tried it until his arms shook. On the last try, as his breath went out, the dust in front of his spear point lifted. A dry thistle there began to smoke.

From the top of the slope Liè felt his warm flare, the whole hair-thin thread of it at once. Then it went quiet again. She stayed where she was.

In the morning there was a black ring in the dust where the thistle had been. The drill master found it, looked at it for a while, and went to find the boy.

"Don't burn my posts," he said.
