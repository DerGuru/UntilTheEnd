The rain went on into the evening. Neither of them got up. The ash under her cooled. The mist came all the way back and closed over the ring.

At some point he went into his cave. He came back with an old blanket around his shoulders, carrying something in both hands. It was the cup. It had stood on the doorstep since the first morning. The doorstep had cracked into three pieces, but the cup had come through the fire whole, as fired clay does. It was full to the brim with rain and ash.

He held it out to her.

"It's gone cold," he said.

She sat up. It took her two tries. She took the cup in both hands. She drank it down: rain, ash, and whatever was left of the rough tea.

It was terrible. She drank all of it.

He took the cup back and set it beside him. They sat in the ring and watched the mist move.

When it was dark he helped her up the slope and into the mouth of the cave. Water ran down the walls inside, and the floor was wet. He spread a second blanket on a shelf of rock that was only a little less wet than the floor and sat her on it. Then he knelt by the old hearth with a handful of dry tinder he had kept somewhere, wrapped in a skin, and looked at her.

"You could light this faster than I can," he said.

She reached for the warm. It came up as far as her wrists and stopped there, a coal under ash. Nothing reached her hands.

He nodded, as if she had answered him, and took out a flint.

It took him a long time. He struck, blew, and struck again, an old man on his knees in a wet cave, muttering at the tinder. When it caught at last, he fed it twigs one at a time, then sticks, and sat back on his heels.

"There," he said. "The slow way. It still works."

She sat by his fire. She could not remember the last time she had sat by a fire someone else had made. The heat reached her face, and she let it.

In the morning he went down the slope to look for his kettle. He was gone a long time. When he came back, he was carrying the lid.

"The rest of it is in a great many places," he said. He set the lid on the shelf beside her, carefully, as though it were the whole kettle. "I'll make another. I made that one."

He boiled water in a cracked pot instead and put the last of his rough leaves in it. He gave her a cup of it, and a lump of something cooked in the ashes that turned out to be a root. She ate it because it had been put in front of her. It tasted of smoke. She noticed that it did.

She stayed three days. She had not decided to. On the first she could not stand for long. On the second she could, but the warm still stopped at her wrists. On the third her fist closed most of the way.

He did not ask her to stay. He did not ask her to go.

He worked. He dragged the three pieces of his doorstep back together. He set them in a bed of gravel and fussed at them until they sat level. He went down to look at the stream where it had found its new bed.

"It's a better spot," he said when he came back. "Nearer. I might have to thank you for that."

He looked up at the cliff over the cave, at the slabs hanging from it.

"Those will come down in the spring," he said. "Not on us, I think. A little to the left."

She watched him. He talked while he worked, about the stream, the slabs, and the weather tomorrow. None of it was about anything. She found that she listened to it.

On the second evening he sat down across the fire from her with the kettle lid on his knee. He turned it over in his hands.

"They call me Zhìwēng, down in the valleys," he said. He shrugged. The shrug went back a long way. "It does as well as any."

"What does it mean?"

"Wise old man." He considered that. "Old, anyway."

He turned the lid over again.

"I had another name before that one," he said. "And one before that. I don't remember the first one anymore." He set the lid down beside him. "I thought it would matter more than it did."

She looked into the fire and said nothing. She would not have known which name to give him.

On the third morning the warm came all the way down into her hands. She stood up, and her legs held. She went out onto the doorstep, which wobbled. She looked down the mountain, south, toward the plains, the hills, and the palace on the highest of them.

He came out behind her with the kettle lid still in his hand.

"You'll go back," he said.

"Yes."

He nodded. He did not ask her why, and he did not tell her not to.

"Mind the path," he said. "It's slippery. It always is."

At the edge of the ring she looked back. He had sat down again on his mended doorstep, with the blanket around him and the kettle lid on his knee. He lifted his hand to her with two fingers raised, the first and the second, held together.

She had seen a hand make that shape before, in a courtyard, calling a man with a knife. This was something else. She did not know what. She did not lift her own hand.

It's a greeting between equals, met on a road. I didn't know that then. The next time I saw him, I knew. Look, the new stick's caught. Hear it spit.

She went down the mountain.
