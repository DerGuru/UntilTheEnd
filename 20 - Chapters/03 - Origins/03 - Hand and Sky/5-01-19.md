The palace was eleven days south. She walked it.

She noticed things on the way. The cold, in the mornings. The smell of the pines in the wet. The stones of the old road under her feet, which were uneven, and which she had walked on for so long she had stopped feeling them. None of it meant anything to her. She noticed it anyway.

The lord was in the courtyard when she came in through the gate. He had come home from the mountains weeks before, when the weather turned. He looked surprised to see her, then pleased.

"Is it done?" he asked.

"I'm done," Liè said.

He nodded.

"Good. There's a town on the eastern road," he said. Then he explained it, from the beginning and in order, as his fathers had. A man in that town had made a song about his great-grandfather, the one who had built the house on the hill. It was not a kind song. People sang it in the markets. The lord had asked the town for the man, and the town had not sent him.

He put his hand on her shoulder. It was the lightest of all of them. It had always rested there like a hand on the back of a chair.

She had noticed the hands before: which was heavy, which was light, which had hesitated. This time she felt it. She felt the weight of it, small as it was, sitting on the bone.

"East," he said.

She went. She had always gone.

The town on the eastern road had heard of her for longer than any of its people had been alive. When she came up the road, they opened the gate before she reached it. Two men came out with the songmaker between them, his hands tied in front of him. They set him down in the road in front of her and backed away. They were offering him.

He was a small man with ink stains on his fingers. He looked up at her. He did not beg. He had probably known for a long time how his song would end.

She put her hand over his face and pushed. The back of his skull came apart against the gatepost.

Then the town. The order had been the town that sheltered him, so she did the town, as she had done every town. The men who had brought the songmaker out were the next to go. She took one through the ribs with her fist. The other ran. She turned from ten paces, and the force caught him from behind. It folded him over backward at the waist. A woman in the gateway screamed at her, and the scream stopped. A man swung a mattock at her head from a doorway. She broke his arm with the mattock still in it, then drove the end of the handle back into his face.

She walked in through the gate, laying her palm on the thatch as she went, and the fire ran ahead of her along the eaves. People came out of the houses into the lanes. She turned in the lanes, and the lanes emptied in rings. The smoke came down low. The heat made the air shake.

In a lane near the middle of the town a child ran out of a burning doorway and into her legs. It fell at her feet. She reached down for it as she had reached down for everything else that day.

Her hand was on it. She saw its face.

I can't give you that face. I have tried every night since. I've tried on nights like this one, with the frost coming down and your fire too small for the cold. It won't come. I know only that there was a face, and that she saw it.

She had it up, out, and set on a wall in three movements.

Then she stood in the lane with her hands empty. Around her the town went on burning. She did not move.

By then the town had stopped being a town.
