Morning came the way it always came—light moving through the trees, birds waking in the branches, the smell of cold ash and dew. The same. Exactly the same. As though nothing had changed, as though the world had not listened to what had happened in the dark and decided it did not matter.

Mei stood at the edge of the camp.

She had not slept. The warm in her chest had come back during the night—slowly, filling the emptiness the way water fills a hole—but it brought no comfort. It sat there, steady and useless, the thing that had saved Koh and ruined everything else.

Nobody came to her. The camp moved around her the way water moves around a stone in the river—flowing past, not touching, leaving a dry space where she stood. Women packed. Children were gathered. The hunters checked their spears. The ordinary rhythm of morning, and Mei was not in it.

Then her mother came.

She came from the direction of the sleeping places, walking in a straight line, not looking left or right. Her face was set—the flat, closed expression she wore when she was carrying something heavy. Not a burden in her arms. A burden behind her eyes.

She stopped in front of Mei.

They looked at each other. Mei waited for words. Her mother's mouth opened once, and then closed, and then opened again, and nothing came out.

She was afraid, Mei realized. Not of the big man. Not of the People. Of Mei.

Her own mother was afraid of her.

The understanding hit like cold water—not sharp, not sudden, but total, soaking through everything at once. Mei's eyes stung. She bit the inside of her cheek until she tasted iron.

Her mother reached into the fold of her carrying skin and pulled out a bundle—small, tight, wrapped in a piece of hide. She pressed it into Mei's hands. Their fingers touched. Her mother's hands were shaking.

Dried meat. Roots. A handful of the good nuts, the ones with the hard shells that lasted. Enough for days, if Mei was careful.

No words. Her mother could not find words for this—for the daughter she had carried and fed and slept beside and kept warm, the daughter she was letting go because the man who led them had said go, and she was not strong enough to say no.

Her hand stayed on Mei's for one breath. Two. Three.

Then she pulled away.

She turned and walked back the way she had come, into the camp, into the group, into the space where she belonged because she had not pushed anyone with something that had no name.

Mei held the bundle against her chest.

She looked for Koh.

Koh was not there. Mei's eyes moved across the camp—the sleeping places, the fire circle, the rocks where they sat, the path to the water. Koh was not at any of them. She scanned the faces, counted them automatically, the way she always did.

One missing.

Koh was hiding. Mei could feel her—faintly, at the far edge of the camp, behind the big rock where the children played. A warmth she knew as well as her own. Koh was there, and Koh was not coming out, and Mei understood this too. Koh was not hiding from Mei. Koh was hiding from the moment—the moment where Mei walked away and Koh stayed, and both of them knew it was happening and neither of them could stop it.

Mei looked at the rock. She could go there. She could find Koh, and hold her, and say something—goodbye or come back or I'm sorry or wait for me. But the words would change nothing, and the holding would only make the letting go harder, and Mei was eleven summers old and already learning that some things are worse when you look at them directly.

She looked away.

The old man stood at the edge of the camp, apart from the others. He was leaning on a stick—not because he needed it, but because it was the habit of a body that had been walking longer than most people had been alive. His face was turned toward Mei.

He did not speak. He had spoken last night, and it had not been enough, and he did not seem like a man who repeated himself.

But he looked at her. A long, steady look—not soft, not kind, not pitying. Something harder than all of those. The look of a man who sees clearly and does not look away, even when what he sees is wrong.

He dipped his head. Once. Small.

Then he turned away too.

Mei shifted the bundle in her arms. She looked at the camp—the fires, the skins, the shapes of people she had known since before she could walk. The sounds of morning. The smell of home.

She turned.

One step. The ground was hard and cold under her bare foot. Morning dew on the grass, cool between her toes. The air smelled of earth and distance.

Another step. The camp sounds were already dimmer—not because they were far, but because her ears were adjusting, making space, preparing for the silence that was coming.

Another.

The warm sat in her chest—heavy, full, the only warmth that was hers. The only thing she carried that could not be taken away, that did not depend on the permission of others, that did not require her to be small enough or quiet enough or normal enough to keep.

She did not look back.

The trees took her. The shadows between the trunks closed around her like water closing over a stone, and the camp was behind her, and the People were behind her, and Koh was behind her, and her mother was behind her.

Ahead there was nothing she knew.

She walked into it.
