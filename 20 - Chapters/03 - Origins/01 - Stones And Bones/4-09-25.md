Summer arrived. And the need came as a pressure behind her ribs that had nothing to do with the warm. A human pressure. The ancient, undeniable weight of a social animal separated from its kind.

She had heard a bird that morning — two notes, repeated, a call meant for another bird. The answer came from across the valley, faint but certain. Two voices finding each other across distance. Mei sat in the cave entrance and listened until the calling stopped.

Then she packed.

The descent was easier now. She knew the mountain — every ridge, every game trail, every place where the footing was bad. Her body was different. Longer, harder, her legs corded with muscle from climbing, her hands rough as bark from stone, wood, and cold. She was not a child anymore. She was not sure what she was.

She found a group by the river, in the place where the water widened and slowed. A large group — thirty-five, maybe more. They had good fires, lean-tos of branches and skins. Children ran at the water's edge. The sound of their voices drifted up the slope like smoke. Mei stood in the tree line and let the sound wash over her — the children's voices, the crack of a fire being fed, a woman calling. The sounds filled a space that had been empty so long she had forgotten it was there.

Mei approached carefully. She carried meat — fresh, from a kill two days old. She walked slowly, hands visible. She had practiced this. The approach, the offering, the posture that said I am useful, not dangerous. She had rehearsed being small.

The group was wary but not hostile. A woman took the meat. A man pointed to a spot at the edge of camp. Mei sat.

This time, she was careful.

She did not react when the warm told her things. When a predator moved in the trees beyond the camp, she held still and let the group's own watchers notice it. When she felt the fish gathering in a pool downstream — dense, bright clusters of warmth — she walked there casually and pointed, as though she had seen ripples, nothing more.

She ate what they shared. She worked when they worked. She slept at the edge, where the firelight barely reached. The effort of suppression was constant. When the warm flared at a rustle in the brush or at a shift in the air, she breathed through it, kept her face still, forced her body into the posture of someone who had heard nothing. It was exhausting. Harder than hunting. Pretending to be less than she was took more strength than being what she was.

Days passed. The group relaxed around her and the space around her shrank. A woman sat closer at the fire. A man tossed her a piece of dried fish without ceremony. The children lost their wariness. They circled, stared, brought her things to hold. A girl with tangled hair put a caterpillar on Mei's knee and waited for a reaction. Mei picked it up and pretended to eat it. The girl shrieked with delight.

On the fifth night, an old woman came to sit beside Mei. She touched Mei's hair. It had become long, tangled and matted where rain had worked it into ropes. She made a sound that was half disapproval, half amusement. Then, without asking, she began to work her fingers through it.

Mei held very still. The old woman's hands were warm and rough but patient. She worked from the ends, separating the knots with her fingertips, pulling gently where the strands had fused. The pulling hurt a little. A small, clean pain. Mei's scalp tingled. Her eyes burned. She did not know why — or she did, which was worse than the burning itself. The last person who had touched her hair had been her mother.

The old woman hummed while she worked. A sound that said *I am here, doing this, taking my time*. When she finished, she patted Mei's head once but firm.

More days went by. Mei helped build a new lean-to. She carried water. She showed the children how to find grubs under bark — pulling away the loose pieces, pointing at the white shapes curling in the rot. The children squealed and jostled for position. One of them grabbed her hand and did not let go. Mei let herself be held. The child's palm was small, sticky, and perfect.

She ate with them at the fire. Not longer the edge, but in the circle. Someone passed her a rib bone with meat still clinging to it. The fat was rich. The meat was tough. The taste was better than anything she had cooked alone — not because of the food, but because of the hands that passed it. The woman beside her spoke a question, the words foreign but the shape familiar. Mei shook her head and smiled. The woman smiled back.

She almost stayed. The almost was a weight in her chest, warm and terrible. Every morning she woke and the group was there, breathing, moving, being. Every morning the weight grew heavier.

She told herself that she'd stay one more day. Every night at the fire's edge, she told herself that. Every morning the one-more-day became another day, until the counting stopped mattering.

They were sitting in the circle — Mei, the women, the children drowsy against their mothers. The fire was low, the coals bright. A log split — a sharp crack, resin bursting inside the wood — and a chunk of glowing coal the size of a pebble shot from the flames toward the girl's face.

Mei's hand moved. She caught the coal mid-air and flicked it back into the fire in one motion. The girl did not flinch. She had not seen it. But the woman beside Mei had seen the hand. The hand that moved too fast. The hand that caught a burning coal from the air the way you catch a falling leaf. The hand that was not burned.

Mei looked down. Her palm was unmarked. The warm had rushed there — instant, instinctive. She had not told it to.

Their eyes met. And Mei met the look people give to things that should not exist.

Mei closed her hand. But it was too late. She looked down at the fire, stood up and gathered her things. The child who had held her hand was turning towards her. It raised one hand — small, open. A wave. Or a reaching.

Mei turned and walked toward the mountains.

The climb back was long. She did not hurry. She had hidden the warm. She had been careful, patient, useful. She had done everything right. And it had not been enough, because the thing that was wrong was not something she did. It was something she was. She had known this before, but this time the knowing was deeper. Not a bruise. A scar. Scars do not hurt. They are simply part of the shape of you.

The cave entrance was dark, narrow, and familiar. She squeezed through. The warm of the cave met her — dense, patient, unchanged.

She sat on the stone floor. Pressed her palms flat. Let the warm pour in.

Her eyes did not burn this time. She was past that. Her body felt strange — too full of the cave's warmth, too empty of everything else. Days among people, and then this. The contrast was a blade in itself.

She began to shape a new blade.
