They found the nest because of the ants.

Mei saw them first—a line of black bodies moving up a birch trunk, each one carrying a pale grain twice the size of its head. She stopped, crouched, pressed her nose close to the bark. The ants smelled of something sharp and sour, like crushed leaves left in rain.

"Koh."

"What?"

"Look."

Koh came back, because Koh always came back when Mei said look, even when what Mei wanted to show her was a beetle or a pattern in the mud or the way light fell through a certain leaf. Koh would look, shrug, and say something like "Good beetle" or "Bad mud" and that would be enough.

But this time Mei pointed up.

"They carry."

"Ants carry. That is what ants do."

"No. They carry from somewhere." Mei followed the line with her finger—up the trunk, along a branch, across to the next tree, and there, where the branches forked and a tangle of dead leaves had collected in the crook, a dark shape. Too large for a knot. Too round.

"Nest," Mei said.

Koh squinted. Then her eyes went wide.

"Eggs?"

"Maybe."

They looked at each other. Koh grinned. Mei grinned back.

The tree was not tall, but the lowest branch was higher than Koh could reach, and Koh was the tallest of the children. Mei walked around the trunk, pressing her palms against the bark, feeling for holds. Rough here. Smooth there. A ridge where the bark had split and hardened again, just wide enough for a toe.

"I go," Koh said.

"You fall."

"I do not fall."

"You fell at the river."

"The river was wet."

Mei pointed at the ridge in the bark, then at a knob higher up, then at the branch. "There. There. There."

Koh studied the path Mei had traced. Tried it. Her foot found the ridge, her hand found the knob, and she pulled herself up with the graceless efficiency of someone who trusted her body more than she planned her route. The branch bent under her weight. She crawled along it, hand over hand, until she reached the fork.

Mei watched from below, her neck craned back, the sharp smell of tree sap and crushed ants filling her nose.

"Three," Koh called down.

"Three?"

"Three eggs. Big."

Koh tucked them into the fold of her wrap, one at a time—careful, slow, her tongue poking out between her teeth the way it always did when she concentrated. Then she climbed down, faster than she had gone up, and dropped the last body-length to the ground.

She held them out. Three eggs, mottled brown and cream, each one the size of Mei's fist. Warm.

"How did you know?" Koh asked.

Mei shrugged. "Ants go where food is."

"I do not watch ants."

"I do."

Koh laughed—the big, sudden laugh that startled birds and made the adults frown—and handed Mei an egg. They walked back toward camp, and the eggs were warm against Mei's palms, and the afternoon sun was warm on her shoulders, and somewhere behind them a bird sang three notes and stopped, and everything was exactly as it should be.

They came back to the People with dirty knees and three eggs and no explanation for where they had been. Mei's mother looked at the eggs, looked at Mei, looked at Koh. Shook her head once. Said nothing.

Koh cracked her egg on a stone and drank it raw, the yolk running down her chin. Mei ate hers slower, tipping the shell to let the white slide onto her tongue. It tasted of salt and something else—the faint mineral flavor of the nest, of the tree, of the place where it had been made.

The old man watched them from across the fire. He said something to the woman beside him—too quiet for Mei to hear—and the woman smiled and looked away.

Koh wiped her chin with the back of her hand and bumped Mei's shoulder.

"Tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow."

They did not need to say more. Tomorrow meant: again. Out there, together, looking for whatever the world had hidden and Mei could find. This was how it worked. Koh was the legs and the nerve. Mei was the eyes.

Neither of them knew this was remarkable. It was simply who they were.
