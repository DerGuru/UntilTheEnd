He waited until the fires were lit. Mei sat at the edge of the camp, where she had been since the clearing. Nobody had come near her. Not the women, not the children, not even Koh — someone had pulled Koh away. Koh had gone, looking back over her shoulder. Mei had watched her go and had not followed because following would have made it worse. For Koh. For all of it.

The warm was still thin in her chest. A thread where a river had been. She felt empty, cold, and very, very tired.

Her hands were trembling. She pressed them flat against the ground to make them stop. The earth was cold. The warm in her chest was barely there — a wisp, a thread. The push that had thrown the big man had taken almost everything she had. She was empty in a way she had never been empty before.

The big man stood by the main fire. He stood as he always stood — wide, planted, taking up the space that was his by size and by habit. But tonight there was something else in the way he held himself. A purpose. A direction. He had been waiting for this, Mei realized. Not for today specifically — but for a reason. For the moment when the thing Mei was became something he could use.

He spoke.

Not to Mei. To the People.

His voice was loud enough to carry across the camp — the fire-voice, the voice that meant everyone listen. He pointed at himself. At the place in the dust where he had landed. He made the sound of impact — hands slapping together, hard. He showed how far he had flown — three steps, four, exaggerated, arms wide. The People watched.

Then he pointed at Mei.

The word he used was not a word Mei knew. It was old, guttural, a sound that meant something between wrong, sick, and unclean. The kind of sound you made when food had turned, when water smelled of death, when the ground shook and didn't stop.

He said it again. Louder. Pointing.

Then he swept his hand across the camp — the children, the women, the hunters. His voice dropped. The meaning was clear even without understanding every word: What if she does that to your child? What if that happens to you? To your woman? To your old one?

The People shifted. Not all of them — but enough. Heads turned toward Mei. The firelight caught the whites of their eyes.

The big man spoke more. He spoke for a long time. He spoke with his body — gestures, postures, the full performance of a man who understood that fear was not an argument but a feeling, and that feelings did not need to be right to be believed. He reminded them of the cat — how Mei had known. The healing — how fast, how wrong. The water, the flint, the waking in the night. Each thing that had once been useful, he turned. Each thing that had once been a gift, he made a threat.

He mimicked the push — clutching his chest, staggering, falling. He did it twice. The second time, he pointed at a child. A specific child — the youngest girl, two summers old, asleep in her mother's arms. The meaning was plain: What if the next time, it's her?

The woman who had pulled her child away from Mei, moons ago — she nodded first. Her chin dipped, just once, and she held her child tighter.

The hunter who had taken the flint — he nodded too. As if agreeing with himself.

One by one, the nods came. Not from everyone. Not from the old man.

The old man stood up.

He was thin, bent. The firelight made his face look like carved wood. He spoke differently from the big man — quieter, slower, with the patience of someone who had been alive long enough to know that patience was the only weapon that lasted.

The fire lit his face from below — deep shadows in the hollows of his cheeks, the ridge of his brow dark against the flame-light. He had earned his place not by size or strength but by the accumulation of winters survived, decisions made correctly, dangers predicted. He was the memory of the People. When he spoke, people listened — not because they feared him, but because he had been right more often than he had been wrong.

He pointed at Mei. Then at the earth — the places where she had found water. At the sky — the mornings she had read the weather. At the trees — the directions she had known. His meaning was plain: she helps. She finds. She warns. Why throw away what serves you?

He spoke for a long time. Longer than the big man had. He spoke about the drought, when Mei had found water where no one expected it. About the cat, when her warning had saved the column. About the flint, the weather, the hundred small things she had done that made the group's life easier. Each thing he named was true. Each thing he named made Mei sound useful, necessary, worth keeping.

But he spoke with facts, and the big man had spoken with fear. Facts require thought. Fear only requires a heartbeat.

The big man listened with his arms crossed. When the old man finished, the big man said one word. Mei could not hear it clearly, but the shape of it was a question: And when she throws you?

The old man had no answer for that.

Silence again. The fire popped. Sparks rose and died.

The big man looked around the circle. His eyes stopped on each face. The ones who nodded. The ones who looked away. The ones who stared at the ground. He was counting, Mei realized. The same way she counted. But he was counting something different — he was counting who belonged to him, who didn't, and whether the number was enough.

It was enough.

Mei counted them — the ones who nodded, the ones who looked away, the ones who stared at the ground. She counted automatically, thoroughly. Fourteen had nodded or stayed silent. Three had looked uncomfortable — the woman who shared water with Mei's mother, a young hunter who had once used the flint Mei found, and the old man. Three was not enough. Three had never been enough.

He spoke one more time. Short. Final. A sound that meant: decided. Done. He did not need to raise his voice. The decision had been made while the old man was still speaking — made in the nods, in the silences, in the way the group had already rearranged itself around the big man's gravity.

Then his eyes found Mei, at the edge of the dark, and he held them there for a long moment — not gloating, not angry, just flat, immovable, with the weight of stone behind them.

The People did not look at her.

Mei sat in the dark with her arms wrapped around her knees and listened to the sound of her world ending. It sounded like fire, silence, and the absence of anyone saying *wait*.
