He waited until the fires were lit.

Mei sat at the edge of the camp, where she had been since the clearing. Nobody had come near her. Not the women, not the children, not even Koh—someone had pulled Koh away, and Koh had gone, looking back over her shoulder, and Mei had watched her go and had not followed because following would have made it worse. For Koh. For all of it.

The warm was still thin in her chest. A thread where a river had been. She felt empty and cold and very, very tired.

The big man stood by the main fire. He stood the way he always stood—wide, planted, taking up the space that was his by size and by habit. But tonight there was something else in the way he held himself. A purpose. A direction. He had been waiting for this, Mei realized. Not for today specifically—but for a reason. For the moment when the thing Mei was became something he could use.

He spoke.

Not to Mei. To the People.

His voice was loud enough to carry across the camp—the fire-voice, the voice that meant everyone listen. He pointed at himself. At the place in the dust where he had landed. He made the sound of impact—hands slapping together, hard. He showed how far he had flown—three steps, four, exaggerated, arms wide. The People watched.

Then he pointed at Mei.

The word he used was not a word Mei knew. It was old, guttural, a sound that meant something between wrong and sick and unclean. The kind of sound you made when food had turned, when water smelled of death, when the ground shook and didn't stop.

He said it again. Louder. Pointing.

Then he swept his hand across the camp—the children, the women, the hunters. His voice dropped. The meaning was clear even without understanding every word: What if she does that to your child? What if that happens to you? To your woman? To your old one?

The People shifted. Not all of them—but enough. Heads turned toward Mei. The firelight caught the whites of their eyes.

The big man spoke more. He spoke for a long time. He spoke with his body—gestures, postures, the full performance of a man who understood that fear was not an argument but a feeling, and that feelings did not need to be right to be believed. He reminded them of the cat—how Mei had known. The healing—how fast, how wrong. The water, the flint, the waking in the night. Each thing that had once been useful, he turned. Each thing that had once been a gift, he made a threat.

The woman who had pulled her child away from Mei, moons ago—she nodded first. Her chin dipped, just once, and she held her child tighter.

The hunter who had taken the flint—he nodded too. As if agreeing with himself.

One by one, the nods came. Not from everyone. Not from the old man.

The old man stood up.

He was thin and bent and the firelight made his face look like carved wood. He spoke differently from the big man—quieter, slower, with the patience of someone who had been alive long enough to know that patience was the only weapon that lasted.

He pointed at Mei. Then at the earth—the places where she had found water. At the sky—the mornings she had read the weather. At the trees—the directions she had known. His meaning was plain: she helps. She finds. She warns. Why throw away what serves you?

The big man listened with his arms crossed. When the old man finished, the big man said one word. Mei could not hear it clearly, but the shape of it was a question: And when she throws you?

The old man had no answer for that.

Silence again. The fire popped. Sparks rose and died.

The big man looked around the circle. His eyes stopped on each face. The ones who nodded. The ones who looked away. The ones who stared at the ground. He was counting, Mei realized. The same way she counted. But he was counting something different—he was counting who belonged to him, and who didn't, and whether the number was enough.

It was enough.

He spoke one more time. Short. Final. A sound that meant: decided. Done.

Then his eyes found Mei, at the edge of the dark, and he held them there for a long moment—not gloating, not angry, just flat and certain, the way a stone is certain of its own weight.

The People did not look at her.

Mei sat in the dark with her arms wrapped around her knees and listened to the sound of her world ending, and it sounded like fire and silence and the absence of anyone saying wait.
