The descent had taken her in a direction she had not gone before — south and west, through valleys she had not mapped, past ridges she had not climbed. The warm guided her as it always did. Away from predators, toward water, along paths where the earth was firm and the footing held. She was not looking for people. She was not looking for anything. She was walking because the mountain was behind her and the world was ahead. After many summers of stillness, movement felt like something her body remembered how to want.

She smelled the smoke first.

It came on a shift in the wind — faint, almost lost in the scent of pine and wet stone. But smoke was smoke, and her body knew what smoke meant before her mind caught up. She slowed. Crouched. Let the warm expand — pushing it to her ears and her nose. The world sharpened.

Voices. Distant, indistinct — the murmur of a group, the sound that twenty-odd people make when they are together and not trying to be quiet. Children. A woman's call. A man's laugh.

Mei moved closer. Carefully, as she'd approach a predator's territory — upwind, silent, using the terrain. She found a ridge above a clearing where a river bent. She lay flat in the scrub and looked down.

The camp was below her. Fires. Shelters. People moving in the patterns she knew — the women at work, the children in orbit, the men at the edges. Ordinary. Familiar.

Too familiar.

The recognition came in pieces. She was too far for faces, but the way they moved. A woman who walked with her weight on her left hip, as she always had, because of the knee she had hurt years ago. 

Mei's breath stopped.

She pushed warm to her eyes. The distance collapsed. Faces resolved.

She knew them.

Not all of them — some were new. Young ones who had been babies when she left, now walking, now old enough to carry things. A little girl ran between the shelters with her arms out — headlong, trusting the ground to catch her. Mei's throat closed. The girl ran like Koh used to run.

A woman she did not recognize, stood near the fire. But the rest — the shapes, the postures, the arrangement of bodies around the central fire — she knew. She had counted these faces every evening for the first eleven summers of her life.

Her People.

The big man was there. Older, thicker, his hair greyer at the temples. But the same — the same broad shoulders, the same way of occupying the best space, the same quiet assumption that everything around him was his. He sat by the fire and ate. The others arranged themselves around him with the careful geometry of people who know where the power is.

Mei's eyes moved across the camp. Searching. She did not know for whom — for all of them, for anyone, for the specific shape of a woman whose arm around her shoulders had been the warmest thing in the world.

She found her mother. Thinner. Older. Sitting apart from the main group, near a shelter at the edge. Her hair was longer and streaked with grey that had not been there when Mei had left. She was working — scraping a hide, the motion automatic, the rhythm Mei had watched ten thousand times. A rhythm that had once meant safety, home, the world working as it should. Her mother's shoulders curved inward now, as though she was trying to take up less space. As though she had learned that lesson too.

Mei's chest hurt. The summers in her cave, bears, winters that should have killed her. Summers of training the warm, shaping stone, surviving. And now, looking down at this camp — the fires, the shelters, the ordinary pattern of people living together — all of it crumbled. The warm had no answer for this. It could not protect her from the ache of seeing home from the outside.

She looked for the old man. Scanned every face, every shape, every seated figure. He was not there. His absence was a cold spot in the warm map of the camp — a place where something should have been and wasn't.

Gone. The word arrived without ceremony. He was gone.

The old man — who had spoken for her, who had dipped his head, who had been the only one in the camp to look at her without flinching — was not at his place by the fire. The stick he leaned on was not propped against any shelter. His warmth was not in her range. The cold spot was absolute.

Mei lay in the scrub, breathed, watched and felt the world tilt under her.

Her face was still and empty. The face of someone who had not had a reason to make expressions in many summers. Inside, everything was in motion. The warm churned. Her heart hammered. Her hands wanted to grip, push, reach. She wanted to walk down the ridge, walk into the camp, sit at the fire, say nothing, just sit. She wanted this so badly her teeth ached.

She should have left. She knew this. Every lesson of the last attempts of joining other people told her to leave. Do not approach. Do not be seen. You do not belong here.

She stayed too long.

A hunter at the tree line turned. Instinct. The wind shifting. The warm, leaking from her despite her control, a signal that said that something is here, which should not be here.

He looked up the ridge. His eyes found the scrub where she lay. He could not see her — she was too still, too well-hidden. But he could feel something, the way prey feels a predator's attention.

He called out. Others came. Three hunters, then five. They spread along the base of the ridge, spears ready, scanning the scrub.

Mei lay still. The warm pulled inward — tight, dense, controlled. She could hide. She had hidden from animals with better senses than these men.

But then one of them climbed.

He came up the ridge — not toward her, toward a rock outcrop thirty paces to her left. But the angle would bring him close. Close enough to see.

Mei stood.

The movement drew every eye. Five hunters below, one on the slope, all of them staring at the figure that had risen from the scrub like a ghost — tall, lean, armed, with eyes that were too steady and a stillness that was not human.

She saw the recognition happen. An older hunter, one she remembered. His name had sounded like a grunt followed by a breath. His face changed. His spear dropped six inches.

He said a word. Her name. The one she had not heard spoken aloud in many summers.

The others looked at him. Back at her. The spears came back up.

They circled. Below and to the sides, cutting off the downhill routes. Mei did not run. She stood on the ridge and watched them arrange themselves around her with the coordination of men who hunted together. She felt the warm pulse in her chest — hot, ready, waiting — *not yet*.

The camp was stirring. People coming to the ridge's base. Faces she knew and faces she didn't, all of them staring up at the figure on the ridge.

The big man pushed through. He stood at the base and looked up at her. Time had not diminished him — he was still the largest person she had ever seen, still radiating the thick, heavy pressure that had made her skin crawl when she was a child.

Their eyes met.

He smiled. It was not a kind smile. It was the smile of a man who had thrown something away, found it coming back, and intended to throw it away again, further this time.

His eyes swept over her — taking in the height, the lean muscle, the spear she hadn't dropped, the absolute stillness of her face. She saw the calculation. Once a child. Now, something else. Something that had survived what he had thrown it into.

He bellowed something to the hunters. Short. Sharp.

The circle tightened.

Mei stood in the center. The warm burned in her chest — brighter than it had been in moons, hotter, as though the nearness of these people had fed it something the cave could not. She looked at the big man. He looked at her.

She shifted her weight. Found a stone under her right foot. Good. Heavy. Sharp.

The right kind of stone.
