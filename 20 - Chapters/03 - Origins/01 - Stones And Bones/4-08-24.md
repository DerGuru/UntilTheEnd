Mei had been tracking a deer — a young buck, limping from a bad leg, an easy kill if she could close the distance. The warm told her where it was. Two hundred paces ahead, moving slowly through the scrub at the bottom of the valley. She had been following for most of the morning, patient, quiet, her feet warm and sure on the rock.

She closed to thirty paces. Twenty. The deer did not know she was there. She drew back her arm — fire-hardened tip, ash shaft, bound with dried sinew. The best weapon she had ever made.

She threw. The spear flew true and hit the deer behind the shoulder. It sank deep. The deer stumbled, fell, kicked once.

In the same moment, the bear came out of the scrub to her left.

It had been in a ravine. Deep, narrow, the rock dense and layered above it. Its warmth hit her all at once — not the gradual approach of a moving animal. A wall. Huge. Dense. Hot. Like stepping into sunlight after being in the cave, except the sunlight had claws and weighed as much as five men.

Brown, massive, shoulders rolling. It had smelled the blood. It saw Mei between itself and the kill.

Mei had no weapon. Her spear was in the deer.

The bear charged.

It hit her with its shoulder. Her body went from standing to flying to hitting the ground with a force that drove every thought from her head. She landed on her back on the rocky slope and felt something crack in her side. Not a clean break — a grinding, wrong sensation, deep.

The bear turned. Its eyes were small, dark, and utterly without mercy. It came at her again.

Mei rolled. The bear's paw hit the ground where her head had been. She felt the impact through the rock, a shudder that ran up through her ribs and made the broken thing inside her shift. Pain exploded — white, total, a sound more than a feeling. She screamed.

The warm surged. Not because she told it to. It went to the broken place. Holding. Bracing. As though something inside her had built a scaffold around the break, keeping the pieces in place while the rest of her body kept moving.

She got to her feet. The bear was turning — big, slow turns, the kind that used the whole body. She had three heartbeats before it came again.

She looked around for a safe place. Behind her was a narrow crack in the rock face. Two boulders leaning against each other, leaving a gap barely wider than her shoulders. Too narrow for the bear. If she could reach it.

She ran. The cracked rib screamed. The warm screamed back — muffling, dampening, pushing the pain down to a place where it still existed but no longer controlled her legs. She ran as she'd learned on the mountain — not fast, but precise. Every foot placed. Every step calculated. The warm helped.

The bear followed. She could feel its warmth behind her — enormous, hot, closing.

*Not yet*, she thought. *Not like this.*

She reached the crack. Turned sideways and squeezed through. The rock scraped her back and her chest. The broken rib shifted. She bit through her lip to keep from screaming again.

The bear hit the gap. The boulders shuddered. Its head came through — jaws wide, breath hot and rotten — but its shoulders did not fit. It roared. The sound in the narrow space was like being inside a drum. Mei pressed herself against the back wall of the crack and watched the bear's claws rake the stone, leaving white marks.

She was bleeding. From the lip. From scrapes on her arms and back. And from inside, where the broken rib had shifted.

The bear raged for a long time. Then, slowly, it pulled back. She felt its warmth recede — still close, still burning, but moving. Circling. Looking for another way in.

There was no other way in.

Mei waited. The warm in her chest went to work — slowly, carefully, the scaffold around the broken rib tightening, the edges of the break pulling together. Not fast. Not as it had healed the thorn-cut or the ibex-wound. This was bigger. This would take time.

The bear circled until the light changed. Then its warmth moved — not away. Toward the deer. She heard it sniff. Heard the wet sound of a muzzle pushing into the wound.

No.

That was *her* deer. Her kill. Her food for the next moon. She had tracked it all morning. She had thrown perfectly. And this thing — this mountain of fur and teeth — was going to eat it because she was hiding in a crack like a rabbit.

The warm flooded her. Arms, legs, chest, back, skull — all at once. Her skin tightened. Her bones felt like stone. Full body. Everything she had.

She squeezed out of the crack. The broken rib screamed. She ignored it.

The bear was at the deer, head down, tearing. It did not hear her until she was close. She grabbed a stone from the ground — big, heavy, the size of both fists together. Pulled the warm into her arm. Into her shoulder. Into the rage.

She swung.

The stone hit the bear's flank with a crack that echoed off the valley walls. The bear roared and spun. She was already picking up another stone — bigger, jagged. She hit it again. Across the snout. Blood sprayed. The bear staggered sideways, shaking its head.

A third stone. This one she threw — qi-fueled, from the gut, from the fury. It struck the bear's shoulder and the animal *yelped*. A sound she had not known bears could make.

It looked at her. Small dark eyes, recalculating. This was not prey anymore. This was something that hurt back.

The bear turned and ran. Crashing through the scrub, its warmth fading fast — hot, dimming, gone.

Mei stood over her deer. Breathing hard. Shaking. The warm drained out of her limbs and the pain came back all at once — the rib, the scrapes, a deep ache in her arms from swinging stones heavier than her head. Her legs folded and she sat down hard next to the carcass.

But the deer was hers.

She pulled the spear free. Cleaned it on the grass and started cutting.

Then she took it back to the cave. Slowly. Carefully. Each step a negotiation between what her body wanted to do and what the warm insisted it could still manage. One more step, one more, one more.

She made it.

She lay on the cave floor, surrounded by strips of meat hung to dry on sticks she had wedged between the rocks. The deer was butchered — every piece she could carry, cut and hung or salted with the mineral crust she scraped from the cave walls. It had taken the rest of the day, working one-handed when the rib made her gasp, but it was done.

She pressed herself against the warm stone and let the warm work. The rib shifted, aligned. The pain dulled from sharp to deep to present. The scrapes closed. The blood dried.

The meat would last for some time. She could rest now.

By the second morning, she could sit up. By the third, she could stand. By the fifth, the rib was a memory — a dull ache when she twisted wrong, nothing more.

But she dreamed of the bear for many nights after — the speed, the weight, the small dark eyes that held nothing personal. It had not hated her. It had not wanted her dead. She had been in its space, and it had responded the way mountains respond to things that stand where rock wants to fall.

She began making a better spear.
