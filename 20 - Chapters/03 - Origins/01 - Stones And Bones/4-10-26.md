The morning air tasted of frost, when she found a stone. Grey-green. Fine-grained. Heavy in her hand, heavier than it looked. When she turned it, the light caught an edge where it had fractured naturally — clean, sharp, the kind of edge that cut without being told to.

Flint. Good flint. The best she had ever held.

She sat in front of the cave and turned the stone in her hands. The warm in her fingers was steady now — constant, unremarkable, like blood flow. She could feel the stone's grain through her skin, how it wanted to break, the lines of least resistance running through it like rivers through a landscape.

She struck it with another stone. The first piece that came off was too thick — chunky, with a blunt edge. She turned the core. Studied the angle. Struck again. Thinner. Better. A flake the length of her hand, curved slightly, with an edge that caught the light.

She pressed the edge against her thumb. Blood welled — a thin, bright line. Clean. The warm rushed towards it, healing it instantly.

She had watched the old man make blades. Summers ago, in the camp, in the other life. He had sat cross-legged near the fire with stones around him like sleeping animals, and his hands had moved — strike, turn, strike, press. Blades had emerged from the stone like things that had always been inside, waiting for someone patient enough to find them.

Mei had watched. She had always watched.

Now her hands remembered what her eyes had seen.

Strike. Turn. Strike. Press. The flint gave up its secrets — blade after blade, each one better than the last. Her warm-strengthened fingers hit harder and more precisely than any normal hand. The stone responded. Flakes came off in shapes she wanted — long, thin, with edges that could cut meat, scrape hide, and carve wood.

She made a hand axe first with the core stone, both faces worked, the edge running all the way around. It sat in her palm like it had been grown there — heavy, balanced, the weight of something serious. She swung it experimentally against a branch. The wood split clean.

She made small ones for cutting sinew. Long ones for slicing meat. A scraper with one flat edge and one sharp, for working hides — pulling the fat and membrane off in long strips, as she'd seen the women do.

Spearheads. This was harder — the stone had to be thin enough to bind to a shaft but thick enough not to shatter on impact. She broke four before she got one right. The fifth was a flat, leaf-shaped thing with a point like a needle and edges that would open flesh like water opening around a diving stone.

She bound it to a shaft with wet sinew — wrapped tight, crisscrossed, the sinew shrinking as it dried until the head was locked on so firmly she could not twist it free. She had seen the hunters do this. She had seen everything the hunters had done. Now, alone on a mountain with warm hands and endless time, she reproduced it.

The spear was good. Better than good. The warm had made her hands precise in ways that no hunter's hands were — fingers that could feel the grain of wood, the tension of sinew, and the exact angle where stone wanted to break. She was not more skilled than the old man had been. She was more precise, the way a blade is more precise than a hand.

She made another spear. And another. Three spears, each slightly different — one heavy for throwing at close range, one light for distance, one with a wide head for cutting rather than piercing.

Then she made traps.

She had seen the women set snares — simple loops of twisted fiber, placed at game trails. Mei's version was different. She built deadfalls. Heavy stones balanced on triggers made of stick and sinew, placed where the warm told her animals moved. The triggers were delicate — a touch, a nudge, and the stone dropped. She tested each one by throwing pebbles at the trigger from a distance. When the stone fell true, she moved on.

She built a new drying rack to replace the crude pile of sticks from before, but a proper structure. Bone uprights driven into cracks in the rock, crosspieces lashed with sinew, a frame that held strips of meat in the wind and the thin mountain sun. She draped hides over a frame of bent branches — a curing rack, where the skins could dry without rotting.

She made rope from twisted plant fiber, soaked, beaten, and twisted again. She made a carrying sack from ibex hide. She made a stone bowl by grinding a depression into soft sandstone with a harder rock — hours of work, days, the hollow deepening by the width of a grain each pass.

The cave transformed.

Spears stood against the wall — three, four, then six. Blades of different sizes lay on a flat stone near the entrance, organized by shape. Dried meat hung on the rack, dark, hard, and lasting. Folded skins, scraped and softened, piled in the sleeping area. A fire pit, ringed with stones. Water stored in the stone bowl. Rope coiled on a ledge.

It was not a camp. It was not a home, exactly — the word carried a weight she was not ready for. It was a place that had been made to serve, and it served well. When Mei stood at the entrance and looked at it, she felt something she had not felt since the exile.

Competence. 

The quiet, deep satisfaction of a thing done properly. Of looking at a problem and solving it. Of looking at the world and saying *I can live in this*.

She went out and hunted. The new spear flew true. The ibex fell with one throw — the spearhead entering behind the shoulder, the animal dead before it touched the ground. She dragged it back. Butchered it with the new blades, which cut so cleanly that the work was done in half the time. She hung the meat, scraped the hide.

When the cold came again, she was ready.

Stores of dried meat. Skins for warmth. Tools for everything. The warm in her chest, full and steady, a furnace that never banked.

The winter was not easy. No winter was easy. But it was not the desperate, gnawing survival of the last one. It was managed. Controlled. The work of someone who had studied her enemy and prepared.

Mei sat in the cave on the coldest night of the deepest cold, wrapped in skins, eating dried meat, and felt the mountain breathe around her. The warm of the cave, the warm in her chest, the warm of the fire — three layers of heat, each one hers.

She moved her fingers. Idle. Slow. A curl, an extension, a turn of the wrist.

The warm followed between her fingers, in the air above her palm. A thread of it — thin, almost nothing, moving where her hand moved. She stopped. It stopped. She moved again — two fingers tracing a slow arc — and the warm went with them. Trailing. Obedient. Like smoke follows the hand that passes through it, except this was the opposite. The hand moved first and the warm came after, drawn by something she had not asked for.

She stared at her own hand.

Mei moved it again. Slower. The warm pooled in the space between her fingers. A density, a warmth-that-was-not-heat in the air an inch from her skin. When she turned her wrist, it turned. When she spread her fingers, it spread. When she drew them together, it gathered.

She brought her other hand up. Both hands now, moving. Slow and without plan. The warm followed both — two currents, meeting between her palms, separating, joining again. It moved the way her hands told it to move. Just — following. As if her hands knew a shape and the warm wanted to fill it.

The fire cracked. A log shifted. Mei blinked.

She looked at her hands as if they belonged to someone else. They were still moving — a slow rotation, fingers opening and closing, tracing something in the air that had no name. She did not know what she was doing. She did not know why it felt like something she had always known how to do.

She stopped. The warm settled back into her chest. The air between her hands was just air again.

She sat for a long time. Staring at the fire. Then she lifted her hands and did it again.

It came back. The warm, following. The shapes her hands made without being told. The feeling of something moving between her palms — invisible, purposeless, right.

She did it until the fire died. Then she slept.

The next night, she did it again. And the night after. It became the thing she did when the work was done and the cave was quiet and there was nothing left but the fire and the dark and herself. Hands moving. The warm following. Shapes that had no name, made for no reason, leading nowhere she could see.

It was not useful. It did not make her stronger or faster or harder. It did not help her hunt or build or survive.

She did it anyway. Every night. The way breathing is done — not because you decide to, but because something in you knows it must be done.
