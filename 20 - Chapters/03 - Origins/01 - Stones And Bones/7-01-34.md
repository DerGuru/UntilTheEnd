She followed their trail as she'd followed thousands of animal trails before — by warmth, broken ground and the smell of fear. They had run northwest, toward the hills. The group had fragmented in the panic, splitting into clusters of three and four. Mei did not pursue the clusters. She pursued the line — the path of the largest warmth, the densest grouping, the direction where the most feet had gone.

She moved at a walk. There was no need to run. They were faster in the sprint — fear gave legs to people who had never run from anything except predators. But fear also made them stupid. They ran without direction, without plan, crashing through brush and tripping over roots. Mei walked the clean line, cutting corners, reading the terrain. The distance between them shrank with every hundred paces.

She caught up to the first two. The broken-knee man and another one supporting his weight, keeping him upright and going. They were running or rather hobbling together — arm over shoulder, dragging the bad leg, leaving a trail a child could follow. They had not stopped to splint it. There was no time. Panic did not allow for medicine. All they wanted, was to get as far away from Mei and as fast as they could, one carrying the other, slowing them both. As Mei came closer, they turned, somehow feeling the approach of a predator even in their panicked state.

They froze. The hunter without the broken knee grabbed for his blade. Resolve showing in his eyes. He was going to fight even if that meant his death.

Mei did not stop. She crossed the clearing in six strides. Her spear — the one she had taken from the camp — went through the standing man's thigh. Sliding and rolling on the shaft, her elbow connecting with his temple. He fell, and the broken-knee man fell with him. She hit the knee-man in the temple with the shaft of the spear. Not hard enough to kill. Hard enough that he would not get up for a while.

She raised her spear again and walked on.

The trail led through a dry streambed, up a slope, through a stand of birches where the white trunks stood like bones in the grey light. Two more hunters waited there — intentionally this time. They had found their courage, or enough of it. They stood with spears ready.

Mei came through the birch trees and read the scene. The one on the left was braced wrong — weight on his back foot, committed to a throw of his spear he had already decided on. The one on the right was better balanced, waiting, his spear low and close.

The left one threw. Mei stepped sideways — half a step, no more — and the spear went past her shoulder. She did not flinch. She walked toward them. The walking was worse than running would have been, because running was something they understood. Walking was something that predators did when they knew the prey could not escape.

The right-hand hunter held his ground for three more heartbeats. Then he broke and ran. Mei closed in on the other. He still tried to pull a knife from his belt, when Mei hit him once, open-handed, in the chest. The warm was behind the blow — not a blast. Just there giving density and weight. He flew backward, hit a tree, and slid down its trunk.

She walked on.

The main group was ahead — she could feel them, a cluster of warmth, fifteen or more. They had stopped running. They had found a place to stand — a rock basin, walled on three sides by steep stone. One way in. Smart. If she had been an animal, it would have worked.

She came up the slope and saw them.

They were arranged in a half-circle at the entrance. The remaining hunters — five, maybe six — shoulder to shoulder, spears out, blocking the way in. Behind them, pressed against the far wall, women and children. Backs to stone. Nowhere left to go.

And in the center, in front of all of them, the big man.

He held a spear — a good one, the best in the camp, heavy-shafted with a long flint head. He stood as he always had: planted, enormous, filling the space around him with the weight of what he was.

Mei stopped at the base of the ridge. He looked down at her. She looked up at him.

How many summers since he had pointed her away? She had gone. The trees had taken her. Seasons in which she had become something he could not have imagined, while he had remained exactly what he was.

He was afraid. She could feel it — the warm told her, as it told her everything. His heartbeat was fast. His breathing was shallow. But he was the kind of man who stood in front of his fear and dared it to move him. His feet were planted. His spear was ready.

Behind him, she saw faces. She saw them without looking for them. Koh — older, thinner, standing near the women, her eyes enormous. Mei's mother — grey-haired, small, clutching the arm of the woman beside her.

Mei walked toward the opening.
