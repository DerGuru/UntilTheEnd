He was bigger than she remembered. Broader. Heavier. The years had thickened him — his neck, his shoulders, the trunk of his body. He stood at the top of the ridge with the sky behind him, the spear in his hands. He was still the largest thing in the world.

Mei climbed. Not fast. Not slow. The pace of someone walking home.

He threw the spear when she was ten paces away.

It was a good throw — hard, fast, aimed at her center. Mei did not dodge. She did not need to. The spear hit her chest. The shaft cracked. The flint tip shattered. The pieces fell at her feet like dead insects.

The big man stared at the broken spear on the ground. His hands, empty now, hung at his sides.

Mei kept climbing.

He charged. Not with a weapon — with his body. He came down the slope like a rockslide, arms wide, roaring. The sound shook the air. Behind him, someone screamed. He hit Mei with everything he had — two hundred pounds of bone, muscle, and the fury of a man who had never lost and could not accept that he was about to.

Mei planted her feet and took it.

The impact drove her back one step. Two. Her feet cut grooves in the loose rock. But she did not fall. The warm was there — everywhere, in every part of her, dense, steady, and absolute. His hands found her shoulders. His fingers dug in. He pushed. Mei did not move.

She looked up at his face. It was close — close enough to see the pores in his skin, the grey in his beard, the veins in his eyes. Close enough to see the moment when fury turned to something else.

She hit him.

One blow. Open palm. Center of the chest. The warm was behind it — not projected, not a wave. Concentrated. A single point of force, delivered from a body that had spent five summers learning exactly where to strike.

He went backward. Not flying — stumbling. Three steps, four, his feet searching for ground that kept moving away from him. He caught himself. Stayed upright. His hand went to his chest where she had hit him, and his face was the face of a man who has just learned that the rules he lived by no longer apply.

He came again. Slower this time. A hook — wide, heavy, the kind of blow that crushed skulls when it landed. Mei ducked it. His fist went over her head and she was inside his reach, close enough to smell him — sweat, smoke, and the particular, sour smell of fear in a man who does not know how to be afraid.

She hit him again. Ribs. Left side. She felt them give under her palm — not breaking, bending, the warm sharpening the impact into something precise and terrible. He grunted. Swung again — an elbow, desperate. It caught her temple. Stars. The world tilted. But the warm held. The stars faded. Her feet stayed.

She hit him in the knee. He dropped to one leg. Hit him in the jaw. His head snapped sideways. Hit him in the chest again — the same spot, the bruised spot — and this time something broke.

He went down.

Not slowly. Not dramatically. He went down the way a tree goes down — one moment standing, the next not. His knees hit the earth, then his hands, then his face. He lay in the dust of the ridge with his chest making sounds that were not breathing and his fingers clawing at the ground.

Mei stood over him.

Her hands were at her sides. They were not shaking. The warm pulsed in her chest — even, steady, the same rhythm it had always had. As though this was nothing. As though this was what she had always been meant to do.

The big man rolled onto his back. He looked up at her. His eyes were clear — pain had not clouded them. He saw her. He saw what she was.

Mei picked up a stone. A good stone — flat, heavy, with an edge.

She did not speak. There were no words for this. There had never been words for this — not when he had pointed at her across the fire, not when the People had nodded, not when she had walked into the trees. This was not something that could be named.

She knelt beside him. Pressed the stone's edge against his throat.

He looked at her. Not with fear — with understanding. The understanding of a man who had lived by strength his whole life and was now being shown what strength actually looked like.

She pressed down.

It was quick. She had killed many things. She had learned that quickness was the only mercy available. She gave it.

The big man died looking at the sky. His warmth faded in Mei's chest — bright, bright, dim, gone. The loudest signal she had ever felt, silenced.

Mei stood.

She looked at the People.

They were arranged behind where he had stood — women, children, the remaining hunters. All of them staring. All of them still. Not one of them moved. Not one of them spoke. The silence was the same silence that had followed the broken spear — the silence of people watching the world change shape.

One by one, the hunters put down their weapons. Not thrown down — placed. Carefully. On the ground. Hands empty. Heads down. The gesture was older than language: I yield.

The women did not move. The children did not move. Koh did not move. Mei's mother did not move.

Mei stood at the top of the ridge with blood on her hands, the warm in her chest, and the body of the biggest man in the world at her feet. The People looked at her. She looked at the People. Nobody said anything, because there was nothing left to say.
