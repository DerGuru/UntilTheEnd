The camp was set in a clearing between two ridges, the kind of place the People liked — sheltered from the wind, close to water, with high ground on two sides for watching. Most of the adults were near the fires. Children played at the edges. The air was warm, heavy with the smell of dust and cooking meat.

Mei was carrying water.

She came around the side of a rock and saw them.

The big man had Koh by the arm. Not her wrist this time — her upper arm, where the flesh was thin over the bone. His fingers were deep in it. Koh's face was white. She was pulling back. Twisting. Her feet scraped in the dirt. She made a sound — not a word, not a scream. A sound like an animal makes when it steps in a trap.

Two women stood nearby. They were not looking.

A hunter sat sharpening a blade, ten paces away. He was not looking.

The big man said something to Koh — low, close to her face. Mei could not hear the words. She did not need to.

Koh's eyes found Mei.

Everything after that was fast.

Mei did not decide. There was no thought, no plan, no moment where she considered what she was doing or what would happen next. There was only the warm — the warm in her chest, which had been sitting heavy and tight since the river, since Koh's voice, since the cold fingers, the turning-away gesture — and it moved.

Not like the careful movements at night, the slow pushing from chest to hand, hand to chest. Not like the trickle she could direct and control.

This was everything at once.

The warm detonated. It went out from her chest in all directions, then gathered — pulled together, focused, aimed — and hit the big man like a boulder rolling downhill.

He flew.

Not far. Three body-lengths, maybe four. But he left the ground. He left Koh's arm. The air made a sound — a crack, like a branch breaking — and he landed on his back in the dust with a force that drove the breath from his body.

Silence.

The water skin fell from Mei's hands. She stood with her arms out, fingers spread, shaking. The warm was gone from her chest — emptied, drained, like a vessel tipped and poured out. Her legs trembled. Her ears rang.

Koh stumbled backward, free. She clutched her arm where his fingers had been and stared at Mei with her mouth open.

The big man lay on his back. For five heartbeats he did not move. Then he rolled onto his side, coughing, and pushed himself up to his hands and knees. Dust clung to his hair and his back. He looked at Mei.

Not with fear. Not with pain. With something slower, more dangerous — the look of a man who has been surprised and does not intend to be surprised again.

Mei's hands were still shaking. The warm crept back into her chest — thin, weak, a trickle where a flood had been. She felt hollow. Her vision blurred at the edges. She tasted copper.

Around the clearing, every face was turned toward her.

The women who had not been looking were looking now. The hunter with the blade had stopped sharpening. Children stood with their mouths open. The old man, at the far edge of the camp, had risen to his feet.

Nobody spoke.

The big man stood. He brushed the dust from his arms with slow, deliberate movements. He looked at Mei the way you look at a tool that has just cut you — not the tool's fault, but something to be dealt with.

Then he turned and walked away.

Koh grabbed Mei's arm — the same arm, the same gesture, but gentle.

"What did you do?"

"I don't know."

"Mei."

"I don't know."

They stood together in the clearing. The People stared. The silence was the loudest sound Mei had ever heard.
