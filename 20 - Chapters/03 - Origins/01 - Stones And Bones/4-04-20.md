She pushed the warm to her hands the way she always had — the same movement she had practiced in secret while the People slept. But there was more now. So much more. The warm rushed down her arms and her hands *ached* — a deep throb in the bones, in the thin spaces between the bones. She pulled it back. Her fingers tingled, numb at the tips.

Too much. 

She tried again. Less. Slower. A thin thread instead of everything at once. The ache came anyway — duller, but there. She held it. Breathed through it. The way she breathed through cold water, through hunger, through the sharp mornings outside. After a while the ache faded. The paths stretched. Or she learned how much they could hold. She was not sure which.

Days of this. Pushing a little more each time — hands, feet, shoulders, spine. Finding the edge where the ache turned sharp and stopping just before. The pain reduced — not quickly, not easily, but what had hurt yesterday sat comfortably today. What was too much this morning was bearable by evening.

Slow work. The kind that made her want to scream and go outside and hit something.

She went outside.

The warm was still in her hands — she had been practicing again and forgot to pull it back. She pressed her palm against the cliff face to steady herself on the slope and noticed: her skin felt different. Harder. Denser. Like the sole of her foot after a full summer of walking on stones — that toughness that came from use, from the ground pressing back until the skin said *fine* and stopped being soft.

But she had not been walking on her hands.

She pressed harder. The stone was rough, sharp-edged. It should have scraped her palm. It did not. She dragged her hand across the rock. Nothing. No blood. No sting.

She looked at her hand. The same hand. Brown. Scarred. Small. But full of the warm.

She made a fist.

A stone beside the cave entrance was flat and grey. She had walked past it a thousand times. She hit it. Gently — a tap, knuckles against rock. Nothing. No pain. She hit harder. Still nothing in her hand. She pulled back and swung with everything she had.

Her fist struck the stone and stopped dead. Her hand felt nothing — no ache, no bruise, nothing broken. But her elbow screamed. Her shoulder wrenched sideways, a hot tearing feeling that made her gasp and grab her arm. She stumbled back, cradling it, teeth clenched. The joint was wrong — not broken, but *wrong*, loose in a way it should not be. She rolled her shoulder, grinding through the pain until something shifted back into place with a dull pop.

Her hand was fine. Her arm was not.

She went back inside and sat against the wall, cradling her shoulder, waiting for the warm to mend what she had broken. It gathered at the joint the way it always did — slow, patient, pulling the torn edges together. She had nothing to do but sit and wait.

She thought about the warm in her hands. How it had made them hard. What would it do elsewhere?

She pushed the warm to her eyes.

The cave detonated with light. Not light. Sharper than light. Everything blazed with a brightness that had no color, no source. The walls. The stone. The air itself — *glowing*, faintly, thin threads drifting through the dark like smoke she had never seen before. The warm. She was seeing the warm. It hung in the air in lazy ribbons, pooled in the cracks of the floor, streamed from deeper in the mountain in slow rivers.

She slammed it back. Darkness. Spots. Her eyes watered and she pressed her palms against them until the afterimage faded.

Too much. Way too much.

She tried again with less. The tiniest push — barely a whisper of warmth behind her eyes. The dark thinned. Shapes emerged. The walls. The ceiling. The basin where she drank. All visible in a way that owed nothing to the sun. And there — faint, ghostly — the threads. The warm made visible. Moving through the cave like breath.

She sat and watched for a long time. 

She pushed it to her ears. Carefully this time — a thread, barely anything. She had learned.

The dripping sharpened. Each drop distinct: forming, stretching, breaking free, falling, striking. She could hear the wind outside. Animals moving on the slope. Her own blood, her own breath.

More. A little more.

The dripping grew louder. The wind became a roar. Her own heartbeat thumped in her skull. She pulled back — not all the way, just enough. Found the edge. Held it there.

She could hear everything. The scratch of insects in crevices. Her own bones creaking when she shifted. The mountain itself humming, deep below.

She pushed further — too far. The dripping exploded into thunder. Each drop a crack that split her head open like a herd of mammoths stampeding through her skull. She slapped her hands over her ears but it made no difference. She yanked the warm back and crouched in the ringing silence, heart slamming against her ribs.

She waited the rest of the day until her shoulder stopped aching. The warm helped her healing. The next morning she tried again.

This time she pushed the warm into her whole arm — hand, wrist, forearm, elbow, shoulder. All of it full, all of it. Dense. She swung at the stone.

The impact went through her arm and hit her chest. Her body shuddered. Her head snapped forward. Her feet left the ground and she flew backward — sideways along the ledge — and landed flat on her back in the dirt. One arm dangling over the edge.

She lay there. Staring up at the sky. Then turned her head right — the slope dropped away, steep and far, loose scree and nothing to catch. One more step of force and she would have gone over.

The stone was unchanged. Not a scratch.

She grinned.

Her back ached, dotted with small bruises where stones had dug in. So she let it heal. Then she stood before the stone once more.

This time, the warm flooded everything. Arms, shoulders, chest, stomach, back, legs — all the way down to her feet digging into the earth. She filled herself until she felt full, dense and taut. Then she swung.

The impact jolted through her — not sideways this time, not backward. Straight down, into her legs, into the ground. She rocked back a half-step. Stayed standing.

And the stone — a thin line. A crack. Hair-thin, running from where her fist had struck to the edge of the flat face.

She touched it. Her finger fit inside. Barely. But it was there. She had put it there.

The grin split her face so wide it hurt.
