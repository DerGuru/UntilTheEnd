The warm had been steady all morning — the low, background hum she carried everywhere now, the pulse beneath the pulse that told her where water moved, animals breathed, and the earth was alive. She was used to it. It was the sound of the world, and she had learned to listen to it as she listened to wind or water: constantly, without effort.

Then it changed.

The hum thickened. Grew denser. Richer. As if the thin note she had been hearing all along was a single voice, and now a choir had joined. The warm in her chest swelled — not pushing outward, not the alarm of danger. Just more. Just fuller. As though the mountain itself was breathing, and she had walked into the path of its breath.

Mei stopped. She was on a slope of loose scree, grey and rust-colored, with scrub bushes growing in the cracks between boulders. Above her, the rock face rose steep and cracked. Nothing remarkable. No sign. No path.

But the warm said: here.

She climbed. Followed the feeling. It led her sideways across the face of the slope, then up through a crack between two boulders that were taller than she was, then over a ledge slick with lichen. Her fingers scraped rock. Her toes found holds. The warm grew stronger with every body-length.

She found the opening behind a curtain of dead vines. Not large — barely wide enough for her shoulders, a dark slit in the grey rock. Cool air breathed out of it, carrying a smell she had never encountered: mineral, water, and something else, something that had no name, something that tasted of the warm the way sunlight tastes of yellow.

She squeezed through.

Inside, the darkness was total for three breaths. Then her eyes began to adjust. She saw that the passage opened into a space — neither vast nor small. A room in the rock. The ceiling was low enough that she could have touched it if she stood on her toes and reached. The floor was smooth — worn by water that no longer flowed here, or by something else entirely. Against the far wall, moisture seeped through cracks in the ceiling and collected in a shallow basin in the stone. A single drop fell as she watched. Then another. Steady, even, the same interval each time — the only sound in the dark besides her own breathing.

The warm was everywhere. It filled the cave the way water fills a bowl — completely, evenly, without gaps or thin spots. It hummed against her skin. It hummed inside her chest. It hummed in her bones, her teeth, the backs of her eyes. Every part of her was immersed in it, soaked in it, and for the first time since she could remember, the warm inside her and the warm outside her were the same thing.

Mei stood in the center of the cave and breathed.

The air was warm. Not fire-warm — deeper. The warmth of stone that has been heated from within, the warmth of a living thing's belly, the warmth of the earth itself, pulsing slow, vast, and patient. She could feel it in the soles of her feet, rising through the smooth rock floor. She could feel it on her face, gentle as breath.

She sat down.

The stone was smooth, warm, and dry. She placed her palms flat on the floor. The warm pushed up through them — dense, steady, endless. Not like the thin trickle she pulled from the ground when she searched for water. Not like the scattered signals of animals and plants. This was a source. A place where the warm collected, concentrated, waited.

For what?

Mei did not ask the question. She was eleven summers old, very tired, very alone. The cave was warm, safe, and dry. The warm was there. It did not judge her or send her away or look at her with fear in its eyes.

She lay down on the stone floor and curled up. The warm wrapped around her the way her mother's arm used to — not holding, not squeezing. Just there. Present. Complete.

She slept.

The dripping water entered her dreams.

The steady, slow rhythm it had always been. It quickened. Becoming faster. Faster than her heartbeat. Faster than sticks on wood when the People sang. The drops blurred together, became a stream, a rush, the roar of water falling from stone — a roar that filled the cave and filled her dream.

Then it slowed.

The roar thinned. Became a stream again. Became drops again. One. Two. Three. The old rhythm, patient, unchanged. As though nothing had happened. As though the river had never been.

She was still in the cave. The same cave. But a fire burned in the center now. Small, contained, the coals orange and breathing.

A woman sat beside it.

She was oddly familiar, but old. Older than anyone Mei had ever seen — older than Omu. Her hair was grey, her face lined, the skin folded at the corners of her eyes and mouth in the patterns that time carves when it carves for a very long time. She sat cross-legged with her hands on her knees. Still. Not sleeping — something else. Something between sleeping and being awake, something Mei had no word for.

She did not see Mei. Her eyes were open but they looked through her, past her, at something that was outside the cave. Mei was not there for her.

Beside her lay a fox. Small, red-furred, with the narrow face and pointed ears that Mei had seen in glimpses at the edges of clearings — always running, always gone before you could look properly. She knew the animal. But she had never seen one lie still beside a person. Never seen one choose to stay.

This one lay with its chin on its paws, pressed against the woman's thigh. Its orange eyes found Mei. Its ears came forward — alert, focused, interested. It saw her. It saw her in a way the woman could not.

Then clouds came. White, slow, rolling in from the cave's mouth, across the floor like breath on a cold morning. They filled the space between Mei and the fire. The woman faded. The fox faded — the last thing visible was the orange of its eyes, watching her through the white, and then gone.

The fire remained. A glow in the mist, dim, warm, steady. Then that too faded.

The drops fell. One. Two. Three.

When she woke, she did not know how long she had been under. The cave was the same — dark, warm, the slow pulse of the warm rising through the stone. But something in her had changed. She felt full. Not food-full — the hunger was still there, a hollow ache below her ribs. But the warm in her chest was bigger than it had ever been. Brighter. Denser. As though the cave had been pouring into her while she slept, filling a vessel she hadn't known was empty.

She sat up. Pressed her hand to her chest. The warm responded immediatley, strong and eager. When she pushed it to her fingers, it went without resistance. When she pulled it back, it came. Fast. Clean. Like water in a channel that has been carved smooth by years of flow.

Outside, the wind blew. She could hear it through the entrance — distant, cold, belonging to a world that was not this one. Not yet.

Mei looked at the cave. At the walls, the basin, the smooth floor.

She was staying.
