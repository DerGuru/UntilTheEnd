The fire burned.

Mei had built it herself—the way she built fires on the mountain, alone, with dry wood and friction and the patience of someone who had done it a thousand times. The People had watched her do it. They watched everything she did now, the way you watch a storm: constantly, carefully, from a distance that feels safe but might not be.

She sat beside the fire in the place that had been his place. The best spot—where the smoke did not reach and the heat was strongest. Nobody had told her to sit there. Nobody needed to.

The camp was quiet. Not the ordinary quiet of evening—the deeper quiet of people who are holding their breath, waiting to see what the world will do next. Women sat with their children. The hunters sat apart, tending their wounds. Nobody spoke above a murmur.

Mei looked at the fire. The flames were orange and yellow and the wood popped and sparks rose and died, and the smell was the same as it had always been—smoke and fat and the sweet undertone of sap. The same fire. The same smell. As though nothing had changed.

Everything had changed.

She felt them—all of them. The warm mapped the camp the way it mapped the mountain: every body a point of heat, every heartbeat a pulse. She knew where each person sat. She knew who was afraid and who was numb and who was already adjusting, the way people always adjust, to the new shape of power. She knew which children were asleep and which were pretending.

Koh was sitting near the women. Mei felt her warmth—familiar, specific, the particular signature she would have known in the dark, in a crowd, in a storm. Koh was looking at her. Mei could feel the weight of that gaze without turning her head.

She did not turn.

Her mother was at the edge of the camp. The same place she had always been—the periphery, the margin. Her warmth was thin. Faded. The warmth of a body that had been tired for a long time. She was looking at Mei too.

Mei did not turn.

The warm in her chest was vast. It filled her the way the cave's warm had filled her—completely, evenly, without gaps. But this was different. The cave's warm had been external—a place she went to be full. This was internal. This was her. The warm and the body and the thing beneath the warm—the not now, the refusal, the continuation—were no longer separate things. They were one thing. She was one thing.

She looked at her hands. Blood on them. Not hers. The firelight made the blood look black.

She did not clean them.

The People breathed. The fire cracked. An insect hummed somewhere in the dark. The river, distant, whispered. The sounds of the world, continuing.

Mei looked at the faces around the fire. She saw them the way she saw everything now—clearly, completely, without the softening that distance or sentiment or hope provided. These were the people who had cast her out. These were the people who had nodded when the big man pointed. These were the people who had watched an eleven-year-old girl walk into the trees and had gone back to their fires and eaten their food and slept their sleep.

These were her people now.

Not because they chose her. Not because she earned them. Not because she belonged.

Because she was the strongest thing here, and strength was the only language this world spoke, and she had learned to speak it fluently.

The place where the old man had sat was empty. Mei looked at it for a long time. A gap in the circle, shaped like a man who had once stood up for her when standing up was useless. She would not fill that gap. She would leave it empty. Let it stay the shape it was.

A child cried somewhere at the edge of the camp—a small, thin sound, quickly hushed. The sound did something to the warm in Mei's chest. Not a surge, not a pulse. A settling. A deepening. As though the warm recognized the sound and answered it with something that was not warmth and not cold but heavier than both.

Mei stood.

The People flinched.

She stood still for a moment, letting them see her. Tall. Lean. Scarred at the arms and legs and chest. Sixteen summers, with the eyes of something older. The fire at her back. The dark around her. Blood on her hands.

She had wanted to belong. She had spent five summers learning that she couldn't—that the shape of her did not fit the shape of them, that the warm made her wrong and the wrong made her alone, and alone was the only place the world had left for her.

Now she stood above them, and they were hers, and the wanting was gone, replaced by something she did not have a name for. Something heavy. Something that sat in her chest beside the warm and weighed as much as the mountain.

Responsibility, someone might have called it, if anyone here had the word. Or burden. Or simply: what comes next.

The fire burned lower. The faces around it grew dim. One by one, the People lay down—carefully, slowly, the way you lie down when you are not sure whether sleep is safe. Mothers curled around children. Hunters pressed their backs against rocks. Eyes closed. Breath slowed.

Mei did not lie down.

She sat by the fire—their fire, her fire—and she watched the dark, and the warm pulsed in her chest, and the night was long, and the People slept, and she kept them safe because that was what the strongest thing did.

The stars moved. The fire sank to coals. The coals breathed orange and black.

Mei sat with her hands in her lap—blood drying, warm pulsing, eyes open—and she waited for morning, because morning would come, because it always came, because continuing was the only thing she knew how to do.

And somewhere, in the deep place beneath the warm, beneath the bone, beneath all of it, the thing that would one day be called a Dao settled into its permanent shape, and it was not a word and not a thought and not a decision.

It was simply this: *not yet.*

Not yet done. Not yet finished. Not yet over.

The fire breathed. The people breathed. Mei breathed.

The world continued.
