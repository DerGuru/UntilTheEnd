The cave was dark and cold. The fire had died and she had no way to relight it — her flint was blades now, spearheads, scrapers. Tools she had spent days shaping. She was not going to strike them against stone until they chipped. She ate dried meat in the dark, wrapped herself in skins, and lay on the stone floor with nothing but the warm in her chest for company.

It was enough. It had to be enough.

The memory came that night without invitation. Dak, the big man. His hand on Koh's arm. The terror in Koh's eyes. And the blast. The warm erupting out of her, slamming into him, sending him tumbling like a leaf in a storm.

She had pushed the warm outward. Into the air. Into him.

She went out and faced the cracked stone. Mei drew the warm into her hand. Imagined it leaving — shooting from her palm like a thrown spear, striking the stone. Pushed.

Nothing.

The warm pooled in her palm. Dense, hot, waiting. But it would not leave. She pushed harder. It pressed against the inside of her skin like something trapped. She pushed until her hand ached. Nothing left. It stayed inside her, stubborn, refusing.

She tried again. And again. 

Nothing.

She went inside and slept.

For days she tried. Sitting before the stone, hand outstretched, pushing with everything she had. The warm grew hot in her palm. Her skin reddened. But nothing left. Nothing touched the stone.

She screamed. Slammed her hand against the ground — but still nothing. Nothing. Nothing. Nothing. The warm would not leave her body.

She kicked the wall of the entrance in frustration and stamped her foot against the floor.

*Crack.*

She looked down. A thin line in the stone — running from under her heel in two directions. She had not pushed the warm to her foot. She had not thought about directing it at all. She had just been *angry*.

She tilted her head.

She turned back to the cracked stone. Drew the warm into her hand. Thought about Dak. About his weight. About the fear and the rage and the helplessness. The warm grew hot — hotter than before, hotter than practice had ever made it. She imagined it hitting the stone the way it had hit Dak. Not a technique. Just — *go*.

A pulse. Small. Weak. But something pushed *outward* from her palm through the air between her and the stone. She felt it leave. A breath of heat that crossed the gap and touched the rock.

She sat back. Breathing. Thinking. 

Days followed. She practiced anger — real anger, not pretend. Thought about Koh's. About Dak pointing at her at the fire, talking to the others. About the ibex, ripping her skin with its horns. About the bear, ramming her and eating *her* kill. She let the rage build until the warm burned in her palm. Then aimed it.

The pulses grew stronger — from a breath to a gust to a shove she could feel in the air. And then one morning she aimed at the stone and let it all go — all the fury, all the frustration, all the moons of trying — and the warm left her hand like a fist.

The stone split. A piece the size of her head broke free and tumbled to the ground.

She stood there. Hand still raised. Palm still hot. The stone broken before her.

The grin came back. Wider than before. Wide enough to ache.

Her palm was still hot. Not warm — *hot*. Like holding it over a fire. The warm radiated from her skin in waves she could almost see.

Fire.

She gathered dry grass. A stick. The shredded bark she used for kindling. Piled it the way she had watched the People do it. She drew the warm back into her hand and let the anger build — not the rage this time. Frustration. Impatience. The low burn of wanting something and being denied.

She pressed her palm against the kindling. Too cold. She pushed harder, drew more warm into her hand. She felt it build — hotter, hotter. The bark darkened under her palm. Curled. Began to smoke.

She pulled her hand away. The smoke thinned. Died.

Again. More this time. Hotter. She held her palm against the bark and pushed until sweat ran down her arm and the heat in her hand was nearly painful. The bark browned. Smoked. A thin orange line appeared at the edge. Glowing and spreading.

She blew on it. Gentle. The way she had seen the women blow on embers.

Flame lit. Small, fragile, licking up through the bark and catching the dry grass. She fed it gently, carefully, the way she remembered, until it crackled and grew and lit the cave wall with dancing orange.

She sat by the fire — *her* fire, made with nothing but the warm and her own hands — and watched it burn.
