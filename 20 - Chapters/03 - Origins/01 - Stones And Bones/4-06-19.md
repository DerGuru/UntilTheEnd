The silence drove her down.

Not all at once—not a single morning where she woke and decided. It built. Day by day, night by night. The cave was safe. The cave was warm. The cave was hers in a way that nothing had ever been hers. But the cave did not breathe beside her. The cave did not laugh. The cave did not bump her shoulder or say tomorrow? The cave was a place, not a person, and Mei was twelve summers old and still, underneath everything she was becoming, a child who needed the sound of someone else's voice.

She had been alone for—how long? She counted by moons. Three? Four? The ibex meat was gone. She had hunted again—smaller things, rabbits, birds, once a marmot that she caught by hand when the warm told her exactly where it was hiding. She knew the mountain now. Its ridges and valleys and the places where water gathered and the places where predators moved. The warm mapped it for her, a living chart of heat and life and emptiness.

But the map had no people in it.

One morning she woke and the silence was too much. She packed what she could carry—dried meat, a skin of water, the good spear she had spent days making. She looked at the cave. The warm pulsed there—steady, patient, the heartbeat of the mountain.

She would come back. She knew this the way she knew how to breathe. But she needed something the cave could not give her.

She climbed down.

The descent took two days. The air warmed. The trees returned—first the twisted, wind-bent kind, then broader, softer. Birds called. Insects hummed. The world below the mountain was loud in a way she had forgotten, full of small lives and moving things.

On the third day, she found the river.

It was not the same river—or maybe it was, further along, wider. It didn't matter. What mattered was the smoke.

A group. Fifteen, maybe twenty. Camped at a bend where the river widened into a shallow pool. They had fires and shelters made of branches and skins, and children played at the water's edge, and the sound of voices carried across the flat ground, and Mei stood at the tree line and listened to it the way a starving person smells cooking food.

She approached differently this time. Not desperate. Not small. She walked upright, spear held low, not threatening but present. She was taller now—the moons alone had stretched her, thinned her face, hardened the lines of her body. She did not look like a lost child anymore. She did not know what she looked like.

The group watched her come. A man stood up—one of the younger ones, alert, a spear in his hand. But he did not raise it. He watched her the way you watch a wolf that walks close but does not bare its teeth: wary, curious, waiting.

Mei stopped at the edge of their camp. She set her spear on the ground—not surrendering it, just making it horizontal. She opened her hands. Showed the dried meat. An offering.

The man looked at the meat. Looked at her. Looked at someone behind him—an older woman, thick-set, with grey streaking her hair. A decision-maker.

The woman came forward. She looked at Mei for a long time. Then she took the meat, sniffed it, broke a piece off and chewed.

She pointed at a spot near the edge of the camp. The spot said: you can sit here. Not: you belong.

Mei sat.

Days passed. Mei worked. She gathered—berries, roots, the things women gathered. She carried water. She helped with the shelters when the group moved camp. She made herself useful in the quiet, invisible way she had learned from watching her own People: do more than your share, ask for nothing, occupy the smallest space possible.

The group tolerated her. The children played near her. One of the women shared food. The decision-maker watched from a distance with an expression that was not hostile and not warm—evaluating. Measuring. Deciding.

For five days, it was almost enough.

On the sixth morning, a snake came to the camp.

Mei felt it before it reached the sleeping area—a thin, sharp line of warm, fast-moving, close to the ground. She was already awake, sitting at the edge of the camp in the pre-dawn grey, and she felt it and she stood and she said:

"Snake."

Her voice was too loud. The sound cut the morning silence like a thrown stone. Heads turned. Bodies stiffened.

The snake emerged from the grass near a sleeping child—long, patterned, its head raised. The child was still asleep. The mother was asleep.

Mei moved. Fast—faster than anyone expected, faster than she should have been able to move. She crossed the camp in six strides, scooped the child off the ground, and stepped back. The snake struck at empty air.

The camp erupted. Someone killed the snake—a spear, a rock, she didn't see. The mother grabbed the child from Mei's arms. There was screaming, chaos, the particular panic of people who have woken to danger.

Then it settled. The snake was dead. The child was fine. And everyone was looking at Mei.

The decision-maker looked longest.

How had she known? How had she moved that fast? How had she been awake, in the right place, at the right moment? The questions were not spoken, but they hung in the air like the smell of the dead snake.

Mei felt it coming—the same shift, the same tightening. The same invisible wall going up between her and them. She had seen it before. She would see it again.

She did not wait for the pointing.

She picked up her spear. Took her water skin. Looked at the group one last time—the children, the fires, the woman who had shared food with her for five days.

She turned and walked toward the mountains.

The climb back was familiar. The trees thinned. The air cooled. The rock rose. And the warm in her chest, which had dimmed during the days below—stretched thin by the noise and the nearness of so many humming lives—grew denser with each step toward the cave.

She squeezed through the entrance. The warm of the cave met her—full, steady, unchanged. Waiting.

She sat on the smooth stone. Pressed her palms to the floor. The warm flooded in—fast, eager, as though it had missed her.

Her eyes burned. She pressed her palms harder against the stone and breathed through it.

The cave was warm. The cave was safe. The cave did not care what she was.

It was not enough. But it was what she had.
