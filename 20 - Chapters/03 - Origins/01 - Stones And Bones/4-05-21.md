The ibex stood on a ledge above her, thirty paces up, perfectly still. Mei crouched behind a boulder and watched it. Her breath made small clouds in the cold air. The animal was bigger than she had expected — heavy, muscled, with curved horns that swept back from its skull like weapons made of bone. Its coat was thick and dark, winter-ready.

She could feel its warmth. Not the diffuse hum of small creatures — mice, insects, birds — but a solid, bright presence, like a coal. Alive. Alert. Its head turned, and she felt the attention move with it — a shift in the warm's direction, as though the animal's awareness had weight.

She had a spear. A straight branch, fire-hardened at the tip as she'd seen the hunters do, the point blackened and hard. She had made it in the cave entrance, working by feel, scraping the wood with a sharp stone until the end was narrow and stiff. It was too light. Too short. But it was what she had.

The ibex could not be charged. It was higher, faster, surer on the rock. If she came at it directly, it would be gone before she cleared three steps. She had watched the hunters enough to know: you do not chase what is faster than you. You make it come to you, or you take away its choices.

She looked at the terrain. The ledge the ibex stood on narrowed to the right — a shelf that thinned to nothing against a sheer drop. To the left, the slope opened into scrub. Below, the boulder field where she crouched. Above, smooth rock with no holds.

If the ibex went right, it would run out of ledge. If it went left, it would come down the slope — toward her.

Mei circled. Slowly. Staying low, using the boulders as cover. She pushed warm to her feet — a way of making them lighter, quieter, more precise on the rock. Her feet found holds without searching. Her weight shifted without sound.

She came around to the left side, below the slope. Now she was between the ibex and the open ground. If it ran left, it ran toward her.

She picked up a stone. Threw it at the ledge above the ibex — not at the animal, above it. The stone cracked against rock. The ibex startled and bolted right.

The ledge narrowed. The ibex slowed, realized, turned. Came down the slope — fast, hooves sure, heading for the open ground.

Heading for Mei.

She braced. Pushed warm to her arms, to her hands, to the place where the spear met her palm. The warm obeyed. It flickered under the pressure of her heartbeat, the adrenaline, the fear. Calm, she told herself. The warm works best when you're calm.

But she was not calm.

The ibex came down the slope in a shower of loose stone. It saw her at the last moment — a shape rising from behind a boulder, arm drawn back. Its eyes went wide. It tried to turn.

Mei threw.

The spear was too light. She knew it the moment it left her hand. The tip hit the ibex's shoulder, not the neck. It did not go deep — the animal screamed — a sound she had never heard and did not want to hear again. It stumbled but did not fall. It twisted, hooves scraping, lunged past her. One horn caught her arm — a line of fire from elbow to wrist — and then it was past, bleeding from the shoulder, running for the open ground.

Mei did not think. She ran.

The warm flooded her legs on instinct. The way it had come when Dak was grabbing Koh. Her feet hit the ground and the ground threw her forward. The ibex was fast — sure-footed, powerful, built for this rock — but Mei was faster. She did not know she could be this fast. The gap closed in heartbeats. Three paces. Two. One.

She grabbed a stone from the ground — jagged, heavy enough to matter —  and drew the warm into her arm. She thought about the cracked stone in the cave. About the split. About anger.

She threw.

Not at the ibex's body — at its head. The stone left her hand with a force that surprised her, a snap of heat from her palm. It struck behind the ear with a sound like a branch breaking. The ibex's legs folded mid-stride. It hit the ground rolling, tumbled twice, and lay still.

It was not dead yet. Its legs kicked. Its eyes rolled. Blood ran from the shoulder wound and the dent behind its ear, dark on the dark rock. Mei knelt beside it. Her hands were shaking. The warm in her chest was thin — drained by the throwing, the running, the pushing to her limbs. She felt hollow, sick. Her arm was bleeding where the horn had caught her.

She needed to end it. Looked for a sharp rock — something with an edge. Nothing. Just smooth round gravel, dirt. The ibex's legs kicked again, weaker. Its breath came in wet rattles.

She pushed the warm into her fist. Made it hard. Made it heavy. 

"Sorry," she said. It was the first word she had spoken in months. Her voice sounded strange — thin, rusty, as though the air had forgotten how to carry it.

Then she brought her fist down on the animal's skull.

The bone gave. The kicking stopped.

She crawled three steps away and vomited. Her stomach heaved until nothing was left, then heaved again. Her hand was hot and wet — not blood, or not only blood. She wiped it on the ground and retched once more.

When it was over she sat in the dirt, shaking, empty, staring at the animal. At what she had done with her hands.

She dragged it back to the cave in pieces. It took the rest of the day. Her arm throbbed where the horn had cut her, but the warm was already there — gathering at the wound, mending, as it did. By the time she had the meat inside and a fire built at the entrance, the cut was a pink line. By morning, it would be a scar.

She roasted meat over the fire. The fat dripped and hissed on the coals. The smell filled the cave — rich, heavy, the first real food in since she entered the cave for the first time. She ate with her hands. The meat was tough, gamey, and perfect.

The last sunlight faded. The fire painted the cave walls in orange and shadow.

Mei sat with her back against warm stone, full for the first time since her group sent her away, thinking about the ibex — how it had moved, where it had been vulnerable, what she would do differently next time. Because there would be a next time. A time after that. Each time she would be better, because the mountain did not forgive mistakes, and she intended to stop making them.

She wiped her hands onto her thighs and curled up to sleep.
