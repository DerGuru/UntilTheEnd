The fire burned. The People had watched her light it. They watched everything she did now, the way you watch a storm. Constantly, carefully, from a distance that feels safe but might not be.

She sat beside the fire in the place that had been his place. The best spot — where the smoke did not reach and the heat was strongest. Nobody had told her to sit there. Nobody needed to. She took it.

The camp was quiet. The deeper quiet of people who are holding their breath, waiting to see what the world will do next. Women sat with their children. The hunters sat apart, tending their wounds. Nobody spoke above a murmur.

Mei looked at the fire. The flames were orange and yellow. The wood popped. Sparks rose and died. The smell was the same as it had always been — smoke, fat, and the sweet undertone of sap. The same fire. The same smell. As though nothing had changed.

Everything had changed.

She felt them — all of them. The warm mapped the camp as it mapped the mountain. Every body a point of heat, every heartbeat a pulse. She knew where each person sat. She knew who was afraid, who was numb, who was already adjusting to the new shape of power. She knew which children were asleep and which were pretending.

Koh was sitting near the women. Mei felt her warmth — familiar, a signature she would have known in the dark, in a crowd, in a storm. Koh was looking at her. Mei could feel the weight of that gaze without turning her head.

She didn't turn.

Her mother was at the edge of the camp. The same place she had always been — the periphery, the margin. Her warmth was thin. Faded. The warmth of a body that had been tired for a long time. She was looking at Mei too.

Mei didn't look back.

The warm in her chest was vast. It filled her completely, evenly, without gaps. The warm, the body, and the thing beneath the warm were no longer separate things. She had become one thing.

She looked at her still bloody hands. The firelight made the blood look black.

She hadn't cleaned them.

The People breathed. The fire cracked. An insect hummed somewhere in the dark. The river, distant, whispered. The sounds of the world, continuing.

Mei looked at the faces around the fire. She saw them as she saw everything now, without the softening that distance or sentiment or hope provided. These were the people who had cast her out. These were the people who had nodded when the big man pointed. These were the people who had watched an eleven-year-old girl walk into the trees and had gone back to their fires. Eaten their food. Slept their sleep.

They were her people now.

She was the strongest thing here and strength was the only language this world spoke. She had learned to speak it fluently.

The place where the old man had sat was empty. Mei looked at it for a long time. A gap in the circle, shaped like a man who had once stood up for her when standing up was useless. She would not fill that gap. She would leave it empty. Let it stay the shape it was.

A child cried somewhere at the edge of the camp — a small, thin sound, quickly hushed. The sound did something to the warm in Mei's chest. Not a surge or a pulse. A settling. A deepening. As though the warm recognized the sound and answered it with something that was not warmth and not cold but heavier than both.

Mei stood.

The People flinched.

She walked past them. Through the camp, past the fires, past the shelters, into the dark beyond the light's edge. Nobody breathed. She felt their eyes on her back — twenty pairs, waiting for the thing that would happen next.

Nothing happened. She walked the perimeter. Slowly. The warm reaching outward, reading the dark the way it read everything — for warmth, for movement, for threat. The night was empty. No predators. No danger. Just the cold air, the stars, and the quiet sounds of a world that did not care what had happened here today.

She came back and sat back down at the fire — in his place, the best place — and put another branch on the coals.

The People watched her do it. Then, one by one, they lay down — carefully, slowly, the way you lie down when you are not sure whether sleep is safe. Mothers curled around children. Hunters pressed their backs against rocks. Eyes closed. Breath slowed.

Mei did not lie down.

She sat by the fire — their fire, her fire — and she watched the dark, and the warm pulsed in her chest. The night was long, the People slept. She kept them safe because that was what the strongest thing did.

The stars moved. The fire sank to coals. The coals breathed orange and black.

Mei sat with her hands in her lap — blood drying, warm pulsing, eyes open — and she waited for morning, because morning would come. It always came. And continuing was the only thing she knew how to do.

And somewhere, in the deep place beneath the warm, beneath the bone, beneath all of it, something settled into its permanent shape. It was not a word and not a thought. It was not a decision.

It was simply this: *not yet.*

Not yet done. Not yet finished. Not yet over.

The fire breathed. The people breathed. Mei breathed.

They continued.
