Spring arrived and with it, there were people. In the valley, near the water, where she always found them. Smoke first. Then voices. Then the warm shapes of twenty-odd bodies, clustered around fires, moving in the patterns that were always the same. Women working, children playing, men watching the tree line. The architecture of the group, repeated and repeated, as though every tribe in the world had been built from the same drawing.

Mei sat on the ridge and watched.

She did not go down.

The distance was her choice — too far to hear words, close enough to see shapes. Close enough to watch a woman lift a child onto her hip. Close enough to see a man throw a spear at a target and the others laugh when it missed. Close enough to see the fire spark, rise, and settle.

She sat there all morning.

The sun crossed the sky. Shadows shortened, lengthened. The group moved through their day — a day like any day, full of the small business of staying alive together. A woman braided another woman's hair. A child fell in the water, came up screaming. Someone pulled it out, shook it and carried it to the fire. An old man sat apart from the others, working a piece of bone with a stone tool.

Mei's hands were still. Her breathing was even. The warm sat in her chest, quiet and neutral — it had nothing to say about this. No alarm. No pull. Just information. People are there. People are warm. People are alive.

She could go down. She had done it before. She knew the approach — the slow walk, the offered meat, the empty hands. She knew how it went. The cautious welcome, the days of usefulness, the growing comfort. Then the moment when the mask slipped, something moved too fast, or something was seen that should not be seen, and the group turned on her. The moment when she had to leave again, alone, climbing back up the mountain with nothing but the warm in her chest and the cold in her bones.

She could go down and try again.

She stood up. Took a step down the slope and stopped.

Her legs did not move. The warm could have carried her down the mountain at a run if she'd wanted. But something else held her. Something that lived in the same place as the warm but was not the warm. Something quieter.

She looked at the group. The woman with the child on her hip. The man practicing his throw. The old one with the bone. She could see them clearly — every detail, every gesture, every small expression of a life lived among others.

She could not see herself among them.

Not because they would reject her — though they would. Not because she was different — though she was. Because the shape of her no longer fit the shape of that. The days in the cave, the winters, the hunts, the silence — they had carved her into something that did not match the pattern anymore. A tool made for a different hand. A stone from a different mountain.

She stood on the slope for a long time.

The sun moved. The shadows moved. The group ate, talked, and lived. Mei watched them do it, and the watching was a kind of goodbye — not to them specifically, but to the idea of them. To the belief that somewhere, if she looked long enough, there was a fire with a space beside it that was shaped like her.

There wasn't. She knew that now. The wound of her exile had closed in the other summers, the other descents. This was the ground after. Flat. Hard. Quiet.

The fire below flickered. A child laughed — high, bright, carrying on the wind. Mei heard it clearly.

She turned.

The mountain was in front of her. The cave was above her. The warm in her chest was full, steady, and hers. The mountain was hers. The cave was hers. That was what she had — and having what you have, not pretending it was more than it was, not mourning what it wasn't — that was a kind of living. Not the kind she would have chosen. But the kind she had.

She climbed.

She did not look back.

She would not go down again.
