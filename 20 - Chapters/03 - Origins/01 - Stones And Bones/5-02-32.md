The first hunter came from the right. Mei let him come. His spear was high — a throwing grip, aimed at her chest. He was fast. She was faster.

She stepped inside the throw. The spear passed over her shoulder — she felt the shaft brush her neck — and her hand found the stone she had picked up when she stood, the one with the sharp edge that fit her palm like it had been made for it. She hit him in the throat. Not hard enough to kill. Hard enough to drop him, and he went down making sounds that were not words.

The second came from the left. She had already turned — the warm mapped their positions, six bright shapes converging, and she moved between them the way she moved between rocks on a mountainside, using the angles, keeping someone between her and someone else.

The second hunter's spear caught her arm. A line of fire from shoulder to elbow. She did not slow. The warm flooded the wound — not healing yet. But Holding. The pain was there. She put it somewhere else, in the room she had built for pain over the summers of getting hurt and not being able to stop.

She hit the second hunter with the heel of her palm — low, angled down, aimed at the knee the way the bear had hit her, all the weight behind a single point. His leg folded sideways. He went down screaming, both hands on the joint that was no longer shaped as a joint should be.

Two down. Five standing.

They were scared now. She could feel it — the warm read their heartbeats, fast and ragged, the rhythm of men who had expected to capture a girl and were instead fighting something they did not understand. But they were also hunters. Hunters did not run from prey. Pride held them where fear told them to go.

The third and fourth came together. Coordinated — one high, one low. Mei leaped the low spear, kicked the high one aside and landed between them. Her stone found the third man's wrist. The crunch of bone was loud in the morning air. He screamed. The fourth swung his shaft at her head. She ducked. The shaft caught her wounded shoulder. The pain broke through the room where she kept it. She staggered.

The fifth hit her from behind. A thrown stone — heavy, accurate, hitting the back of her skull with a sound like two rocks cracking together. The world went white. Then grey. The ground came up. Her knees hit first, then her hands. She tasted dirt and blood. The warm surged — desperate, flooding — but the grey was thick and her body was not listening as it should.

Hands. On her arms. On her legs. Weight. A knee on her back. Fingers in her hair, pulling her head up. She thrashed — an animal's thrash, pure instinct — and someone else hit the ground. More hands came. More weight. The warm screamed inside her, pushing outward, but she was already drained from the fighting. The stone to her head had scrambled something. The push was weak — a gust where she needed a storm.

She was pressed into the earth. Face down. Dirt in her mouth. Blood in her eyes from the scalp wound. Warm and thick, running down her forehead. Her arms were pinned behind her. Someone's foot was on her neck.

She stopped fighting.

The warm was thin. The grey was clearing, slowly, but her body was shaking. The wound on her arm was bleeding freely. The back of her skull felt wrong. She could not fight twenty people. She could not fight five, not from the ground, not half-blind, not with a cracked skull and empty reserves.

She lay still. Breathed. Let the warm gather — slowly, carefully, as she gathered it in the cave. Not to fight. To heal. To hold the broken things together while her body remembered how to work.

The foot on her neck pressed harder.

A voice above her. The big man's voice — she would know it anywhere, the sound of it was carved into her bones. He was speaking to the hunters. His tone was not angry. It was satisfied. The tone of a man whose prediction had been proven right.

Mei turned her head and looked up.

He stood over her. His shadow fell across her body. He looked down at her face with the expression she remembered from the clearing, five summers ago — the expression of a man assessing a tool that had cut him.

"Should have stayed gone," he said.

Mei said nothing. Her mouth was full of dirt and blood. The warm was doing its work, slowly, inside her skull, and she needed every breath for living.

The big man looked at the hunters. At the one on the ground, clutching his throat. At the one with the broken wrist. At the others, bruised, bleeding, and staring at the girl in the dirt who had done this.

He spoke again. A single word. The word for the thing they did with animals too dangerous to keep and too stubborn to die.

Mei closed her eyes. The warm pulsed. The earth was cold under her cheek. The sky was bright above her. And somewhere, in the deep place below the warm, something stirred.

*Not yet*, it said.

*Not yet.*
