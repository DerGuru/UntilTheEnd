She went down in the second summer. The need came as it always did — not as a thought, not as a decision, but as a pressure behind her ribs that had nothing to do with the warm. A human pressure. The ancient, undeniable weight of a social animal separated from its kind.

She had heard a bird that morning — two notes, repeated, a call meant for another bird. The answer came from across the valley, faint but certain. Two voices finding each other across distance. Mei sat in the cave entrance and listened until the calling stopped.

Then she packed.

The descent was easier now. She knew the mountain — every ridge, every game trail, every place where the footing was bad. Her body was different: longer, harder, her legs corded with muscle from climbing, her hands rough as bark from stone, wood, and cold. She was not a child anymore. She was not sure what she was.

She found a group by the river, in the place where the water widened and slowed. A small group — eighteen, maybe twenty. They had good fires, lean-tos of branches and skins. Children ran at the water's edge. The sound of their voices drifted up the slope like smoke. Mei stood in the tree line and let the sound wash over her — the children's voices, the crack of a fire being fed, a woman calling. The sounds filled a space that had been empty so long she had forgotten it was there.

Mei approached carefully. She carried meat — fresh, from a kill two days old. She walked slowly, hands visible. She had practiced this: the approach, the offering, the posture that said I am useful, not dangerous. She had rehearsed being small.

The group was wary but not hostile. A woman took the meat. A man pointed to a spot at the edge of camp. Mei sat.

This time, she was careful.

She did not react when the warm told her things. When a predator moved in the trees beyond the camp, she held still and let the group's own watchers notice it. When she felt the fish gathering in a pool downstream — dense, bright clusters of warmth — she walked there casually and pointed, as though she had seen ripples, nothing more.

She ate what they shared. She worked when they worked. She slept at the edge, where the firelight barely reached. The effort of suppression was constant. When the warm flared — at a rustle in the brush, at a shift in the air — she breathed through it, kept her face still, forced her body into the posture of someone who had heard nothing. It was exhausting. Harder than hunting. Pretending to be less than she was took more strength than being what she was.

Days passed. The group relaxed around her — not fully, never fully, but the space around her shrank. A woman sat closer at the fire. A man tossed her a piece of dried fish without ceremony. The children lost their wariness first — they circled, stared, brought her things to hold. A girl with tangled hair put a caterpillar on Mei's knee and waited for a reaction. Mei picked it up, set it on a leaf. The girl shrieked with delight.

On the fifth night, the old woman came to sit beside Mei. She touched Mei's hair — long, tangled, matted where rain had worked it into ropes — and made a sound that was half disapproval, half amusement. Then, without asking, she began to work her fingers through it.

Mei held very still. The old woman's hands were warm, rough, patient. She worked from the ends, separating the knots with her fingertips, pulling gently where the strands had fused. The pulling hurt — a small, clean pain. Mei's scalp tingled. Her eyes burned. She did not know why — or she did, and the knowing was worse than the burning. The last person who had touched her hair had been her mother.

The old woman hummed while she worked. A low, tuneless sound — not a melody, a vibration. A sound that said: I am here, doing this, taking my time. When she finished, she patted Mei's head once. Firm.

More days. Mei helped build a new lean-to. She carried water. She showed the children how to find grubs under bark — pulling away the loose pieces, pointing at the white shapes curling in the rot. The children squealed and jostled for position. One of them grabbed her hand, did not let go. Mei let herself be held. The child's palm was small, sticky, and perfect.

She ate with them at the fire — not at the edge, in the circle. Someone passed her a rib bone with meat still clinging to it. The fat was rich. The meat was tough. The taste was fire, salt, the faint sweetness of shared food. The woman beside her spoke — a question, the words foreign but the shape familiar. Mei shook her head and smiled. The woman smiled back.

She almost stayed. The almost was a physical thing — a weight in her chest, warm and terrible. Every morning she woke and the group was there, breathing, moving, being. Every morning the weight grew heavier.

She told herself: one more day. Every night at the fire's edge, she told herself that. Every morning the one-more-day became another day, until the counting stopped mattering.

On the ninth night, a sound woke her.

Something in the scrub — large, moving. The warm flared: predator, close, approaching from the north. Mei's body reacted before her mind caught up. She was on her feet, crouched, her eyes scanning the dark. The warm pushed to her senses — sight sharpening, the darkness thinning, the shapes of trees, rocks, and the thing moving between them resolving into clarity.

She saw the wolf. Grey, thin, alone — a scout or a young one separated from its pack. Not a real threat — one wolf, no larger than a dog. But she was standing, in the dark, with eyes that caught no firelight and a posture that was not human. Coiled. Ready. Predatory.

A woman sitting near the fire saw her.

Their eyes met. The woman looked at Mei's face — at whatever was on her face, whatever the warm did to her eyes when she pushed it there. The woman's mouth opened. Closed. She pulled her sleeping child closer without looking away from Mei.

In that look was everything Mei had spent nine days hiding. The woman had not seen Mei carry water, build shelters, find grubs for the children. She had seen this — the thing in the dark with shining eyes. One moment had undone nine days.

Mei felt it happen — the shift, the wall, the invisible line being drawn. She had seen it before. She knew exactly what it looked like from the inside.

In the morning, the space around her was wider. The woman who had untangled her hair did not come near. The children were kept away. Food was not shared.

Nobody pointed. Nobody spoke the words. But the camp had decided, as camps always did — silently, collectively, in the space between breaths.

Mei stood up. She took her spear, her water skin. She looked at the group one last time. The old woman sat near the fire, hands in her lap, not looking up. The spot where she had untangled Mei's hair was empty.

The child who had held her hand was watching from behind its mother's leg. It raised one hand — small, open. A wave. Or a reaching.

Mei turned and walked toward the mountains.

The climb back was long. She did not hurry. The understanding settled over her like weather — not sudden, not dramatic. Just a fact, arriving. She had hidden the warm. She had been careful, patient, useful. She had done everything right. And it had not been enough, because the thing that was wrong was not something she did. It was something she was. She had known this before — at the river, at every camp that pointed her away. But this time the knowing was deeper. Not a bruise. A scar. Scars do not hurt. They are simply part of the shape of you.

The cave entrance was dark, narrow, and familiar. She squeezed through. The warm of the cave met her — dense, patient, unchanged.

She sat on the stone floor. Pressed her palms flat. Let the warm pour in.

Her eyes did not burn this time. She was past that. Her body felt strange — too full of the cave's warmth, too empty of everything else. Nine days among people, then this. The contrast was a blade in itself.

She began to shape a new blade.
