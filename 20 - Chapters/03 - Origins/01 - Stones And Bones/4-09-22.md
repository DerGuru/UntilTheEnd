She went down in the third summer.

The need came the way it always came—not as a thought, not as a decision, but as a pressure behind her ribs that had nothing to do with the warm. A human pressure. The ancient, undeniable weight of a social animal separated from its kind.

The descent was easier now. She knew the mountain—every ridge, every game trail, every place where the footing was bad. Her body was different: longer, harder, her legs corded with muscle from climbing, her hands rough as bark from stone and wood and cold. She was not a child anymore. She was not sure what she was.

She found a group by the river, in the place where the water widened and slowed. A small group—eighteen, maybe twenty. They had good fires and lean-tos of branches and skins, and children ran at the water's edge, and the sound of their voices drifted up the slope like smoke.

Mei approached carefully. She carried meat—fresh, from a kill two days old. She walked slowly, hands visible. She had practiced this: the approach, the offering, the posture that said I am useful, not dangerous.

The group was wary but not hostile. A woman took the meat. A man pointed to a spot at the edge of camp. Mei sat.

This time, she was careful.

She did not react when the warm told her things. When a predator moved in the trees beyond the camp, she held still and let the group's own watchers notice it. When she felt the fish gathering in a pool downstream—dense, bright clusters of warmth—she walked there casually and pointed, as though she had seen ripples, nothing more.

She ate what they shared. She worked when they worked. She slept at the edge, where the firelight barely reached.

Days passed. The group relaxed around her—not fully, never fully, but the space around her shrank. A woman sat closer. A child came to stare at her, and she held out a piece of dried meat, and the child took it and ran away laughing. An old woman touched Mei's hair—long, tangled, matted in places—and made a sound that was half disapproval, half amusement. She began to untangle it with her fingers.

Mei held very still. The old woman's hands were warm and rough and patient, and the pulling was gentle, and Mei's eyes burned and she did not know why.

More days. Mei helped build a new lean-to. She carried water. She showed the children how to find grubs under bark—pulling away the loose pieces, pointing at the white shapes curling in the rot. The children squealed. One of them grabbed her hand and did not let go, and Mei let herself be held, and the child's palm was small and sticky and perfect.

She almost stayed.

On the ninth night, a sound woke her.

Something in the scrub—large, moving. The warm flared: predator, close, approaching from the north. Mei's body reacted before her mind caught up. She was on her feet, crouched, her eyes scanning the dark. The warm pushed to her senses—sight sharpening, the darkness thinning, the shapes of trees and rocks and the thing moving between them resolving into clarity.

She saw the wolf. Grey, thin, alone—a scout or a young one separated from its pack. Not a real threat—one wolf, no larger than a dog. But she was standing, in the dark, with eyes that caught no firelight and a posture that was not human. Coiled. Ready. Predatory.

A woman sitting near the fire saw her.

Their eyes met. The woman looked at Mei's face—at whatever was on her face, whatever the warm did to her eyes when she pushed it there. The woman's mouth opened. Closed. She pulled her sleeping child closer without looking away from Mei.

Mei felt it happen—the shift, the wall, the invisible line being drawn. She had seen it before. She knew exactly what it looked like from the inside.

In the morning, the space around her was wider. The woman who had untangled her hair did not come near. The children were kept away. Food was not shared.

Nobody pointed. Nobody spoke the words. But the camp had decided, the way camps always decided—silently, collectively, in the space between breaths.

Mei stood up. She took her spear, her water skin. She looked at the group one last time.

The child who had held her hand was watching from behind its mother's leg. It raised one hand—small, open. A wave. Or a reaching.

Mei turned and walked toward the mountains.

The climb back was long. She did not hurry. The understanding settled over her like weather—not sudden, not dramatic. Just a fact, arriving. She had hidden the warm. She had been careful, patient, useful. She had done everything right. And it had not been enough, because the thing that was wrong was not something she did. It was something she was.

The cave entrance was dark and narrow and familiar. She squeezed through. The warm of the cave met her—dense, patient, unchanged.

She sat on the stone floor. Pressed her palms flat. Let the warm pour in.

Her eyes did not burn this time. She was past that.

She began to shape a new blade.
