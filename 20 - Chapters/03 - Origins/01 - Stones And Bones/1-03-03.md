The fire cracked and spit, and the fat from the meat hissed where it dripped onto the coals.

Mei sat between Koh and her mother, close enough that their arms touched. Around them, the People ate. Hands tore at strips of flesh. Teeth cracked small bones for the marrow inside. A child gnawed on a sinew with the serious concentration of someone doing important work.

The smoke rose straight in the still air, pale against the darkening sky. It smelled of burning wood and animal fat and something sweeter underneath—the sap from the green branches someone had used when the dry ones ran out. Mei breathed it in and let it settle in her chest. This smell. This sound. The murmur of voices around her, overlapping and soft, not words so much as the texture of people being together.

This was the best part of any day.

Someone passed a skin of water. Mei drank and passed it on. Her mother took it without looking, drank, passed it further. The rhythm was automatic. Food moved from hand to hand. No one asked, no one thanked. It moved because it was supposed to move, and that was enough.

Mei counted the faces around the fire without thinking about it. She always counted. Not because anyone had told her to, but because the number mattered in a way she could not have explained. When the number was right, the world felt balanced. When someone was missing, the balance tilted.

Tonight the number was wrong.

She counted again. Looked at the spaces between the bodies, the gaps where shoulders should touch but didn't.

Ren was not here.

He had gone out with the hunters at dawn—one of the younger men, broad across the chest, quick to laugh. He had been carrying two spears, the good ones with the fire-hardened tips, and he had waved at the children as the hunters left, the way he always did.

The other hunters had come back before dark. Ren had not.

Nobody said his name. The adults spoke in low sounds, leaning close, and then they stopped speaking and ate. One of the women—his woman, the one who slept beside him—sat at the edge of the group with her food untouched in her lap. She stared at the fire. Her face was flat and still, like water with no wind.

A child tugged at the woman next to her. Pointed at the empty space.

"Ren?"

The woman glanced down. Made a small gesture with her hand—outward, away. Gone.

The child looked at the space where Ren usually sat. Then looked at the fire. Then went back to eating.

Mei watched all of this. She did not understand death—not as a concept, not as a word. But she understood absence. She understood that sometimes a face was there in the morning and not there at night, and that the gap it left was shaped exactly like the person who had filled it, and that by tomorrow the gap would begin to close, because gaps always closed, and by the next moon nobody would sit in that specific spot anymore but nobody would leave it empty on purpose either.

This was the way of things. She did not need to be told.

Across the fire, the big man sat in the best place—the place where the smoke did not reach and the heat was strongest. He was broader than any other man in the group, his shoulders like boulders set on either side of a thick neck. He ate first, and he ate the most, and nobody questioned this because nobody wanted to be the one who did.

Mei watched him. She watched the way the others shifted when he moved—small adjustments, like water flowing around a stone. Not fear, exactly. Awareness. The knowledge that this man took up more space than his body filled.

He laughed at something one of the hunters said, a big sound that rolled across the camp. The children near him flinched. Not badly. Just a little.

Mei did not like looking at him. She could not have said why. He had never touched her, never spoken to her, never done anything that she could point to and say: that. But something about him made her skin feel tight. Too close, even from across the fire. Too much of something. She turned her head and looked at the ground.

Her mother's arm came around her shoulder. Not pulling, not squeezing. Just there. The weight of it settled on Mei like a second skin.

Mei leaned in. Pressed her cheek against her mother's ribs. Listened to the heartbeat there—steady, slow, the most familiar sound in the world. Her mother smelled of smoke and dried grass and the particular salt of her own skin, a smell that Mei could have found in the dark, in a crowd, in a storm.

The fire burned lower. The voices softened. One by one, bodies folded toward the ground, curling into sleeping positions worn smooth by repetition. Someone threw a last branch on the coals, and the sparks rose like bright insects and vanished into the dark.

Koh was already asleep beside her, breathing through her mouth the way she did, her hand flung out and resting on Mei's leg. Mei did not move it. The weight was good.

Her mother's arm tightened once—brief, barely there—and then relaxed.

Mei closed her eyes. The fire popped. An insect hummed somewhere in the dark. The smell of smoke wrapped around her like a blanket.

The People breathed together, and the night was safe, and everything that mattered was here.
