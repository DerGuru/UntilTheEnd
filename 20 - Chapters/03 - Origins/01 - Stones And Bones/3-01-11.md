They were sitting on the flat stone — their stone, the one where they always sat when the camp was near water. Mei's feet hung over the edge. The river ran below, low and clear. She could see the dark shapes of fish holding themselves still against the current. The air smelled of wet rock and the green, sharp scent of the plants that grew where the bank was soft.

This was their place. They had been coming here — or places like it, flat stones near water, wherever the camp stopped — since Mei could remember. It was where Koh talked about things she didn't talk about at the fire. It was where Mei showed Koh the warm, pressing her hands to the stone, pushing warmth into the surface until Koh could feel it if she concentrated. Their place. The one part of the world that belonged to them alone.

Koh was not climbing. Koh was not talking. Koh was sitting with her knees pulled up, arms wrapped around them, staring at the water with the expression of someone who is looking at something but seeing something else. Her hair hung in her face, unwashed, tangled. There was a bruise on her shoulder that had not been there yesterday — a dark mark, shaped like fingers, high on the muscle where the arm met the neck. Koh had not explained it. Mei had not asked.

Mei waited. Koh would talk when Koh wanted to talk.

The river moved below. A bird crossed the water — low, fast, its wings almost touching the surface. The sun was warm on Mei's back. Somewhere in the camp behind them, a child was crying — the thin, exhausted sound of a child who had been crying for a long time. Koh did not seem to hear it.

It took a long time.

"He comes near," Koh said.

Mei looked at her. "Who?"

"Big man."

The words dropped from her mouth like stones — flat, heavy, falling into the silence between them.

The words were flat. Koh said them the way you say a stone is heavy or water is wet — stating a fact, not complaining. But her hands were tight on her knees, the knuckles pale under the brown of her skin.

"Near how?"

"Stands. Watches. Comes where I go."

Mei felt the warm pulse in her chest. Not the warning-pulse — not the hot, dense alarm of a predator. Something else. Something slow and thick, like mud settling in a pool.

"When?"

"Many days. More now."

Koh unlocked one hand from her knees and made a gesture — a pulling gesture, toward herself. Then a pushing gesture, away. Toward, away. Toward, away. The meaning was clear even without words: he comes closer. She moves back. He comes closer again.

Her hands were shaking. Koh's hands did not shake. Koh's hands climbed trees, killed fish, threw stones with an accuracy that made the hunters nod. Koh's hands did not shake.

"Touched?" Mei asked.

"Arm. Here." Koh touched her own wrist. "Holds."

Mei's jaw tightened. The warm in her chest moved — up, thick, pressing against the inside of her ribs. She breathed through it.

The warm was reacting to something it had no words for — anger, fear, the particular sick heat of understanding what was happening to someone she loved. It pushed against her ribs, dense, slow, coiling. She pressed her fist against her sternum.

"Others see?"

"Some. Nobody — " Koh made a gesture that was not a word. A turning away. A looking-somewhere-else. Mei understood: nobody did anything.

"The old man?"

"He is old. Big man is big."

The sentence contained everything — the shape of their world, where size was authority and age was irrelevance. The old man could speak. The old man could object. The old man could not stop the big man from doing what the big man wanted.

They sat in silence. The river moved below. A fish broke the surface and vanished. The sound of the camp was behind them — distant, ordinary, like something happening in another world.

Koh was thirteen summers. The big man was grown — fully, heavily, immovably grown. And in the tribe, what the big man wanted, the big man took, because that was the shape of the world. The shape of the world was not something two girls on a rock could change.

Mei reached over and took Koh's hand. Koh's fingers were cold. Cold hands — Koh, who ran warm, who had warm hands and a warm face and the careless energy of a body that had never doubted its own safety. Her fingers were cold now.

"I won't let him."

"Can't."

"I won't."

"He's stronger. Stronger than anyone."

Mei knew this. She had felt the big man's heat — the dense, heavy pressure of whatever he carried inside him. It was bigger than her warm. Bigger than anything she had felt. But the knowing did not change the answer.

"I don't care."

Koh turned her head and looked at Mei. Her eyes were wet, but nothing fell. Koh did not cry. Koh had never cried in front of Mei, not once, not even when she fell from the high tree, her ankle swelled to the size of a fist, and she had to be carried for three days.

"Mei."

"I won't let him."

Koh's mouth did something that was almost a smile and wasn't. She squeezed Mei's hand once, hard. Then let go.

They sat on the rock until the light changed and someone called them back. They went back together, shoulders touching. Neither of them said anything else.

The warm in Mei's chest had not settled. It sat there, heavy, dense, and waiting, like a fist that had not yet decided to open.

Koh leaned against Mei's shoulder. Not a bump — a lean. The full weight of her body, resting. The weight of everything she had said, resting.

The river ran below. The sun moved. The shadow of the stone crept across the water, changing nothing.
