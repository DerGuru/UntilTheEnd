The People moved at first light. Mei carried the bundle on her back as she'd seen the women carry theirs — spine straight, chin up, both hands gripping the cord across her chest. The bundle was not heavy. Dried roots, two handfuls of seed pods, a scraping stone wrapped in grass. But she carried it as though it mattered, because when you carried something, you were part of the moving.

Around her, the world smelled of cold earth, wet bark. The rain had stopped sometime in the night, and the ground was soft under her feet, pressing up between her toes with every step. Somewhere ahead, a man grunted under the weight of a carcass slung across his shoulders. Behind her, a woman adjusted a sleeping child on her hip without breaking stride.

Nobody explained where they were going. Nobody needed to.

The People followed the water. When the water moved, they moved. When the banks grew too steep or the crossing too deep, they turned and walked along the ridge until the ground opened again. Mei had never known a different life. She did not know there could be one.

Koh caught up to her from behind, bumping her shoulder. Two summers older, a full head taller, and loud enough that birds startled from the branches when she laughed.

"Slow."

"Not slow."

"Slow slow." Koh grinned and pushed ahead, weaving between the adults with the easy grace of someone who had never once considered that she might not belong exactly where she was.

Mei followed. She always followed Koh, and Koh always went first.

The column of bodies stretched along the ridgeline like a living thing — twenty-three people, give or take, depending on who counted and whether you counted the baby that had not yet survived its first cold season. The column had its own sound — feet on packed earth, the creak of carrying frames, a baby's whimper answered by a low hum, the occasional sharp word from one adult to another. It was the sound of a thing that worked.

The hunters walked at the edges, eyes on the tree line. The gatherers walked the center, children orbiting them like stones tossed in water — circling, drifting, always pulled back.

Mei watched everything.

She watched Asa test the ground ahead with her foot before stepping, pressing the ball down first, then the heel. She watched the old man — the one everyone listened to, the one whose name sounded like a cough — pause at the top of a rise and turn his face into the wind, nostrils wide, reading something in the air that Mei could not yet name. She watched a beetle climb a stem of grass near her ankle, its shell the color of wet clay, and she stopped walking to see where it would go.

"Mei."

Her mother's voice. Not sharp — just there. The way a hand on the shoulder is there.

Mei left the beetle and caught up.

Her mother walked two body-lengths ahead — steady, straight-backed, the carrying bundle balanced on her hip with the ease of someone who had been carrying things since before Mei was born. She smelled of sweat, leather, and the dried herbs she kept in her belt pouch. When Mei caught up, her mother reached back without looking and touched the top of Mei's head. Once. Brief. Then forward again.

The sun climbed. The column shifted, spreading where the ground was flat, tightening where the trees closed in. Children ran between the legs of the walkers, were scooped up, set down, shoved gently forward. A boy two summers older than Mei picked up a stick and swung it at nothing, practicing the motion he had seen the hunters make. An older woman took the stick from his hand without a word and broke it in half. The boy stared. The woman walked on. He picked up a different stick.

Nobody taught the children. Not with words. You watched, you tried, you were corrected. A tap on the wrist meant wrong. A nod meant right. A hand on the back of the head, pushing you forward, meant stop looking and start moving.

Mei understood this without thinking. It was simply how things worked.

By midday, they stopped. The water was wide here and shallow enough to drink from without kneeling too deep. The People spread along the bank. Someone built a fire from the embers carried in the clay pot — the pot that was always someone's job to carry, though whose job it was seemed to shift without discussion. Smoke rose thin and pale into the canopy.

The meat came out — yesterday's kill, dark, salted with ash from the smoking. Two women cut it. The hunters got the biggest portions. The old man got the softest piece, because his teeth were not what they had been. Children got what was left, which was always enough. One woman sliced. The other tore. Different hands, different methods, same result.

Mei crouched at the water's edge and drank. The water tasted of stone and something green — algae, maybe, or the leaves that had fallen upstream. She splashed her face and watched the ripples break her reflection into pieces.

Koh sat beside her, chewing on a strip of dried meat she had taken from somewhere.

"Good water."

"Cold," Mei said.

"Good cold."

They sat with their feet in the shallows, the water cold around their ankles. Small fish darted between the stones — dark shapes, quick, gone. Koh splashed at them. Missed. Splashed again.

Mei cupped more water and let it run through her fingers. The light caught the drops — bright, scattered, gone.

Her mother was nearby. Not beside her, not across the camp, but in the middle distance that meant I am here and I see you. Mei did not need to look to know this. She could feel it — the particular quality of being watched by someone who would not let harm come close. Not warmth exactly. Presence. Like standing near a fire that you cannot see but whose heat finds the back of your neck.

One of the men came back from scouting upstream. He spoke to the old man in short, clipped sounds — gestures more than words. Pointed upriver. Shook his head. Pointed east. The old man listened, touched his own chin, looked at the sky.

They would stay here tonight.

The children knew before anyone said it. Something in the way the adults stopped moving with purpose and began moving with rest — shoulders dropping, bundles set down, hands reaching for food. The shift was small and total. Mei felt it pass through the group like a shiver. Then it was done. They were home. Not a place. A pause.

Koh was already up, pulling Mei by the arm.

"Come."

"Where?"

"There." Koh pointed at a cluster of boulders near the tree line and ran — arms out, head back, as though the air itself would carry her if she moved fast enough.

Mei set her bundle down beside her mother's feet. Her mother glanced down at the bundle, then at Mei, then back at whatever she was doing with the sinew and the scraping tool.

Mei ran after Koh.

The boulders were warm from the sun. They climbed, slipped, climbed again. Koh reached the top first and stood with her hands on her hips, surveying the world below them as though she had conquered it. Mei pulled herself up beside her, breathing hard, knees scraped.

From here, the People looked small. Fires flickered. Voices carried — soft, shapeless, like water over stone. The river glinted through the trees.

"Big," Koh said.

Mei looked at the sky, at the ridge, at the endless green that rolled in every direction.

"Big," she agreed.

The wind moved their hair. Below, a woman was singing — thin, tuneless, a working song meant for nobody. Koh leaned back on her hands and closed her eyes. Mei watched the ridge, the trees, the light on the river. She did not have a word for what she felt. She would not have used it if she had. She was here. Koh was here. The People were below. The sky was large enough to hold all of it.

They sat on the warm stone until someone called them back for food.
