The mountains did not welcome her. They did not send her away either. They simply were — stone, wind, and the slow, grinding indifference of things that had been there since before anything with legs had walked on them.

Mei climbed.

The terrain changed with every day. Below, the trees had been broad and soft, with leaves that gave shade and roots that held water. Here the trees thinned, grew shorter, twisted by wind into shapes that looked like they were trying to escape from the ground. The underbrush was sharp — thorns that caught skin, drew thin lines of blood, low bushes with berries she did not trust, tough grass that cut if you grabbed it wrong.

She ate less. The food from her mother's bundle was gone. She found things — a nest of eggs, larvae under bark, mushrooms growing in the crevice of a fallen trunk. The warm told her which ones were wrong — a faint, sour note in her chest, different from the clean pulse of safe things. She did not eat the wrong ones. She ate everything else. It was not enough. The hunger became a thing she carried like a second bundle: always there, shifting in weight but never absent.

Her feet hardened. She had been walking barefoot since she could remember, but mountain stone was different from river mud and forest floor. Sharp, unforgiving. By the end of the first days her soles were cracked and bleeding. The warm helped — slowly, as it had with the thorn-cut, mending what was broken while she slept. Each morning the cracks were shallower. Each evening they came back. But her skin thickened, and after a while the stone stopped cutting.

The first real danger came on a ridge.

She was crossing a narrow spine of rock, wind pulling at her from both sides, when the warm in her chest flared — hot, sudden, the predator-warning she had not felt since the cat. She dropped flat against the stone.

Below her, on the slope to the left, something moved. Low. Tawny. Shoulders that rolled with the easy, terrible grace of a thing designed to kill.

A cat. Not the same one — smaller, younger maybe, but the same shape, the same promise of speed, teeth, and the complete absence of mercy.

It had not seen her. The wind was blowing across the ridge, carrying her scent away. Mei pressed herself against the stone, breathed through her mouth, did not move. The rock was cold under her belly. Her heartbeat was loud in her ears — too loud, she was sure the cat could hear it, sure the entire mountain could hear it.

The warm surged in her chest — desperate, pushing outward as it had pushed at the big man. Her body wanted to do it again. Wanted to blast this thing away, to push it off the slope, down the mountain, and into nothing.

She clenched her fists. Not now. Not now. She could not afford to empty herself as she had in the camp. If the blast didn't work — if the cat was too far, or she was too weak — she'd be drained and helpless on a ridge with a predator.

She waited.

The cat moved on. Unhurried. It flowed down the slope and into the scrub below. After a long time Mei could not feel its warmth anymore. After a longer time she unclenched her fists and found that her nails had left red crescents in her palms.

She stood. Her legs shook. She walked on.

The air got thinner. Colder. She made fire every night — as the People had taught, friction, patience, and dry grass. She slept close to the flames. She woke to find frost on the ground around her. The warm in her chest helped with the cold too, though she did not understand how. She pushed it outward — not in a blast, but gently, a slow seep of heat that kept the worst of the chill from settling in her bones. It worked, but it tired her. In the mornings she was drained, the warm thin and weak. She spent the first hours of walking in the cold before it filled again.

She was getting stronger. Not in the way of muscles — though those were coming too, her arms harder, her legs longer, the softness of childhood burning away under the demands of climbing, carrying, and staying alive. She was getting stronger in the other way. The warm way. It filled faster now. It held longer. When she pushed it to her hands, it stayed — steady and bright — instead of fading as it used to.

This was what the mountains gave her: not comfort, not safety, not belonging. Only the slow, grinding education of a body and a gift that were learning to work together because the alternative was dying.

She climbed higher. The trees stopped. The rock was bare, grey, streaked with lichen in colors she had no names for. The wind was constant. The sky was enormous — bigger than it had been below, wider, as though the world was opening up in compensation for everything it had taken away.

Mei stood on a ridge and looked down at the world she had left. Somewhere down there, far away, the river glittered. Somewhere along that river, people sat around fires. Breathed together. Slept in piles. Belonged to each other.

She turned her back and kept climbing.
