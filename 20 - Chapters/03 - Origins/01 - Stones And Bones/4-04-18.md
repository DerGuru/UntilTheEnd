Mei sat cross-legged on the smooth stone, hands on her knees. Still. Eyes closed. She had nothing else to do. The hunger was a constant now — not sharp enough to force her out, not gone enough to forget. The water from the basin was clean, cold. It tasted of mineral and deep places. It kept her alive. But she could not eat stone, and the warm, for all its brightness, was not food.

So she sat. She listened. The warm taught her.

She breathed in. The warm from the cave came with the breath — not air, not temperature, but something that rode alongside both. It entered through her mouth, her skin, the soles of her feet, the backs of her hands. It flowed down into the place behind her ribs where the warm lived.

She breathed out. Most of it flowed back. Out through her skin, her breath, returning to the cave. But not all. A trace remained — thin, faint, clinging to the place behind her ribs like moisture on stone.

She breathed in. More came.

She breathed out. Most left again.

In. Out. In. Out. Each breath, the warm inside her grew a little denser — but slowly. So slowly. Like silt building in a riverbed, one grain at a time, invisible unless you watched for seasons.

She did this for days. She did not count them. Time in the cave was not time as she had understood it before, marked by sun, moon, and the movement of the People from one place to another. Time here was measured in breaths and in the drips of water from the ceiling.

She tried to reach for it.

Not with her hands. With the thing inside her chest — the way she would reach toward a fire on a cold night. She reached, and nothing happened. The warm was there, all around her, but it did not come faster. It came as it always came: slowly, with the breath, at its own pace.

She tried again. Harder. Straining the thing inside her, pulling, the way she pulled water from deep roots. Nothing. Or worse — a headache, sharp behind her eyes, that lasted until she slept.

She tried the next day. And the day after. Each time, the warm ignored her reaching as completely as stone ignores shouting.

She stopped trying.

She sat. She breathed. She drank from the basin. When the hunger grew too sharp, she went outside — blinking, weak-legged — and found what she could. Roots. Grubs. Once, a lizard too slow to run. Then she came back. Squeezed through the entrance. Sat down. Breathed.

Days passed. Many days. She stopped counting. The first time she went out for food might have been after three days or five. The second time was longer. The third time, she barely noticed leaving.

She reached again. Imagined it. The warm flowing into her with the breath. Saw it in her mind: the cave's warmth gathering, thickening, moving toward her the way mist gathers into droplets on cold stone. Ribbons of warmth, condensing out of the air, flowing in through her skin, her mouth, the soles of her feet.

And the warm responded. It came. A little more than breathing brought. A little faster. Thin ribbons, barely there, but real. She felt them for three breaths before the image slipped and she was back to the slow drip of before.

She tried again. The image came — ribbons, condensing, flowing in — but her stomach cramped and the picture broke apart. Hunger made it hard to hold anything in her mind that was not food.

She went outside. The light hurt. The cold bit into her skin after the cave's warmth. She searched the loose soil below the scree, digging with her fingers until her nails cracked, and found two roots — thin, woody, barely worth chewing. She ate them raw, dirt still on the skin. It was not enough. It was never enough. But she came back. Sat down. And tried again.

The ribbons came. Three breaths. Four. Then gone.

She slept, but when she woke the image would not form at all. She lay on the stone, staring at the dark ceiling, trying to see the ribbons, trying to feel the gathering — but her mind kept sliding toward the ache in her stomach, the hollow that grew sharper each day. She tried again in the evening, after a day spending to find eatable. She went further this time — down the slope to where the scrub grew thick, her legs shaking from days of sitting. She turned over stones, peeled bark, dug in the cold soil until her fingers bled. She found some grubs and a handful of berries, sour and small, half of them shriveled. It took most of the day to find enough to quiet the hollow for a while.

Back in the cave. Cross-legged. Hands on knees. The image. The ribbons. Seven breaths. Eight. Nine. Gone — but gently this time, fading rather than breaking.

Each time she sucessfully tried, the reaching lasted longer. Each time, the image required less effort to hold — less like lifting something heavy, more like remembering a familiar path. The ribbons stopped being something she built in her mind and became something she noticed. They were already there. She just had to see them.

And then one day she breathed in, and the reaching was simply part of the breath. No image. No effort. The warm flowed toward her because she was open to it, the way water flows toward the lowest point. Not because she pulled. Because she allowed. And the reaching finally came as easily as breathing.

She pulled too much. It poured into the place behind her ribs — fast, concentrated, a stream where before there had been mist. The place glowed. Her whole body warmed, and for a moment she felt as though she were made of light.

Then her head spun, her stomach cramped, and she had to stop. Too much. Too fast. The nausea hit her — sudden, violent, her throat tightening, her mouth flooding with spit. She clenched her jaw. Swallowed. Not this. She was not giving back the food she had spent half a day bleeding for. She pressed her forehead against the cool stone and fought the heaving in her chest, her stomach, her throat. It took a long time. Her body wanted to empty itself and she would not let it.

When the sickness passed, she lay still on the stone, breathing, trembling, the warm settling back to its usual low hum. She had been greedy. She would not be greedy again. Slow. Steady. A trickle, not a stream. The warm was not going anywhere. It would be here tomorrow, the day after, and the day after that. She did not need to grab it. She needed to let it come.

She curled up on the smooth stone. The warm pulsed beneath her. The drops fell from the ceiling into the basin. One. Two. Three. The same rhythm as always. The same rhythm as the first night.

She closed her eyes and matched her breathing to the dripping.

Tomorrow she would try again. Slower this time.
