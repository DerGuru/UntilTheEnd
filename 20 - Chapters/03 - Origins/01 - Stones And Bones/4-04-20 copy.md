She pushed it to her ears.

The dripping — the constant, familiar dripping of the cave — hit her like a herd of mammoths stampeding through her skull. Each drop was a crack of thunder. She slapped her hands over her ears but it made no difference — the sound was inside. She yanked the warm back so fast it hurt, and crouched in the ringing silence with her heart slamming against her ribs.

She did not try her ears again for a long time.

When she did — less. A thread. The dripping became distinct: she could hear each drop forming, stretching, breaking free, falling, striking. She could hear the wind outside. Animals moving on the slope. Her own blood, her own breath, her own bones creaking when she shifted.

Enough. She pulled it back. Silence was a kindness she had not appreciated before.


She sat in the cave one evening and the memory came without invitation. Dak. His weight on top of her. The terror. And then — the blast. The warm erupting out of her, slamming into him, sending him across the clearing like a leaf in a storm.

She had pushed it outward. Not into her body — out of it. Into the air. Into him.

She faced the cracked stone. Drew the warm into her hand. Imagined it leaving — shooting from her palm like a thrown spear, striking the stone. Pushed.

Nothing.

The warm pooled in her palm. Dense, hot, waiting. But it would not leave. She pushed harder. It pressed against the inside of her skin like something trapped. She pushed until her hand ached. Nothing left. It stayed inside her, stubborn, refusing.

She tried again. And again. For days — sitting before the stone, hand outstretched, pushing with everything she had. The warm grew hot in her palm. Her skin reddened. But nothing left. Nothing touched the stone.

She screamed. Slammed her hand against the ground — and nothing. Tried again the next day. Nothing. Tried imagining it differently — a thrown rock, a gust of wind, a shove. Nothing. The warm would not leave her body.

She kicked the wall in frustration. Stamped her foot against the cave floor.

*Crack.*

She looked down. A thin line in the stone — running from under her heel in two directions. She had not pushed the warm to her foot. She had not thought about directing it at all. She had just been *angry*.

She tilted her head.

She turned back to the cracked stone. Drew the warm into her hand. Thought about Dak. About his weight. About the fear and the rage and the helplessness. The warm grew hot — hotter than before, hotter than practice had ever made it. She imagined it hitting the stone the way it had hit Dak. Not technique. Not precision. Just — *go*.

A pulse. Small. Weak. But her palm pushed *outward* — not against the stone, but through the air between them. She felt it leave. A breath of heat that crossed the gap and touched the rock.

Not enough. But it left.

She sat back. Breathing. Thinking. Emotions. The warm listened to emotions the way it did not listen to thought alone. She had been trying to push with her mind. It needed her *gut*.

Days. She practiced anger — real anger, not pretend. Thought about Koh's body in the dirt. About the blood. About being alone. Let the rage build until the warm burned in her palm. Then aimed it.

Closer. Closer each day. The pulses grew stronger — from a breath to a gust to a shove she could feel in the air. And then one morning she aimed at the stone and let it all go — all the fury, all the loneliness, all the months of trying — and the warm left her hand like a fist.

The stone split. Not a crack — a *split*. A piece the size of her head broke free and tumbled to the ground.

She stood there. Hand still raised. Palm still hot. The stone broken before her.

The grin came back. Wider than before. Wide enough to ache.

---

Her palm was still hot. Not warm — *hot*. Like holding it over a fire. The warm radiated from her skin in waves she could almost see.

Fire.

She gathered dry grass. A stick. The shredded bark she used for kindling. Piled it the way she had watched the People do it, before. Drew the warm back into her hand and let the anger build — but not rage this time. Frustration. Impatience. The low burn of wanting something and being denied.

She pressed her palm against the kindling. Too cold. She pushed harder. Drew more warm into her hand. Felt it build — hotter, hotter. The bark darkened under her palm. Curled. Began to smoke.

She pulled her hand away. The smoke thinned. Died.

Again. More this time. Hotter. She held her palm against the bark and pushed until sweat ran down her arm and the heat in her hand was nearly painful. The bark browned. Smoked. A thin orange line appeared at the edge — glowing, spreading.

She blew on it. Gentle. The way she had seen the women blow on embers.

Flame. Small, fragile, licking up through the bark and catching the dry grass. She fed it — gently, carefully, the way she remembered — until it crackled and grew and lit the cave wall with dancing orange.

She had not made fire since the People. Since before. She had eaten cold meat and raw roots for so long she had forgotten what cooked food tasted like.

She sat by the fire — *her* fire, made with nothing but the warm and her own hands — and watched it burn.

She was staying. And she was not cold anymore.
