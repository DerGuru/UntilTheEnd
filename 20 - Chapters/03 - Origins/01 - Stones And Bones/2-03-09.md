Mei learned to be careful.

Not all at once—it came in pieces, the way a child learns not to touch fire. First the cat, and the way the People had looked at her afterward. Then the wound, and Koh's flat voice saying don't show them. Then a dozen smaller things, each one a lesson:

When the water tasted wrong and Mei said so and nobody else could taste it, and they drank anyway, and two children were sick by nightfall—nobody thanked Mei. They looked at her the way they looked at the sick children. As if the knowing and the sickness were the same kind of wrong.

When Mei found the good flint—the kind that chipped clean and held an edge—by following the warm under the ground to a place nobody had thought to dig, one of the hunters took it from her hands without a word and walked away. Later she heard him tell another hunter that the ground had been obvious, that anyone could have seen it, that the girl was lucky, nothing more.

When she woke one night to the sound of something moving in the dark—something large, something that hummed hot and dense against her ribs—and she shook the nearest adult awake, and the adult shouted and the camp rose and they found bear tracks in the mud at the edge of the clearing. Nobody asked how she had known. Nobody wanted to know.

Mei understood. Not in words—she did not have the words yet—but in the body, in the way a hunted animal understands which paths are safe. The warm was useful when it served the People. The warm was dangerous when it marked her as different. And the line between useful and dangerous was not fixed. It moved, depending on who was watching, depending on how afraid they were, depending on whether the thing Mei did could be explained by luck or eyes or instinct—normal things, acceptable things, things that did not make the skin on the back of your neck rise.

She practiced being less.

She stopped pointing at things before they appeared. When the warm told her that an animal was near, she waited until someone else saw it—a hunter, a dog, anyone—and then she nodded along with the rest. When the ground hummed with water or stone, she wandered near it casually, as if by accident, and let others take credit for the finding.

She stopped talking about the warm.

She stopped moving it in front of others—the trick of pushing it from her chest to her hands, the way it flowed along her arms and collected in her palms. She did that only at night now, when the People were asleep and the coals were low and nobody could see her hands in the dark.

It was lonely.

Not the sharp loneliness of being alone—she was never alone, not physically. The People moved as a group, ate as a group, slept as a group. Bodies were always near. Warmth was always close.

But Mei lived with a secret now, and secrets are a kind of wall, and walls are a kind of distance, and distance, even when you are surrounded by people, is a kind of alone.

Koh helped.

Koh was the one person who knew, and knowing made Koh neither afraid nor impressed. To Koh, the warm was like Mei's habit of counting faces or noticing ant trails—interesting, sometimes useful, not a reason to treat her differently. When Mei found water, Koh drank. When Mei said bad place, Koh went around. Simple.

"You're doing it again," Koh said one afternoon. They were sitting at the edge of camp, watching the hunters come back.

"Doing what?"

"Making yourself small."

Mei looked down at herself. She was sitting the way she sat now—hunched, arms wrapped around her knees, taking up as little space as possible. She hadn't noticed.

"They watch me less when I'm small."

"They watch you the same. You just can't see it."

Mei said nothing. The hunters were coming in—four of them, carrying a deer between them, their arms bloody to the elbows. The big man walked behind, not carrying, just walking, and the others adjusted their pace to his without being told.

"The big man watches," Mei said quietly.

"The big man watches everything."

"He watches me different."

Koh was quiet for a moment. Then: "How different?"

"Like—" Mei searched for the word. "Like the cat watched. Before it turned away. Not hungry. Just… measuring."

The word wasn't quite right. Measuring was close, but what the big man did was something colder than measuring. When the warm pulsed near him, it didn't push back the way it pushed back near other strong people—the best hunters, the old man. Near the big man, the warm didn't push. It pressed. Heavy. Slow. Deliberate. Like being held underwater by a hand that wasn't in a hurry.

Koh picked up a stick and drew a line in the dirt. Then another. Then a circle.

"Stay near me," she said.

"I do."

"Nearer."

Mei moved closer. Their shoulders touched. Koh's warmth was ordinary—human-warm, skin-warm, the warmth of a body that had been sitting in the sun. It was the best warmth Mei knew.

They sat like that until the cooking fires were lit and the smell of roasting deer filled the camp, and then they went together to eat, and Mei sat where Koh sat, and she did not look at the big man, and the big man did not look at her, and the night was almost normal.

Almost.
