Koh told her by the river.

They were sitting on the flat stone—their stone, the one where they always sat when the camp was near water. Mei's feet hung over the edge. The river ran below, low and clear, and she could see the dark shapes of fish holding themselves still against the current. The air smelled of wet rock and the green, sharp scent of the plants that grew where the bank was soft.

Koh was not climbing. Koh was not talking. Koh was sitting with her knees pulled up and her arms wrapped around them, and she was staring at the water with the expression of someone who is looking at something but seeing something else.

Mei waited. Koh would talk when Koh wanted to talk.

It took a long time.

"He comes near," Koh said.

Mei looked at her. "Who?"

"Big man."

The words were flat. Koh said them the way you say a stone is heavy or water is wet—stating a fact, not complaining. But her hands were tight on her knees, the knuckles pale under the brown of her skin.

"Near how?"

"Stands. Watches. Comes where I go."

Mei felt the warm pulse in her chest. Not the warning-pulse—not the hot, dense alarm of a predator. Something else. Something slow and thick, like mud settling in a pool.

"When?"

"Many days. More now."

Koh unlocked one hand from her knees and made a gesture—a pulling gesture, toward herself. Then a pushing gesture, away. Toward, away. Toward, away. The meaning was clear even without words: he comes closer and she moves back and he comes closer again.

"Touched?" Mei asked.

"Arm. Here." Koh touched her own wrist. "Holds."

Mei's jaw tightened. The warm in her chest moved—up, thick, pressing against the inside of her ribs. She breathed through it.

"Others see?"

"Some. Nobody—" Koh made a gesture that was not a word. A turning away. A looking-somewhere-else. Mei understood: nobody did anything.

"The old man?"

"He is old. Big man is big."

They sat in silence. The river moved below. A fish broke the surface and vanished. The sound of the camp was behind them—distant, ordinary, like something happening in another world.

Koh was thirteen summers. The big man was grown—fully, heavily, immovably grown. And in the tribe, what the big man wanted, the big man took, because that was the shape of the world, and the shape of the world was not something two girls on a rock could change.

Mei reached over and took Koh's hand. Koh's fingers were cold.

"I won't let him."

"Can't."

"I won't."

"He's stronger. Stronger than anyone."

"I don't care."

Koh turned her head and looked at Mei. Her eyes were wet, but nothing fell. Koh did not cry. Koh had never cried in front of Mei, not once, not even when she fell from the high tree and her ankle swelled to the size of a fist and she had to be carried for three days.

"Mei."

"I won't let him."

Koh's mouth did something that was almost a smile and wasn't. She squeezed Mei's hand once, hard, and then let go.

They sat on the rock until the light changed and someone called them back, and they went back together, shoulders touching, and neither of them said anything else.

The warm in Mei's chest had not settled. It sat there, heavy and dense and waiting, like a fist that had not yet decided to open.
