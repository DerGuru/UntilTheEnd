She found them in the valley, near the water, where she always found them.

Smoke first. Then voices. Then the warm shapes of twenty-odd bodies, clustered around fires, moving in the patterns that were always the same: women working, children playing, men watching the tree line. The architecture of the group, repeated and repeated, as though every tribe in the world had been built from the same drawing.

Mei sat on the ridge and watched.

She did not go down.

The distance was wrong—too far to hear words, close enough to see shapes. Close enough to watch a woman lift a child onto her hip. Close enough to see a man throw a spear at a target and the others laugh when it missed. Close enough to see the fire spark and rise and settle.

She sat there all morning.

The sun crossed the sky. Shadows shortened, lengthened. The group moved through their day—a day like any day, full of the small business of staying alive together. A woman braided another woman's hair. A child fell in the water and came up screaming, and someone pulled it out and shook it and carried it to the fire. An old man sat apart from the others, working a piece of bone with a stone tool.

Mei's hands were still. Her breathing was even. The warm sat in her chest, quiet and neutral—it had nothing to say about this. No alarm. No pull. Just information: people are there. People are warm. People are alive.

She could go down.

She had done it before. Three times. She knew the approach—the slow walk, the offered meat, the empty hands. She knew how it went: the cautious welcome, the days of usefulness, the growing comfort, and then the moment when the mask slipped and the wall came up and the pointing began.

She could go down and try again.

She stood up. Took a step down the slope.

Stopped.

Her legs did not move. Not because they couldn't—the warm could have carried her down the mountain at a run if she'd asked. But something else held her. Something that lived in the same place as the warm but was not the warm. Something quieter. Older.

She looked at the group. The woman with the child on her hip. The man practicing his throw. The old one with the bone. She could see them clearly—every detail, every gesture, every small expression of a life lived among others.

She could not see herself among them.

Not because they would reject her—though they would. Not because she was different—though she was. Because the shape of her no longer fit the shape of that. The days in the cave, the winters, the hunts, the silence—they had carved her into something that did not match the pattern. A tool made for a different hand. A stone from a different mountain.

She stood on the slope for a long time.

The sun moved. The shadows moved. The group ate and talked and lived, and Mei watched them do it, and the watching was a kind of goodbye—not to them specifically, but to the idea of them. To the belief that somewhere, if she looked long enough, there was a fire with a space beside it that was shaped like her.

There wasn't. She knew that now. Not as a wound—the wound had scabbed and scarred in the other summers, the other descents, the other walls. This was something past wounding. This was the terrain after the wound: flat, hard, numb. A place where the thing that used to hurt had been replaced by something heavier and quieter, something she would later learn to call acceptance but which felt, in the moment, like nothing at all.

The fire below flickered. A child laughed—high, bright, carrying on the wind. Mei heard it clearly.

She turned.

The mountain was behind her. The cave was above her. The warm in her chest was full and steady and hers, and the mountain was hers, and the cave was hers, and that was what she had, and having what you have, and not pretending it was more than it was, and not mourning what it wasn't—that was a kind of living. Not the kind she would have chosen. But the kind she had.

She climbed.

She did not look back.

She would not go down again.
