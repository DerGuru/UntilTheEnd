She followed their trail the way she had followed a thousand animal trails—by warmth, by broken ground, by the smell of fear.

They had run northwest, toward the hills. Not together—the group had fragmented in the panic, splitting into clusters of three and four. Mei did not pursue the clusters. She pursued the line—the path of the largest warmth, the densest grouping, the direction where the most feet had gone.

She moved at a walk. There was no need to run. They were faster in the sprint—fear gave legs to people who had never run from anything except predators. But fear also made them stupid. They ran without direction, without plan, crashing through brush and tripping over roots. Mei walked the clean line, cutting corners, reading the terrain, and the distance between them shrank with every hundred paces.

The first hunter she caught had stopped to help another—the one with the broken wrist, from the fight on the ridge. They were in a clearing, the broken-wrist man sitting on a rock, the other wrapping his arm with a strip of hide. They saw Mei come out of the trees and they stopped moving.

Mei did not stop. She crossed the clearing in six strides. Her spear—the one she had taken from the camp—went through the standing man's thigh. He fell. She turned to the broken-wrist man, who was reaching for a stone with his good hand. She kicked the stone away and hit him in the temple with the shaft of the spear. Not hard enough to kill. Hard enough that he would not get up for a long time.

She pulled the spear free and walked on.

The trail led through a dry streambed, up a slope, through a stand of birch where the white trunks stood like bones in the grey light. Two more hunters waited there—intentionally this time. They had found their courage, or enough of it, and they stood with spears ready.

Mei came through the birch trees and read them the way she read animals. The one on the left was braced wrong—weight on his back foot, committed to a throw he had already decided on. The one on the right was better—balanced, waiting, his spear low and close.

The left one threw. Mei stepped sideways—half a step, no more—and the spear went past her shoulder. She did not flinch. She walked toward them, and the walking was worse than running would have been, because running was something they understood and walking was something that predators did when they knew the prey could not escape.

The right-hand hunter held his ground for three heartbeats. Then he broke and ran. The left-hand one tried to pull a knife from his belt. Mei hit him once, open-handed, in the chest. The warm was behind the blow—not projected, not a blast. Just there. Density. Weight. He went backward, hit a tree, and slid down the trunk.

She walked on.

The main group was ahead—she could feel them, a cluster of warmth, fifteen or more. They had stopped running. They had found a place to stand—a ridge, defensible, with the river at their back. Smart. If she had been an animal, it would have worked.

She came up the slope and saw them.

They were arranged in a half-circle at the top of the ridge. Women behind, children behind the women. The remaining hunters—five, maybe six—in front, with spears and stones and the desperate, bright-eyed look of men who know they are outmatched and are going to fight anyway.

And in the center, in front of all of them, the big man.

He held a spear—a good one, the best in the camp, heavy-shafted with a long flint head. He stood the way he had always stood: planted, enormous, filling the space around him with the weight of what he was.

Mei stopped at the base of the ridge. She looked up at him.

He looked down at her.

Five summers. Five summers since he had pointed her away, and she had gone, and the trees had taken her. Five summers in which she had become something he could not have imagined, and he had remained exactly what he was.

He was afraid. She could feel it—the warm told her, the way it told her everything. His heartbeat was fast. His breathing was shallow. But he was the kind of man who stood in front of his fear and dared it to move him, and his feet were planted and his spear was ready.

Behind him, faces. She saw them without looking for them. Koh—older, thinner, standing near the women, her eyes enormous. Mei's mother—grey-haired, small, clutching the arm of the woman beside her.

Mei climbed the ridge.
