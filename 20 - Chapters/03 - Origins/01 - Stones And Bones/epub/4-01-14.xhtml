Stones and Bones - 14

She followed the river because the river was the only thing that moved in the direction she needed to go. She needed to go somewhere. Standing still meant thinking. Thinking meant remembering. Remembering was a blade she was not ready to hold.

So she walked. She kept her eyes on the water, on the stones, on the patches of mud where animal tracks crossed. She counted the prints — fox, deer, something larger with clawed toes. Counting was safe. Counting did not hurt.

The water was low. It ran over flat stones with a sound like whispered conversation — the kind you could hear but not understand. Mei walked at the edge, where the mud was soft and her feet sank a little with each step. The mud was cold between her toes. She did not mind. Cold was a thing her body knew how to answer.

She ate from the bundle her mother had given her. Sparingly — a strip of dried meat in the morning, a few nuts at midday, a root chewed slowly in the fading light. The meat was tough, tasted of smoke and salt. Every time she put it in her mouth she smelled her mother’s hands. Every time she smelled her mother’s hands she stopped chewing and breathed through her nose until the feeling passed. The bundle was getting lighter. She ate carefully, making each piece last. But she could count the strips that remained, and the number was not enough.

The warm helped.

Not with the grief — the warm had nothing to say about grief. But it found water when the river branched and she did not know which way to go. It told her where the fish gathered in the shallows — places where the current slowed and the rocks made pockets of calm water. She could stand very still with a sharpened stick and wait for a shape to drift close enough. She missed more than she hit. But she hit enough.

The first fish she caught was small — barely a meal. She held it in her hands, still thrashing, its scales cold and slick against her palms. She killed it by striking its head against a stone. Ate it raw by the bank, the flesh tasting of river and blood. It was not good. It was food. She learned that the difference mattered less than she had thought.

She walked for days. The land changed — flatter first, then rising. Trees thickened. The air grew cooler and smelled of earth, decaying leaves, and the sharp, clean scent of stone.

On the fifth day — or the sixth, she had lost count — she saw smoke.

Her chest seized. Smoke meant fire. Fire meant people. People meant—

She crouched behind a fallen tree and watched.

A group. Smaller than her own — twelve, maybe fifteen. They sat around a fire near the river’s edge, in a clearing where the trees had been pushed back by old floods. Children played at the water. Women worked. A man stood at the edge of the camp, scanning the tree line with the casual vigilance of someone who had done it ten thousand times and expected nothing.

Mei watched for a long time. Her heart beat in a way that was not the warm — just the ordinary, animal rhythm of want and fear pressed together.

She stood up. Walked toward them. Slowly. Hands open, empty, visible — the way you approached any group that was not yours.

The man at the tree line saw her first. His posture changed — he straightened, and his hand moved to the spear leaning against the tree beside him. He said something. Others looked up.

Mei stopped. She was close enough now to see their faces — weathered, wary, not hostile. She showed her empty hands again. She was a child. She was alone. She was thin, dirty. Her feet were bleeding from days of walking over stone.

An older woman said something. The tone was a question — where, why, how. Mei did not understand the words. The shape of the sounds was different from her People’s sounds, arranged in patterns she did not recognize.

She pointed behind her. Then made a gesture — pushing, outward, away. The gesture for gone. For sent away.

The woman’s face changed. She looked at the man with the spear. The man looked at Mei. At her size. At the fact that she was alone. At the fact that a child alone meant something wrong.

The man spoke. Loud. Short. He pointed away from the camp — not the direction Mei had come from, just away.

“Go,” was not the word he used, but the meaning was the same.

Mei stood there. She did not beg — she did not have the words for it, and even if she had, something in her would not have bent that far. But she did not leave immediately. She looked at the fire. At the children by the water. At The women, the food, the ordinary, unbearable warmth of people together.

The man stepped forward. His spear was still against the tree, but his body said enough.

Mei turned and walked back to the river. The walk away was the worst part — worse than the rejection itself. Her back to them, the fire getting smaller behind her, the dark getting larger ahead.

She found another group two days later. Larger, noisier. She approached more carefully this time — waited at the edge, let them see her, let them decide. A woman gave her a piece of fish. A child stared at her. She sat at the perimeter and ate. The fish tasted of river and smoke. For one evening she was near the sound of voices.

In the morning they pointed her away.

She tried again. And again. Each time, the same pattern — a moment of uncertain welcome, a night or two of thin tolerance, and then the pointing. The away-gesture. The tightening of faces.

The third group was camped where three streams met. They let Mei sit by their fire for two nights. On the third morning, she woke and found the camp gone — packed, moved, vanished in the dark while she slept. They had left a strip of meat on a stone beside her. Pity or guilt, she could not tell which.

Where water is, there are people. Where people are, they don’t want me.

The understanding settled over her — damp, heavy, the weight of twelve days and four groups and not one that wanted her for more than a night.

Mei stood on the riverbank on a morning that was colder than the mornings before. The leaves on the trees had begun to turn — gold at the edges, curling inward. Behind her, the river ran south, toward warmth, toward water, toward every group that had sent her away.

She turned and looked up.

Mountains. Grey, green, and rising above the tree line into haze. No smoke. No voices. No one.

She started climbing.

The air thinned. The trees changed — shorter, wind-bent, clinging to the rock with the stubbornness of things that grew where nothing should. Mei’s legs burned. Her hands were raw from gripping stone. But the burning was clean. It did not come with the look on people’s faces when they pointed away.
