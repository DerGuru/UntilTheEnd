Stones and Bones - 25

The cat found her on the ridge where the wind never stopped. She had been tracking an ibex herd — six animals, moving in a line along the skyline, their warmth bright and clear against the cold air. She was above them, on the narrow spine of rock that connected two peaks, and she was thinking about angle, distance, which one was limping, whether the wind would shift.

The cat’s warmth hit her chest like a thrown stone.

Close. Impossibly close. She had been focused forward — every thread of the warm pushed to the ibex, to the angle, to the kill. Behind her had been silence. Twenty paces. Moving fast.

Mei turned.

The cat was tawny, low, and long. Already in the air.

She had no time to throw. She dropped flat. The cat sailed over her — she felt the displaced air, felt claws rake her shoulder through the skin she wore, felt the weight of the animal pass above her like a falling tree.

It landed. Turned. Its eyes were amber, flat, and carried no emotion at all.

Mei rolled to her feet. Her spear was in her hand — the heavy one, the close-range one. Blood ran down her shoulder where the claws had caught, warm and fast. The warm in her chest flared, and she let it — let it flood her arms, her legs, her senses. The world sharpened. The cat’s heartbeat was a drum. Its breath was visible — not steam, but heat, shimmering against the cold air.

The cat charged.

Mei braced and thrust. The spear caught the cat in the chest — not deep, not killing, but enough to turn it. The animal screamed, twisted. One paw came around, hit the shaft. The wood snapped. Mei stumbled back with a broken stick in her hand, the cat between her and the cliff edge, the ibex forgotten, the world reduced to one simple equation: kill or die.

Not now.

Not a thought. Not a decision. Something deeper — below the warm, below the bone, in a place she had not known existed until this moment. A refusal. Not of death specifically — death was a thing she understood, had understood since Ren did not come back from the hunt. A refusal of this death. This moment. This ending.

Not now.

The warm answered. Not as it usually answered — flowing where she pushed it, doing what she told it. This was different. The warm moved on its own. It surged through her — all of it, every drop, every thread — gathered in her center, then expanded outward, not as a blast (she had not done that since the big man) but as a wave. A hardening. Her body became — more. Not stronger exactly. Denser. As though the space between her bones had been filled with stone, the space between her muscles filled with iron, and she was not a girl anymore but a thing that could not be broken.

The cat hit her. Full force — a hundred-some pounds of predator, claws out, jaws wide. It hit her and she did not fall. She slid backward — feet cutting grooves in the scree — but she stayed upright. The impact that should have crushed her ribs did not crush them. The claws that should have opened her belly scraped across skin that was harder than it had any right to be.

The cat was confused. Animals did not do this. Prey did not stand. Prey fell.

Mei grabbed the broken spear shaft and drove it forward. Into the cat’s open mouth. Through the soft palate. Deep.

The cat made a sound that was not a scream. It was a gurgling, wet, terrible sound. It thrashed. Its claws raked her arms. The blood — hers and the cat’s — mixed on the stone. Mei held on. Not because she was brave. Because her hands would not let go.

The cat died.

It took a long time. Longer than the ibex, longer than any animal she had killed. It died in convulsions, its body refusing to accept what had already happened. Mei held the broken shaft, watched, and felt the warmth of the animal fade in her chest — bright, bright, bright, then dim, then gone.

Silence.

Mei let go. She sat down on the ridge, heavily, and looked at her hands. They were red. Both of them. The blood was on her arms, her chest, her face. Her own and the cat’s, mixed so thoroughly that she could not tell which was which.

The warm retreated. The hardening dissolved — slowly, like ice melting — and her body became her body again: soft, breakable, human. The shoulder wound opened properly now. The claw-marks on her arms and belly began to bleed in earnest. The pain arrived — all of it, at once, like a wave that had been held back by a dam that was no longer there.

She lay back on the cold stone and stared at the sky.

Not now.

She did not know what it was. Not the warm — she knew the warm, knew its rhythms and its limits. This was something else. Something that lived beneath the warm, or beside it, or inside it — she could not tell. It had spoken with her voice, in a language she did not know she spoke. The warm had listened. Her body had become something impossible, and she had not died.

The healing started. The shoulder wound closed first — slowly, the edges pulling together like a mouth closing. Then the claw marks, shallower, sealing in the cold air. The deeper wounds would take longer. Days, maybe.

She lay on the ridge until the light changed. Then she stood — slowly, carefully, every movement a conversation between what hurt and what could bear it. She walked back to the cave.

The warm of the cave met her. Dense. Patient. She collapsed onto the stone floor, pressed her face against the warm rock, and let the cave’s warmth pour into her, filling the places the fight had emptied.

Not now, she thought. Or did not think. It was not a thought. It was the shape of what she was.

She slept. The warm healed her. When she woke, the cat was already becoming a memory, and the thing that had kept her alive was already retreating to the deep place where it lived, waiting.
