Stones and Bones - 17

Not in the way that a person teaches — with words, gestures, and the expectation of understanding. The cave taught the way stone teaches water to flow: by being there, unchanging, and letting the thing that moves find its own path.

Mei sat.

Cross-legged on the smooth stone, palms on her knees, eyes closed. She had nothing else to do. The hunger was a constant now — not sharp enough to force her out, not gone enough to forget. The water from the basin was clean, cold. It tasted of mineral and deep places. It kept her alive. But she could not eat stone, and the warm, for all its brightness, was not food.

So she sat. She listened. The warm taught her.

She breathed in. The warm from the cave came with the breath — not air, not temperature, but something that rode alongside both. It entered through her mouth, her skin, the soles of her feet, the backs of her hands. It flowed down into the place behind her ribs where the warm lived. It settled there. It stayed.

She breathed out. The warm in her chest pulsed — a slow expansion, like a heart beating. Not losing anything. Just moving. Circulating.

She breathed in. More came.

She breathed out. The pulse steadied.

In. Out. In. Out. Each breath, the warm inside her grew a little denser. A little brighter. A little more like the warm of the cave itself — vast, patient, and full of something she could not name.

She did this for hours. She did not count them. Time in the cave was not time as she understood it — marked by sun, moon, and the movement of the People from one place to another. Time in the cave was measured in breaths, in pulses, in the slow accretion of the warm building inside her like silt building in a riverbed.

On the first day — or what she thought was the first day — she learned that she could pull.

Not just breathe in and let the warm come. Pull. Actively. Reaching out with the thing inside her chest, like reaching out a hand, drawing the warm of the cave toward her. It came — faster than breathing, more concentrated, a stream where before there had been mist. It poured into the place behind her ribs. The place glowed. Her whole body warmed, and for a moment she felt as though she were made of light.

Then her head spun, her stomach cramped, and she had to stop. Too much. Too fast. Like drinking water too quickly after a long thirst — the body rebelled against the abundance.

She learned the pace. Slow. Steady. Not greedy. The warm was not going anywhere. It would be here tomorrow, the day after, and the day after that. She did not need to grab it. She needed to let it come.

On the second day, she learned to hold.

Before, the warm had been a current — always moving, flowing through her, never still. When she stopped pulling, it drifted back to the cave, returning to its source. Now she found the trick of keeping it. Closing something — not her hands, not her mouth. Something inside. A gate she had not known existed. She closed it, and the warm stayed. It sat in her chest like a coal in a banked fire — hot, dense, waiting.

She opened the gate. The warm flowed out. She closed it. The warm stayed.

Open. Close. Open. Close.

By the third day, she had enough warm inside her to push it wherever she wanted. Not the clumsy, desperate shove that had sent the big man flying. This was precise — a thin stream, directed, controlled. She pushed it to her hands. They grew warm. Not glowing, not visible — but warmer than the stone, warmer than the air. She held them up in the dark and felt the heat radiating from her palms like two small fires.

She pushed it to her eyes. The darkness thinned. Not light — she was not creating light. But the dark became less absolute, less solid, as though her eyes had learned to see with something other than light. Shapes resolved. The walls of the cave, the texture of the stone, the glint of water in the basin. All visible, faintly, in a way that owed nothing to the sun.

She pushed it to her ears. The cave erupted in sound. Water moving through cracks deep in the mountain. The scratch of insects in crevices. Her own heartbeat, loud and steady. Below that — below everything — a low, constant vibration that was the mountain itself, the earth itself, the hum that she had been listening to since she was eight summers old and had never truly heard until now.

She pulled the warm back. The silence returned. She pushed it out. The world opened.

She spent days like this — pushing, pulling, holding, releasing. Learning the warm the way she had once learned to walk: through repetition, failure, and the body’s stubborn insistence on getting better.

On what might have been the fifth day, or the seventh, the hunger drove her out.

She stood at the cave entrance, blinking in light that felt like a blow. The sun was high. The air was cold, smelling of pine, frost, and distance. Her legs were weak from sitting, her stomach a fist of emptiness.

But the warm inside her was full. Brighter, denser than it had ever been. And when she breathed the mountain air, she felt the warm in it — thin, scattered, nothing like the cave, but there. Everywhere. The whole world was threaded with it.

She looked down the slope. Somewhere below, in the scrub and the rocks, something lived. Something she could hunt.

She started down.
