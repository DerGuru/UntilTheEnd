Stones and Bones - 09

Not all at once — it came in pieces, the way a child learns not to touch fire. First the cat, and how the People had looked at her afterward. Then the wound, and Koh’s flat voice saying don’t show them. Then a dozen smaller things, each one a lesson:

When the water tasted wrong, Mei said so, and nobody else could taste it — they drank anyway. Two children were sick by nightfall. Nobody thanked Mei. They looked at her the way they looked at the sick children. As if the knowing and the sickness were the same kind of wrong.

Mei sat by the fire that night and felt the understanding arrive — not in words, but in the body, in the tightness of her chest. She had tried to help. She had been right. It did not matter. Being right was not the same as being safe.

When Mei found the good flint — the kind that chipped clean and held an edge — by following the warm under the ground to a place nobody had thought to dig, one of the hunters took it from her hands without a word and walked away. Later she heard him tell another hunter that the ground had been obvious, that anyone could have seen it, that the girl was lucky, nothing more.

Lucky. The word stuck in her. She had followed the warm down through the earth, had felt the density of the stone before her hands touched it. But the hunter who took it called it luck, and luck was an explanation that did not make people afraid.

When she woke one night to the sound of something moving in the dark — something large, something that hummed hot and dense against her ribs — she shook the nearest adult awake. The adult shouted. The camp rose. They found bear tracks in the mud at the edge of the clearing. Nobody asked how she had known. Nobody wanted to know.

The camp went back to sleep. Mei did not. She lay in the dark and listened to the silence that follows panic — the particular, charged quiet of people who have been woken by fear and are pretending they have gone back to sleep. She could hear their breathing. None of it was slow.

Mei understood. Not in words — she did not have the words yet — but in the body, in the way a hunted animal understands which paths are safe. The warm was useful when it served the People. The warm was dangerous when it marked her as different. And the line between useful and dangerous was not fixed. It moved, depending on who was watching, on how afraid they were, on whether the thing Mei did could be explained by luck, eyes, or instinct — normal things, acceptable things, things that did not make the skin on the back of your neck rise.

She practiced being less.

She stopped pointing at things before they appeared. When the warm told her that an animal was near, she waited until someone else saw it — a hunter, a dog, anyone — and then she nodded along with the rest. When the ground hummed with water or stone, she wandered near it casually, as if by accident, and let others take credit for the finding.

Each time was a small death. Each time she swallowed the knowledge — that was water, that was danger, that was food — and kept her face still, she felt something close inside her. A door she was pulling shut on herself, from the inside.

She got good at it. That was the worst part. Good at the shrug, the blank look, the casual glance that said I don’t know, I just guessed. The lie became a reflex. The easier it got, the heavier it felt.

She stopped talking about the warm. Not because anyone told her to — nobody had said don’t speak of it. The silence around the subject was its own instruction, as clear as a hand held up.

She stopped moving it in front of others — the trick of pushing it from her chest to her hands, how it flowed along her arms and collected in her palms. She did that only at night now, when the People were asleep, the coals were low, and nobody could see her hands in the dark.

She would sit with her back against a tree, pressing her palms to the earth. The warm responded — rising to meet her, flowing through her fingers, pooling in her palms. She practiced moving it: chest to arm, arm to fingers, fingers to nothing. When she opened her hands wide, she could feel the air change — warmer, thicker, alive. It was beautiful. It was hers. She did it in the dark because the dark was the only place where she could be all of herself without consequence.

It was lonely.

Not the sharp loneliness of being alone — she was never alone, not physically. The People moved as a group, ate as a group, slept as a group. Bodies were always near. Warmth was always close.

But Mei lived with a secret now, and secrets are a kind of wall, and walls are a kind of distance, and distance, even when you are surrounded by people, is a kind of alone.

Koh helped.

Koh was the one person who knew, and knowing made Koh neither afraid nor impressed. To Koh, the warm was like Mei’s habit of counting faces or noticing ant trails — interesting, sometimes useful, not a reason to treat her differently. When Mei found water, Koh drank. When Mei said bad place, Koh went around. Simple.

Koh did not understand the warm. She could not feel it, could not see it, had no frame for what it meant. But she understood Mei — how Mei’s voice got quieter when people watched, how her shoulders hunched at the fire, how she ate less and slept further from the center. Koh noticed all of this because Koh noticed everything about the people she loved, even without words for the noticing.

“You’re doing it again,” Koh said one afternoon. They were sitting at the edge of camp, watching the hunters come back.

“Doing what?”

“Making yourself small.”

Mei looked down at herself. She was hunched, arms wrapped around her knees, taking up as little space as possible. She hadn’t noticed.

“They watch me less when I’m small.”

“They watch you the same. You just can’t see it.”

Mei said nothing. The hunters were coming in — four of them, carrying a deer between them, their arms bloody to the elbows. The big man walked behind, not carrying, just walking, and the others adjusted their pace to his without being told.

“The big man watches,” Mei said quietly.

“The big man watches everything.”

“He watches me different.”

Koh was quiet for a moment. Then: “How different?”

“Like — ” Mei searched for the word. “Like the cat watched. Before it turned away. Not hungry. Just… measuring.”

The word wasn’t quite right. Measuring was close, but what the big man did was something colder than measuring. When the warm pulsed near him, it didn’t push back as it did near other strong people — the best hunters, the old man. Near the big man, the warm didn’t push. It pressed. Heavy. Slow. Deliberate. Like being held underwater by a hand that wasn’t in a hurry.

Koh picked up a stick and drew a line in the dirt. Then another. Then a circle.

“Stay near me,” she said.

“I do.”

“Nearer.”

Mei moved closer. Their shoulders touched. Koh’s warmth was ordinary — human-warm, skin-warm, the warmth of a body that had been sitting in the sun. It was the best warmth Mei knew.

They sat like that until the cooking fires were lit and the smell of roasting deer filled the camp. They went together to eat. Mei sat where Koh sat. She did not look at the big man. The big man did not look at her. The night was almost normal.

Almost.

Koh shifted in her sleep and muttered something — a half-word, swallowed by the dark. Mei listened to it. One voice. One person who did not look at her differently. One shoulder that still bumped hers in the morning.

The warm pulsed in her chest, slow and constant. Outside, an owl called — two notes, low, patient. The night answered with silence.
