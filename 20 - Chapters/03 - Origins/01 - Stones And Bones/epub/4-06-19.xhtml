Stones and Bones - 19

Not all at once — not a single morning where she woke and decided. It built. Day by day, night by night. The cave was safe. The cave was warm. The cave was hers in a way that nothing had ever been hers. But the cave did not breathe beside her. It did not laugh. It did not bump her shoulder or say tomorrow? The cave was a place, not a person. Mei was twelve summers old and still, underneath everything she was becoming, a child who needed the sound of someone else’s voice.

She had been alone for — how long? She counted by moons. Three? Four? The ibex meat was gone. She had hunted again — smaller things, rabbits, birds, once a marmot that she caught by hand when the warm told her exactly where it was hiding. She knew the mountain now. Its ridges, its valleys, the places where water gathered, the places where predators moved. The warm mapped it for her, a living chart of heat, life, and emptiness.

But the map had no people in it.

One morning she woke and the silence was too much. She packed what she could carry — dried meat, a skin of water, the good spear she had spent days making. She looked at the cave. The warm pulsed there — steady, patient, the heartbeat of the mountain.

She would come back. She knew this without thinking. But she needed something the cave could not give her.

She climbed down.

The descent took two days. The air warmed. The trees returned — first the twisted, wind-bent kind, then broader, softer. Birds called. Insects hummed. The world below the mountain was loud in a way she had forgotten, full of small lives and moving things.

On the third day, she found the river.

It was not the same river — or maybe it was, further along, wider. It didn’t matter. What mattered was the smoke.

Her chest tightened — not the warm, just the ordinary animal response. She had been alone for four moons. Four moons of stone, wind, the warm. And here was smoke.

A group. Fifteen, maybe twenty. Camped at a bend where the river widened into a shallow pool. They had fires, shelters made of branches and skins. Children played at the water’s edge. The sound of voices carried across the flat ground. Mei stood at the tree line and listened to it the way a starving person smells cooking food.

She approached differently this time. Not desperate. Not small. She walked upright, spear held low, not threatening but present. She was taller now — the moons alone had stretched her, thinned her face, hardened the lines of her body. She did not look like a lost child anymore. She did not know what she looked like. She knew what she wanted — voices, fire that someone else had built, the ordinary weight of a body sleeping beside hers. She wanted it badly enough that the wanting was a taste in her mouth, metallic and sharp.

The group watched her come. A man stood up — one of the younger ones, alert, a spear in his hand. But he did not raise it. He watched her the way you watch a wolf that walks close but does not bare its teeth: wary, curious, waiting.

Mei stopped at the edge of their camp. She set her spear on the ground — not surrendering it, just making it horizontal. She opened her hands. Showed the dried meat. An offering.

The man looked at the meat. Looked at her. Looked at someone behind him — an older woman, thick-set, with grey streaking her hair. A decision-maker.

The woman came forward. She looked at Mei for a long time. Then she took the meat, sniffed it, broke a piece off and chewed.

She pointed at a spot near the edge of the camp. The spot said: you can sit here. Not: you belong.

Mei sat.

Days passed. Mei worked. She gathered — berries, roots, the things women gathered. She carried water. She helped with the shelters when the group moved camp. She made herself useful in the quiet, invisible way she had learned from watching her own People: do more than your share, ask for nothing, occupy the smallest space possible.

The group tolerated her. Not warmly — the space around her stayed wider than the space between other bodies. But they did not point her away.

On the second day, she helped carry water from the river. Heavy skins, slung over the shoulder. The woman walking beside her did not speak, but she adjusted her pace so they walked together — the wordless synchronization of shared labor. When they reached the camp, the woman took the full skin from Mei’s shoulder without asking. Their hands touched. The woman did not pull back.

On the third day, the children came closer. Three of them — two boys and a girl, the youngest no older than five summers. They crouched at the edge of Mei’s space and watched her with the serious, total attention children give to anything new. The girl held out a stone. Small, round, bright with quartz.

Mei took it. Held it. Gave it back.

The girl grinned and ran away. The boys followed. An hour later they returned with two more stones and a piece of bark. Mei arranged the stones on the bark. The children laughed — the real laugh, the full-body laugh of children who have found something worth laughing at. It was the sound she had missed most. Not words. Not sense. Just the sound, close enough to feel in her chest.

On the fourth evening, the decision-maker brought her food. Not sent — brought. Walked over, set a strip of cooked meat on the ground beside Mei, walked back without words. The gesture was small. Mei ate the meat slowly. It tasted of salt, smoke, and the impossible sweetness of being fed by someone who had chosen to feed her.

She helped build a shelter when the group moved camp half a day’s walk downstream. She carried poles, held skins, pulled rope tight. The work was ordinary. Her body knew how to do it — muscle-memory from eleven summers of watching the People build. When the shelter stood, one of the men looked at her and gave a short nod. Nothing more. It was enough to make her chest ache.

At night she lay at the edge of the camp and listened to the breathing. Twenty lungs, in and out, the rhythm not synchronized but close — a living sound, a group at rest. She had forgotten what this felt like. Not the warmth of bodies nearby. The safety of it. The permission to close your eyes and trust that someone else was watching.

The decision-maker still watched from a distance with an expression that was not hostile, not warm. Evaluating. Measuring. Deciding.

For five days, it was almost enough.

On the sixth morning, a snake came to the camp.

Mei felt it before it reached the sleeping area — a thin, sharp line of warm, fast-moving, close to the ground. She was already awake, sitting at the edge of the camp in the pre-dawn grey. She felt it. She stood. She said:

“Snake.”

Her voice was too loud. The sound cut the morning silence like a thrown stone. Heads turned. Bodies stiffened.

The snake emerged from the grass near a sleeping child — long, patterned, its head raised. The child was still asleep. The mother was asleep.

Mei moved. Fast — faster than anyone expected, faster than she should have been able to move. She crossed the camp in six strides, scooped the child off the ground, and stepped back. The snake struck at empty air.

The camp erupted. Someone killed the snake — a spear, a rock, she didn’t see. The mother grabbed the child from Mei’s arms. There was screaming, chaos, the particular panic of people who have woken to danger.

Then it settled. The snake was dead. The child was fine. And everyone was looking at Mei.

The decision-maker looked longest.

How had she known? How had she moved that fast? How had she been awake, in the right place, at the right moment? The questions were not spoken, but they hung in the air like the smell of the dead snake.

Mei felt it coming — the same shift, the same tightening. The same invisible wall going up between her and them. She had seen it before. She would see it again.

In the hours after the snake, she watched the camp reorganize around her absence. The woman who had carried water beside her did not come near. The children were kept back. The decision-maker spoke to two of the men, heads close, voices low. Nobody looked at Mei directly. But the space around her grew with every hour.

She did not wait for the pointing.

She picked up her spear. Took her water skin. The round quartz stone was still in her palm — the girl’s gift from the third day, which she had been turning in her hand without thinking. She set it on the ground where she had been sitting.

She looked at the group one last time — the children half-hidden behind their mothers, the fires, the woman who had carried water beside her. Five days of almost.

She turned and walked toward the mountains.

The climb back was familiar. The trees thinned. The air cooled. The rock rose. And the warm in her chest, which had dimmed during the days below — stretched thin by the noise and the nearness of so many humming lives — grew denser with each step toward the cave. Each step away from the group was a loss she could measure — the voices fading, the smoke thinning, the warmth of bodies dropping out of her range one by one until there was only the mountain, the wind, the warm growing louder.

She squeezed through the entrance. The warm of the cave met her — full, steady, unchanged. Waiting.

She sat on the smooth stone. Pressed her palms to the floor. The warm flooded in — fast, eager, as though it had missed her.

Her eyes burned. She pressed her palms harder against the stone and breathed through it.

Five days. It had taken five days for her body to remember what it felt like to be near people. Now the remembering was worse than the forgetting.

The cave was warm. The cave was safe. The cave did not care what she was.

It was not enough. But it was what she had.
