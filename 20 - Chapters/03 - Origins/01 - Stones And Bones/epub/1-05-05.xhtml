Stones and Bones - 05

The deer was there before Mei saw it. She felt it first — a hum in her chest, low and alive, like the sound a wasp makes inside a closed hand. Not a sound she heard with her ears. Something else. Something that pushed against her ribs from inside, warm, steady, and pointed, like a finger saying: there.

The bushes moved. A shape stepped out — small, brown, ears up. It stood in the clearing for three heartbeats, nose working, then it turned and was gone.

“There,” Mei said to Koh. “Deer.”

Koh looked where Mei pointed. The clearing was empty.

“Gone.”

“I know. But it was there.”

“How did you know?”

Mei opened her mouth. Closed it. How did she explain the hum? She didn’t have a word for it. It wasn’t a sound. It wasn’t a feeling exactly. It wasn’t the warm-under-the-earth from before, though it was like that. The same family of things. A language she could hear and no one else seemed to notice.

“Heard it,” she said. It was the closest word she had.

“I heard nothing.”

“Different hearing.”

Koh shrugged, moved on. The morning continued.

But the hum didn’t stop. It was there when they walked — faint, backgrounded, like the smell of smoke when you’ve been near a fire all day. Living things had it. The People had it, each one a slightly different note, a different texture. The women walking ahead hummed in one register. The children in another. Even the insects had something — tiny, thin, barely there, like sparks.

Mei paid attention now.

When they crossed a ridge and descended into a narrow valley, the hum changed. Not louder, not softer — emptier. The trees here were the same, the earth was the same, the sky was the same. But something was missing. Mei slowed. Her skin prickled. The air tasted of nothing, where before it had tasted of green, rot, and water.

She stepped sideways, away from the path the others were walking. The empty feeling pulled at her like a current — not painful, but wrong. Cold, in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. Like a hole in the world where something should have been.

She said nothing. The others walked through without noticing. Their feet crunched on the same leaves, their shoulders brushed the same branches. Whatever Mei felt, they did not.

She hurried to catch up.

That evening, when the fires were lit and the smell of roasting meat filled the camp, Mei sat near the edge of the group. The big man was across the clearing, eating. She could see his shoulders move as he tore meat from bone.

And she could feel him.

The hum in her chest, the one she now carried everywhere like a second heartbeat, grew louder near him. Denser. Hotter. Not the gentle warmth of the underground water or the light flutter of the deer. This was thick — a pressure, like standing too close to a fire, like a hand pressing on her chest from outside.

She shifted back. The pressure eased.

She shifted forward. It returned.

In the firelight, the big man was just a man — broad, loud, chewing with his mouth open. But the hum said something different. The hum said: more. More than the hunters beside him. More than the old man. More than anyone.

Mei looked away. She did not like the feeling. Not because it hurt, but because it was too much, and too-much was a thing she was learning to recognize.

Later, after food, after the fires burned low, Mei sat beside her mother and tried to explain.

“Things hum,” she said.

Her mother looked at her. Waited.

“Animals. In here.” Mei pressed her hand to her chest. “Before I see them. The ground is warm. Some places are cold. Not cold-cold. Different cold.”

Her mother’s face did not change. Not dismissal — blankness. The expression of someone hearing words that don’t arrange into meaning.

“Mmh,” her mother said, and went back to scraping the edge of a blade.

Mei found Koh.

“Animals hum. Ground warm. Some places cold.”

Koh tilted her head. “Warm ground?”

“Under. You can’t feel it.”

“Cold places?”

“Empty. Wrong.”

Koh considered this with the seriousness of someone considering whether a rock was the right shape for throwing.

“Mei-thing,” she said finally. She meant it with affection — the way she might say Koh-thing about her own habit of climbing everything she saw. A quirk. A trait. Nothing to worry about.

Mei nodded. Mei-thing. She accepted this. It was easier than the alternative — that the world spoke to her in a language that existed for no one else.

The fire hissed. Something popped inside a log, sending up a bright shower of sparks that died before they reached the canopy. Mei’s mother called from across the camp — a single sound, not a word exactly, but a shape of voice that meant come here, sleep now.

Mei went. She lay down in her place — between her mother and the edge of the group. She stared at the stars through the gaps in the leaves. The hum was still there. Faint. Background. The People around her, each with their own warmth, their own texture.

And the big man, far across the camp, burning hotter than all of them.

She closed her eyes and let the sound of breathing, of fire, carry her down into sleep.
