Stones and Bones - 02

They had been out since midmorning, following the creek upstream to where the trees grew thicker and the ground was soft with fallen leaves. Koh walked ahead — stepping on roots, testing branches, making enough noise to warn off anything with ears. Mei followed, eyes on the ground. She watched the tracks in the mud, the scratches on bark, the small disturbances that told her what had passed this way before them. A fox, from the narrow prints near the water. Something larger further upstream, from the bent grass.

They found the nest because of the ants. Mei saw them first — a line of black bodies moving up a birch trunk, each one carrying a pale grain twice the size of its head. She stopped, crouched, pressed her nose close to the bark. The ants smelled of something sharp and sour, like crushed leaves left in rain.

“Koh.”

“What?”

“Look.”

Koh came back, because Koh always came back when Mei said look, even when what Mei wanted to show her was a beetle or a pattern in the mud or how light fell through a certain leaf. Koh would look, shrug, and say something like “Good beetle” or “Bad mud.” That would be enough.

But this time Mei pointed up.

“They carry.”

“Ants carry. That is what ants do.”

“No. They carry from somewhere.” Mei followed the line with her finger — up the trunk, along a branch, across to the next tree. There, where the branches forked and a tangle of dead leaves had collected in the crook, a dark shape. Too large for a knot. Too round.

“Nest,” Mei said.

Koh squinted. Then her eyes went wide.

“Eggs?”

“Maybe.”

They looked at each other. Koh grinned. Mei grinned back.

The tree was not tall, but the lowest branch was higher than Koh could reach, and Koh was the tallest of the children. Mei walked around the trunk, pressing her palms against the bark, feeling for holds. Rough here. Smooth there. A ridge where the bark had split and hardened again, just wide enough for a toe.

“I go,” Koh said.

“You fall.”

“I do not fall.”

“You fell at the river.”

“The river was wet.”

Mei pointed at the ridge in the bark, then at a knob higher up, then at the branch. “There. There. There.”

Koh studied the path Mei had traced. Her lips moved as she counted the holds — ridge, knob, branch. Then she tried it. Her foot found the ridge, her hand found the knob, and she pulled herself up with the graceless efficiency of someone who trusted her body more than she planned her route. The branch bent under her weight. She crawled along it, hand over hand, until she reached the fork.

Mei watched from below, her neck craned back, the sharp smell of tree sap and crushed ants filling her nose.

“Three,” Koh called down.

“Three?”

“Three eggs. Big.”

Koh tucked them into the fold of her wrap, one at a time — careful, slow, her tongue poking out between her teeth, as it did when she concentrated. Then she climbed down, faster than she had gone up, and dropped the last body-length to the ground.

She held them out. Three eggs, mottled brown and cream, each one the size of Mei’s fist. Warm. Koh was grinning — the full, wild grin that meant she had done something worth doing. Her wrap was stained with bark dust, a scratch on her shoulder from a branch she had not bothered to avoid.

“How did you know?” Koh asked.

Mei shrugged. “Ants go where food is.”

“I do not watch ants.”

“I do.”

Mei did not know how to explain it. She watched things, and the things she watched told her other things. It was not a skill. It was how her eyes worked.

Koh laughed — the big, sudden laugh that startled birds, made the adults frown — and she handed Mei an egg. They walked back toward camp. The eggs were warm against Mei’s palms. The afternoon sun was warm on her shoulders. Somewhere behind them a bird sang three notes and stopped. Everything was exactly as it should be.

They walked back through the trees, the eggs warm against their bodies. Koh carried two, tucked into the fold of her wrap. Mei carried one in both hands, feeling the warmth of it, the faint weight. The shell was thinner than she expected — she could feel the life inside, the heat.

The sun was lower now. Shadows stretched across the ground. Somewhere behind them, the bird whose nest they had robbed was calling — three notes, fast, then silence. Then three more.

They came back to the People with dirty knees, three eggs, and no explanation for where they had been. Mei’s mother looked at the eggs, looked at Mei, looked at Koh. Shook her head once. Said nothing.

Koh cracked her egg on a stone and drank it raw, the yolk running down her chin. Mei ate hers slower, tipping the shell to let the white slide onto her tongue. It tasted of salt and something else — the faint mineral flavor of the nest, of the tree, of the place where it had been made.

The old man watched them from across the fire. Koh brought him the third egg — she always volunteered for the giving — and placed it in his hands with the careful seriousness of a child presenting something important. He looked at the egg, then at the two girls. His mouth moved in a way that might have been a smile. He cracked it into his palm and drank.

Koh wiped her chin with the back of her hand and bumped Mei’s shoulder.

“Tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow.”

They did not need to say more. Tomorrow meant: again. Out there, together, looking for whatever the world had hidden and Mei could find. This was how it worked. Koh was the legs and the nerve. Mei was the eyes.

They had not chosen this. It had happened the way growth happens — slowly, without discussion, until one day it was simply true.

Neither of them knew this was remarkable. It was simply who they were.
