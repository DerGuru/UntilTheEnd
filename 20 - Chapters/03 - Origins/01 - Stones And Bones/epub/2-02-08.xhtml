Stones and Bones - 08

The thorn went in deep. Mei had been climbing — a dead tree, half-fallen against a rock face, the bark stripped and the wood smooth with rain. Koh was already at the top, of course, because Koh was always at the top first, because Koh climbed the way other people breathed. Mei was halfway up when her foot slipped and her forearm dragged across a branch where something sharp still grew.

The pain was bright and immediate. A line of red opened from her wrist to her elbow — not deep, not dangerous, but long. The blood came quickly, running down her arm, dripping from her fingers in fat drops that hit the pale wood below.

“Mei!”

Koh scrambled down. Mei sat on the branch, arm held out, watching the blood with the calm fascination of a child who has not yet learned to panic at the sight of her own insides.

It hurt. But pain was familiar — scraped knees, bruised shins, the endless minor damage of a body that had not yet learned its edges. This was bigger than most. The cut was ragged, and the blood was not stopping.

“Come down,” Koh said from below. “Show the women.”

Mei came down. One of the older women — the one the children went to when things hurt — looked at the cut, pressed leaves against it, bound it with a strip of skin. She clucked her tongue as she always did, which meant hold still, which meant this will heal, which meant stop climbing dead trees you foolish girl.

That night, Mei’s arm throbbed. The warmth beneath the wrapping pulsed in time with her heartbeat. She gritted her teeth and kept still, because crying was for children who had not learned that crying did not make things stop hurting.

She lay on her side, the arm cradled against her chest. Around her, the camp breathed — the familiar, rhythmic sound of people sleeping. Underneath the pain she felt something else: the warm, moving in her arm with a purpose she had not given it. It was not painful. It was warm, steady, insistent — traveling along the cut like a hand smoothing cloth.

She slept.

She woke before dawn.

The pain was gone.

Not lessened. Not dulled. Gone, the way fire is gone when you pour water on it. No ache, no throb, no heat. Just skin.

Mei unwound the binding in the grey light, carefully, peeling the leaves away from where they had stuck to dried blood. She expected the red line. She expected the raw, wet look of an open wound.

The cut was closed.

Not healed — not the way wounds healed, slowly, with scabs, itching, and new pink skin that took days to form. This was different. The line was still visible, a thin white mark against her brown skin, slightly raised, like a scar that was moons old instead of hours. The skin was smooth, dry, and whole.

Mei ran her finger along it. No pain. No tenderness. Just skin.

She pressed harder. Nothing.

She looked at the binding in her hand, dark with dried blood, and then at her arm, which looked like it had never been cut at all.

The warm was there — in her chest, where it always was, but also in her arm, concentrated along the line of the cut, a faint heat that was already fading. As though it had gone there while she slept. As though it had done this.

She turned her arm in the grey light, examining it from every angle. The scar looked old — moons old, not hours. The skin was smooth and whole except for that thin white line. She pressed her thumb against it. Hard. Nothing. Not a sting, not an ache, not the faintest memory of pain. Something had happened in the night, something the warm had done without asking.

She wrapped the binding back around her arm before anyone could see.

When the People woke and began to move, one of the women asked about her arm. Mei shrugged. Said it didn’t hurt. The woman nodded — children healed fast, everyone knew that, and it had been a shallow cut anyway, hadn’t it?

But another woman — Osa, the gatherer with thick arms and patient hands — saw the arm at midday. The binding had slipped, showing the thin white line where blood had poured the day before.

Osa looked at the line. Looked at Mei. Her face did something complicated — not fear, not yet, but the beginning of a question she did not want to ask. She reached out and touched the scar with one finger. Pressed. Her eyes widened.

She pulled her hand back. Said nothing. Walked away.

That evening, voices. Low, clustered, at the far side of the fire where the adults gathered after the children slept. Mei lay in the dark and listened. She could not hear the words — they were too careful for that — but she heard the tone. The rise and fall of people discussing something they did not understand. And once, twice, she heard the big man’s voice — not loud, not the command-voice. Something quieter. Directed inward, at the fire, not at the tree line.

Koh noticed.

They were walking, late morning, the sun already hot on their necks. Koh grabbed Mei’s wrist and pushed the binding up.

“Show.”

Mei showed.

Koh looked at the thin white line — the ghost of a wound that had been bleeding and raw the day before. She touched it. Pressed. Mei didn’t flinch.

Koh looked at Mei’s face.

“That was deep.”

“Yes.”

“Yesterday.”

“Yes.”

Koh’s fingers traced the line — gently, as though she expected it to hurt. It didn’t. Mei watched Koh’s face and saw the calculation: deep cut, one day, white scar. The math did not work. Koh knew it.

“Mei-thing,” she said.

“Mei-thing.”

Koh let go of her wrist. They walked in silence for a while, and then Koh said:

“Don’t show them.”

She didn’t say who. She didn’t have to. Them was everyone who was not Koh.

Mei pulled the binding back down over the scar.

They walked on.
