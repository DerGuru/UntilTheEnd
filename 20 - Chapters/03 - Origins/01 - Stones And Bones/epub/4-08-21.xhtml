Stones and Bones - 21

The bear came out of the ravine like a landslide with teeth. Mei had been tracking a deer — a young buck, limping from a bad leg, an easy kill if she could close the distance. The warm told her where it was: two hundred paces ahead, moving slowly through the scrub at the bottom of the valley. She had been following for most of the morning, patient, quiet, her feet warm and sure on the rock.

She did not feel the bear until it was close.

It had been in the ravine — deep, narrow, the rock dense and layered above it. When its warmth hit her chest, it was not the gradual approach of a moving animal — it was a wall. Huge. Dense. Hot. Like stepping into sunlight after being in a cave, except the sunlight had claws and weighed as much as five men.

The bear came out of the scrub to her left. Fast — faster than something that large had any right to be. Brown, massive, shoulders rolling. It saw her in the same moment she saw it.

Mei’s spear was in her hand. The good one — fire-hardened tip, ash shaft, bound with dried sinew. She had made it two moons ago. It was the best weapon she had ever made, and it was nothing compared to a bear.

The bear charged.

Mei threw. Not at the head — the skull was too thick. At the shoulder. The spear hit, sank. The bear screamed — a sound that shook the air, the ground, and the bones inside Mei’s body. The spear stuck. The bear did not stop.

It hit her with its shoulder. The impact was not like being pushed or thrown. It was like being erased — her body went from standing to flying to hitting the ground with a force that drove every thought from her head. She landed on her back on the rocky slope and felt something crack in her side. Not a clean break — a grinding, wrong sensation, deep.

The bear turned. Blood ran from its shoulder where the spear stuck out at an angle. Its eyes were small, dark, and utterly without mercy. It came at her again.

Mei rolled. The bear’s paw hit the ground where her head had been — she felt the impact through the rock, a shudder that ran up through her ribs and made the broken thing inside her shift. Pain exploded — white, total, a sound more than a feeling. She screamed.

The warm surged. Not because she told it to. It went to the broken place — not healing, not yet. Holding. Bracing. As though something inside her had built a scaffold around the break, keeping the pieces in place while the rest of her body kept moving.

She got to her feet. The bear was turning — big, slow turns, the kind that used the whole body. She had three heartbeats before it came again.

She looked. Behind her — a narrow crack in the rock face. Two boulders leaning against each other, leaving a gap barely wider than her shoulders. Too narrow for the bear. If she could reach it.

She ran. The cracked rib screamed. The warm screamed back — muffling, dampening, pushing the pain down to a place where it still existed but no longer controlled her legs. She ran as she’d learned on the mountain — not fast, but precise. Every foot placed. Every step calculated.

The bear followed. She could feel its warmth behind her — enormous, hot, closing.

She reached the crack. Turned sideways. Squeezed through. The rock scraped her back and her chest. The broken rib shifted. She bit through her lip to keep from screaming again.

The bear hit the gap. The boulders shuddered. Its head came through — jaws wide, breath hot and rotten — but its shoulders did not fit. It roared. The sound in the narrow space was like being inside a drum. Mei pressed herself against the back wall of the crack and watched the bear’s claws rake the stone, leaving white marks.

She was bleeding. From the lip. From scrapes on her arms and back. And from inside, where the broken rib had shifted.

The bear raged for a long time. Then, slowly, it pulled back. She felt its warmth recede — still close, still burning, but moving. Circling. Looking for another way in.

There was no other way in.

Mei waited. The warm in her chest went to work — slowly, carefully, the scaffold around the broken rib tightening, the edges of the break pulling together. Not fast. Not as it had healed the thorn-cut or the ibex-wound. This was bigger. This would take time.

The bear circled until the light changed. Then it found the spear — Mei felt its warmth settle, heard the sounds of an animal eating, and understood: it was eating the deer she had been tracking. The one with the bad leg. The easy kill.

When the bear was gone — truly gone, its warmth faded to nothing — Mei squeezed out of the crack.

She stood in the fading light and breathed. Each breath was a lesson in what broken ribs felt like. But the warm held. The scaffold held.

She looked at the blood on the rocks. At the claw marks on the boulders. At her own hands, shaking and scraped.

Then she walked back to the cave. Slowly. Carefully. Each step a negotiation between what her body wanted to do (collapse) and what the warm insisted it could still manage (one more step, one more, one more).

She made it.

She lay on the cave floor, pressed herself against the warm stone, and let the warm work. The rib shifted, aligned. The pain dulled from sharp to deep to present. The scrapes closed. The blood dried.

By the second morning, she could sit up. By the third, she could stand.

By the fifth, she was hunting again.

But she dreamed of the bear for many nights after — the speed, the weight, the small dark eyes that held nothing personal. It had not hated her. It had not wanted her dead. She had been in its space, and it had responded the way mountains respond to things that stand where rock wants to fall.

She began making a better spear.
