Stones and Bones - 13

Morning came — light moving through the trees, birds waking in the branches, the smell of cold ash and dew. The same. Exactly the same. As though nothing had changed, as though the world had not listened to what had happened in the dark and decided it did not matter.

Mei stood at the edge of the camp.

She had not slept. Her body was thin with exhaustion — the kind that passes tiredness into something else, where sounds arrive a breath too late, where edges are too sharp. The warm in her chest had come back during the night — slowly, filling the emptiness slowly, like water in a hole — but it brought no comfort. It sat there, steady and useless, the thing that had saved Koh and ruined everything else.

Nobody came to her. The camp moved around her the way water moves around a stone in the river — flowing past, not touching, leaving a dry space where she stood. Women packed. Children were gathered. The hunters checked their spears. The ordinary rhythm of morning, and Mei was not in it.

A boy — the one who always swung sticks at nothing — rolled his sleeping furs with the same quick motions he used every morning. Near the fire pit, two women divided the last of yesterday’s meat, hands moving in the practiced rhythm of people who had done this a thousand times. Smoke rose from the banked coals, thin against the pale sky. The smell was the same as always — ash, fat, the faint sweetness of grass that had been slept on. It was the most ordinary morning Mei had known.

She stood and watched it happen. A child ran past the place where she stood, close enough to touch, curving around her without looking up. One of the hunters stretched, rolled his neck, picked up a spear. His eyes moved across her without stopping — the same unseeing sweep he gave the rocks, the trees, the ground.

Mei’s feet were cold. She noticed this as she noticed everything now — clearly, separately, as though her senses had been sharpened by the night’s worth of silence, by the understanding that was settling into her bones like cold water.

Then her mother came.

She came from the direction of the sleeping places, walking in a straight line, not looking left or right. Her face was set — the flat, closed expression she wore when she was carrying something heavy. Not a burden in her arms. A burden behind her eyes.

She stopped in front of Mei.

They looked at each other. Mei waited for words. Her mother’s mouth opened once. Closed. Opened again. Nothing came out.

She was afraid, Mei realized. Not of the big man. Not of the People. Of Mei.

Her own mother was afraid of her.

The understanding hit like cold water — not sharp, not sudden, but total, soaking through everything at once. Mei’s eyes stung. She bit the inside of her cheek until she tasted iron.

Her mother reached into the fold of her carrying skin and pulled out a bundle — small, tight, wrapped in a piece of hide. She pressed it into Mei’s hands. Their fingers touched. Her mother’s hands were shaking.

Dried meat. Roots. A handful of the good nuts, the ones with the hard shells that lasted. Enough for days, if Mei was careful.

No words. Her mother could not find words for this — for the daughter she had carried, fed, slept beside, kept warm. The daughter she was letting go because the man who led them had said go, and she was not strong enough to say no.

Her hand stayed on Mei’s for one breath. Two. Three.

Then she pulled away.

She turned and walked back the way she had come, into the camp, into the group, into the space where she belonged because she had not pushed anyone with something that had no name.

Mei held the bundle against her chest. The hide was still warm from her mother’s hands — fading, but there. She pressed it against her ribs, where the warm sat. Two kinds of warmth. One was leaving. The other would stay whether she wanted it to or not.

She stood in the dry space the camp had made around her. The smoke drifted. Someone laughed — a quick, sharp sound, a child’s sound, already gone. The camp moved on without her. Living things do not wait for the parts they shed.

She looked for Koh.

Koh was not there. Mei’s eyes moved across the camp — the sleeping places, the fire circle, the rocks where they sat, the path to the water. Koh was not at any of them. She scanned the faces, counted them automatically, as always.

One missing. Not like when Ren went missing — that had been the absence of someone who would not come back. This was the absence of someone who was choosing not to be here.

Koh was hiding. Mei could feel her — faintly, at the far edge of the camp, behind the big rock where the children played. A warmth she knew as well as her own. Koh was there. Koh was not coming out. Mei understood this too. Koh was not hiding from Mei. Koh was hiding from the moment — the moment where Mei walked away and Koh stayed. Both of them knew it was happening. Neither of them could stop it.

Mei looked at the rock. She could go there. She could find Koh, hold her, say something — goodbye or come back or I’m sorry or wait for me. But the words would change nothing. The holding would only make the letting go harder. Mei was eleven summers old and already learning that some things are worse when you look at them directly.

She looked away. Her hands were fists at her sides. She opened them, one finger at a time, and made them hold the bundle instead.

The old man stood at the edge of the camp, apart from the others. He was leaning on a stick — not because he needed it, but because it was the habit of a body that had been walking longer than most people had been alive. His face was turned toward Mei.

He did not speak. He had spoken last night. It had not been enough. He did not seem like a man who repeated himself.

But he looked at her. A long, steady look — not soft, not kind, not pitying. Something harder than all of those. The look of a man who sees clearly and does not look away, even when what he sees is wrong.

He dipped his head. Once. Small. It was the smallest gesture Mei had ever seen carry weight — not approval, not farewell. Something stripped of everything but the seeing.

He turned and walked back toward the fire. His stick tapped the hard ground with each step — a dry, even sound, like counting. Mei watched his back until it became just another shape among the shapes of people who were no longer hers.

Mei shifted the bundle in her arms. She looked at the camp — the fires, the skins, the shapes of people she had known since before she could walk. The sounds of morning. The smell of home.

She turned.

One step. The ground was hard and cold under her bare foot. Morning dew on the grass, cool between her toes. The air smelled of earth and distance.

Another step. The camp sounds were already dimmer — not because they were far, but because her ears were adjusting, making space, preparing for the silence that was coming.

Another. The ground changed under her feet — the packed earth of the camp, worn smooth by every foot she had known, giving way to softer ground where dead leaves gathered and moss grew in patches. She could feel each step now — the cold dampness seeping between her toes, the roughness of roots, the give of soil that no one had walked on in days.

Twenty steps from the camp, a stone rose from the earth, half-buried, shaped like a fist. She had climbed it when she was small. She had sat on it with Koh, eating stolen berries, legs swinging over the edge.

She stepped past it.

The forest closed around her — tall trees, close-set, their trunks still dark with last night’s rain. Birds called in the canopy. Somewhere to her left, water moved over stone.

The warm sat in her chest — heavy, full, the only warmth that was hers. The only thing she carried that could not be taken away, that did not depend on the permission of others, that did not require her to be small enough or quiet enough or normal enough to keep.

She did not look back. She wanted to — the pull was physical, a tightening at the base of her skull, a weight that tried to turn her head. Her body knew what was behind her. Her body wanted one more look at the fires, at the rock where Koh was hiding, at the place where her mother had been.

She kept her eyes forward.

The trees took her. The shadows between the trunks closed around her like water closing over a stone, and the camp was behind her, and the People were behind her, and Koh was behind her, and her mother was behind her.

Ahead there was nothing she knew. Behind her, everything.

The bundle against her ribs. The warm behind the bundle, deeper, steady. These were the things she had.

She walked into it.
