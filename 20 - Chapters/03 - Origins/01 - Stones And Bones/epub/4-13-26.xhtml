Stones and Bones - 26

The mountain changed, and Mei changed with it. She noticed the flowers first — different ones. Where blue had grown, now yellow grew. The bushes at the tree line bore fruit she had not seen before, and birds she did not recognize called from the high branches. The warm told her: same place, different time. Seasons upon seasons upon seasons, layered on top of each other like skins on a sleeping pile.

She stood at the cave entrance in the early light and looked at her hands.

They were not a child’s hands. They had not been for a long time, but the change had been gradual — so slow she had not noticed it happening, the way you do not notice a river cutting through stone until the canyon is already there. Her fingers were long and calloused, the nails cracked, the knuckles scarred from stone-work, hunting, and the thousand small impacts of a body that used itself hard. Her palms were rough as bark.

She looked at the rest of herself. Legs: long, hard with muscle, scarred at the shins and thighs from thorns, claws, and the mountain’s indifferent teeth. Arms: the same — ropy, lean, the kind of arms that could throw a spear through a running animal at thirty paces. Her torso was flat and spare, nothing wasted, nothing extra. She had not seen her own face in moons — there was a pool, deeper in the cave, where the water was still enough to show a reflection, but she did not look often.

The last time she had looked, the face looking back was a stranger’s face. Angular. Hard-edged. Eyes that were older than the rest of it.

The warm sat in her chest the way her heart sat in her chest — present, permanent, as much a part of her as bone. She did not think about it anymore. She did not have to. It moved when she moved. It breathed when she breathed. When she pushed it, it went. When she pulled it, it came. When she was still, it was still. When danger came, it answered — fast, total, the hardening that had stopped the cat, the dampening that had held broken bones together, the sight that saw in darkness and the hearing that counted heartbeats through stone.

She was something that had not existed before her. She knew this the way she knew the mountain was old — not as a fact she had learned, but as a fact she inhabited. There was no one like her. There had never been anyone like her. The warm was hers alone. The thing beneath the warm — the not now, the refusal, the deep and wordless imperative that had crystallized somewhere in the winters, the fights, and the silence — was hers alone too.

She did not call it a name. She did not think of it as a thing she did. It was how she was. The way a river does not decide to flow downhill. The way a fire does not decide to burn.

She sat in the cave and gathered the warm for the last time.

Not the last time ever — the warm would be with her as long as she was with herself. But the last time like this: sitting on the stone floor with her palms flat, drawing the cave’s warmth into the deep place behind her ribs, filling herself the way you fill a vessel before a long journey.

The cave gave. It always gave. Dense, patient, and inexhaustible, the slow heartbeat of the mountain pouring into her without demand or expectation. She sat, breathed, and pulled. The warm came. It filled her. She was full.

She opened her eyes.

The cave was dark, warm, and familiar. Every crack in the wall. Every stain on the stone. The sleeping place, with its pile of skins. The fire pit, cold now. The rack, empty. The spears against the wall — six of them, different lengths, different heads, each one a piece of her history.

She took two. The heavy one and the light one. She took a water skin, a pouch of dried meat, and the good blade — the one she had made from the grey-green flint, the one with the edge that never seemed to dull.

She stood at the entrance.

The morning air was cool, smelling of pine, dew, and the particular, sharp clarity of altitude. Below her, the mountain fell away in slopes of rock, scrub, and the first dark lines of trees. Below that, invisible but present, the rivers ran. Beside the rivers, she knew, were people.

She was not going down to find them. She was not going down because the loneliness pressed. She was not going down because she needed anything from anyone.

She was going down because she was ready.

For what, she did not know. The warm hummed in her chest — even, full, waiting. The thing beneath the warm was quiet. Not asleep — quiet. The way a blade is quiet in a sheath.

Mei stepped out of the cave and into the light. The mountain let her go. She went.
