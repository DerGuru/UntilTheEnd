The cat came from the east, and Mei felt it before the wind turned. She was walking at the edge of the column — the People moving through tall grass, heading for the river crossing they used when the water was low. The sun was high. The grass smelled of dust and seed heads, dry and sweet, brushing against her thighs as she walked.

Koh was somewhere behind, walking with the older children. Mei could hear her laugh — bright, scattered, the sound of someone who had never been wary of a path she hadn't walked. The column moved in its usual rhythm — steady, unhurried, the particular pace of people who had walked together long enough that their steps aligned without effort.

The hum hit her like a fist.

Not the gentle warmth of the deer. Not the slow pulse of water underground. This was dense, hot, wrong — a pressure in her chest that made her breath catch, that tasted of metal and old blood, that screamed in a voice she had no words for: danger. Close. Coming.

Mei stopped.

The woman walking ahead of her did not stop. The children beside her did not stop. Nobody stopped, because nobody felt what Mei felt — the wind was blowing toward the east, the smell had not reached them yet, their ears heard only grass, footsteps, and the distant murmur of the river.

But Mei's chest was on fire.

She grabbed the arm of the woman beside her — Osa, one of the gatherers, thick-armed and patient. Osa looked down. Mei pointed east, toward the tree line where the tall grass met the shadows.

"Bad. There. Bad."

Osa frowned. Looked where Mei pointed. Saw nothing.

"Big teeth," Mei said. She did not know why she said that — the hum did not come in words, did not carry pictures. But something in the density of it, in the heat and the wrongness, matched the feeling she had when the hunters came back with stories of the long-toothed cat that lived in the eastern hills. A predator. Something that hunted the things that hunted them.

Osa's frown deepened. She looked east again, harder this time.

Mei pulled at her arm. "Move. Now. Move."

Her voice was louder than she meant it to be. The people near her turned. The column slowed. Faces looked at her — a child, pulling at a woman's arm, pointing at empty grassland. The big man, three body-lengths ahead, turned his head. The old man stopped walking.

Thirty heartbeats of nothing. Grass moving in the wind. Sun. Dust.

Then the grass at the tree line parted, and the cat came through.

It was the color of dry earth, long and low. It moved with the unhurried certainty of something that had never been prey. Its head swung toward them. Its eyes were the color of old honey.

The column erupted. Women scooped children. Hunters drew together, spears out, forming a line between the group and the animal. The big man shouted — a single, hard sound — and the People broke into the practiced chaos of retreat: fast, tight, children in the center, spears at the back.

The cat watched. It did not charge. After a long, still moment, it turned and flowed back into the grass like water draining into sand.

The People did not stop moving until the river was between them and the eastern bank. By then the sun had shifted. The children were crying. Everybody's hands were shaking with the particular looseness that comes after fear has passed.

Nobody was hurt. Nobody had been close enough. The cat had been far — maybe too far to attack, maybe just testing, maybe simply passing through.

The People stood in the shallows, breathing hard. Women held children against their chests. The hunters' spears pointed at empty grassland. Nobody spoke. The river mud was cold between their toes, and the air smelled of crushed grass and the faint musk the cat had left behind.

But Mei had known.

The looks started that evening. Not hostile — not quite. But different. A quality of attention that had not been there before. Eyes that stayed on Mei too long. Conversations that thinned when she walked past.

In the days that followed, the distance grew. A woman who had always walked near Mei during the march chose a different position — three body-lengths further, four. Mei felt the gap before she saw it, a cooling in the space where the woman's warmth had been.

A boy who had played with Koh and Mei at the river — who had laughed when Koh splashed — was called back by his mother when he wandered near. The calling was casual. The grip on his arm was not.

At the evening fire, Mei reached for a piece of meat from the common pile. A hand pulled the pile six inches further from her. The motion was small. The meaning was not.

One of the hunters asked her. He crouched in front of her, his face level with hers.

"How? You saw?"

"Felt."

"Felt what?"

"Here." She pressed her hand against her chest. "Warm. Wrong warm. Big."

The hunter stared at her hand on her chest. Then at her face. Then he stood up and walked away without saying anything else. His back was stiff. He went straight to the hunters near the fire and spoke to them, low, leaning close. They all looked at Mei. She watched them look. She watched them look away.

Mei sat by the fire and watched the People not-look at her. They were good at it — the way you don't-look at a snake in the path, aware of exactly where it is without turning your head.

Koh sat beside her. Close. Shoulder to shoulder.

"Good that you felt it," Koh said.

Mei said nothing.

"They should be happy. Cat would have come. You saw first."

"I didn't see."

"You knew."

"They don't like that I knew."

The words tasted strange. She had not said it before — the thing she felt, named aloud.

Koh was quiet for a moment. Then she bumped Mei's shoulder with her own.

"I like that you knew."

Mei leaned into the bump. The fire crackled. Somewhere across the camp, the big man was talking to the hunters in a low voice. His eyes moved to Mei once and moved away.

The warm in her chest pulsed — slow, steady, the same as always. As though nothing had changed.

Everything had changed.

Mei put her hands in her lap and made herself small. She was beginning to understand: the same thing could be useful and dangerous. It depended on who was watching.
