The cat came from the east, and Mei felt it before the wind turned.

She was walking at the edge of the column—the People moving through tall grass, heading for the river crossing they used when the water was low. The sun was high. The grass smelled of dust and seed heads, dry and sweet, brushing against her thighs as she walked.

The hum hit her like a fist.

Not the gentle warmth of the deer. Not the slow pulse of water underground. This was dense and hot and wrong—a pressure in her chest that made her breath catch, that tasted of metal and old blood, that screamed in a voice she had no words for: danger. Close. Coming.

Mei stopped.

The woman walking ahead of her did not stop. The children beside her did not stop. Nobody stopped, because nobody felt what Mei felt, because the wind was blowing toward the east and the smell had not reached them yet and their ears heard only grass and footsteps and the distant murmur of the river.

But Mei's chest was on fire.

She grabbed the arm of the woman beside her—Osa, one of the gatherers, thick-armed and patient. Osa looked down. Mei pointed east, toward the tree line where the tall grass met the shadows.

"Bad. There. Bad."

Osa frowned. Looked where Mei pointed. Saw nothing.

"Big teeth," Mei said. She did not know why she said that—the hum did not come in words, did not carry pictures. But something in the density of it, in the heat and the wrongness, matched the feeling she had when the hunters came back with stories of the long-toothed cat that lived in the eastern hills. A predator. Something that hunted the things that hunted them.

Osa's frown deepened. She looked east again, harder this time.

Mei pulled at her arm. "Move. Now. Move."

Her voice was louder than she meant it to be. The people near her turned. The column slowed. Faces looked at her—a child, pulling at a woman's arm, pointing at empty grassland. The big man, three body-lengths ahead, turned his head. The old man stopped walking.

Thirty heartbeats of nothing. Grass moving in the wind. Sun. Dust.

Then the grass at the tree line parted, and the cat came through.

It was the color of dry earth, long and low, and it moved with the unhurried certainty of something that had never been prey. Its head swung toward them. Its eyes were the color of old honey.

The column erupted. Women scooped children. Hunters drew together, spears out, forming a line between the group and the animal. The big man shouted—a single, hard sound—and the People broke into the practiced chaos of retreat: fast, tight, children in the center, spears at the back.

The cat watched. It did not charge. After a long, still moment, it turned and flowed back into the grass like water draining into sand.

The People did not stop moving until the river was between them and the eastern bank. By then the sun had shifted, and the children were crying, and everybody's hands were shaking with the particular looseness that comes after fear has passed.

Nobody was hurt. Nobody had been close enough. The cat had been far—maybe too far to attack, maybe just testing, maybe simply passing through.

But Mei had known.

The looks started that evening. Not hostile. Not yet. Just—different. Eyes that stayed on her a beat too long. Conversations that stopped when she walked past. A woman who pulled her child away when Mei sat down beside them—a small motion, almost casual, but Mei saw it.

One of the hunters asked her. He crouched in front of her, his face level with hers.

"How? You saw?"

"Felt."

"Felt what?"

"Here." She pressed her hand against her chest. "Warm. Wrong warm. Big."

The hunter stared at her hand on her chest. Then at her face. Then he stood up and walked away without saying anything else.

Mei sat by the fire and watched the People not-look at her. They were good at it—the way you don't-look at a snake in the path, aware of exactly where it is without turning your head.

Koh sat beside her. Close. Shoulder to shoulder.

"Good that you felt it," Koh said.

Mei said nothing.

"They should be happy. Cat would have come. You saw first."

"I didn't see."

"You knew."

"They don't like that I knew."

Koh was quiet for a moment. Then she bumped Mei's shoulder with her own.

"I like that you knew."

Mei leaned into the bump. The fire crackled. Somewhere across the camp, the big man was talking to the hunters in a low voice. His eyes moved to Mei once and moved away.

The warm in her chest pulsed—slow, steady, the same as always. As though nothing had changed.

Everything had changed.
