The days accumulated, the way silt accumulates in a river bend. Wake. Breathe. Gather the warm. Go out. Hunt. Come back. Eat. Gather again. Sleep. Wake. Breathe. The same. The same. The same.

The cave became a den. Not the word she would have used — she had no words for most things now, having gone days, sometimes many days, without speaking at all. But the space organized itself around her like a den: sleeping place here, fire place there, meat drying on a rack of sticks near the entrance where the air moved. Skins piled in the corner. A water basin, kept full. Tools — the crude kind, sticks and stones she had shaped with hands that were getting stronger every moon.

When a spear broke on the second throw, she made a better one. When the drying rack collapsed, she rebuilt it with thicker branches and binding made of sinew. When the wind blew smoke into the cave and she woke coughing, she found the crack in the rock face that served as a chimney and cleared the debris from it. Problems presented themselves. She solved them. This was not ingenuity. It was the stubbornness of a thing that refused to die poorly.

The warm grew.

Each morning she sat on the cave floor and pulled — steady, patient, as she had learned. The cave's warmth poured into her, filling the place behind her ribs. Each day the place was a little larger, a little deeper, able to hold a little more. When she hunted now, the warm stayed with her — not the thin, unreliable thread of the early days, but a solid presence, banked and ready. She could push it to her eyes and see in near-darkness. Push it to her ears and hear the heartbeat of a rabbit three trees away. Push it to her feet and cross scree without sound.

The animals of the mountain became familiar. The marmots, which were easy. The ibex, which were not. The birds — good for eggs, not worth the effort of hunting unless they were ground-nesting and fat. The predators — the cats, the wolves, the bears that came through in autumn — she tracked them the way they tracked her. By warmth, by trail, by fear and respect.

The winter alone was the worst.

The cold came like a fist. The air hardened. The scrub went bare. Animals retreated — down the mountain, into dens, into places she could not follow. The snow came late but heavy, and the entrance to the cave narrowed to a gap she had to clear every morning, pushing through wet white that soaked her clothes and numbed her hands.

She ate the dried meat. She ate the stored roots. She ate things she would not have eaten in summer — bark, lichen, the hard inner parts of pinecones that tasted of resin and nothing else. She ate grubs she found under rotting logs, curling white things that burst when she bit down. The warm kept her alive. She pushed it through her body in a slow, constant circulation, a private fire that never went out. But the warm could not fill her stomach, and by the deepest part of the cold she was thin — thinner than she had been since the exile. Her ribs showed. Her face was all angles. 

On the coldest nights, she curled in the deepest part of the cave, wrapped in every skin she had, the warm burning low and constant in her chest. She could feel it working — pushing heat through her blood, keeping her core warm while her fingers and toes went numb. Without the warm she would have died. She knew this without drama, without gratitude. The warm kept her alive. The warm and her stubbornness.

It wasn't easy, but she survived the way stone survives water — by being too stubborn to wear away completely.

When the cold eased and the first green showed on the lower slopes, Mei stood at the cave entrance and looked out at a world that was starting over. The air smelled of mud, melt, and the first hesitant insects.

She had lost weight she could not afford to lose. Her hip bones jutted. Her wrists were thin. But the warm was stronger than before — as though the winter, which had nearly killed the body, had fed the thing inside it.

She was still here.

She went inside, sat on the cave floor and gathered the warm. The warm came — fast, eager, stronger than before the cold. As though it too had been waiting for the season to turn.

At night, the silence pressed.

During the day she could fill it — with work, with hunting, with the small sounds of living. Scraping a hide. Sharpening a stone. The crack of a stick breaking for fire. Movement created noise. Noise created the illusion of company. The illusion was sometimes enough.

At night, the illusion failed.

She lay on the skins in the cave with the warm pulsing in her chest and listened to nothing. Not wind — the cave blocked that. Not animals — they were sleeping or hunting far away. Not fire — the coals were banked, silent. Just the particular, deafening emptiness of being the only person in the world.

She hummed. Low, tuneless, a vibration more felt than heard. She didn't know where the melody came from — or if it was a melody at all. It was a sound. Her sound. Proof that something in this cave could make noise on purpose.

She knocked stones together. Tap, tap, tap. Like the heartbeat she could not hear next to her.

Sometimes she pressed her palm flat against the cave wall and pushed the warm into it. The stone warmed under her hand. She could pretend — for one breath, two — that the warmth was coming from outside, from another body, from someone sleeping beside her. The pretending never lasted. But she did it every night.

She spoke. "Koh." Just the name. Not to Koh — Koh was far away, impossibly far, behind moons, mountains, and everything that had happened. She said it because saying it was different from thinking it. Thinking Koh's name was like holding a stone. Saying it aloud was like setting the stone down — briefly, before picking it up again. Words were meant to be said aloud. If she did not say them they would rot inside her like fruit left too long.

"Mother."

"The old man."

Names. Just names. She said them into the dark and the dark absorbed them without answer.

She pressed her palms against the warm stone and felt the slow, vast pulse of the mountain. It was something. It was not enough. That was how it was.

Morning came. She rose. She gathered. She went out.

The rhythm continued.
