The river was slow here and warm enough to stand in. The water came to Mei's waist, brown-green, carrying leaves and small sticks downstream in lazy turns. The bottom was mud and smooth stones, and when she dug her toes in, the mud pushed up between them, cool and soft.

Koh was already in the deep part, where the current pulled. She stood with her arms out, letting the water press against her chest, leaning into it like she was leaning into wind. Her hair floated behind her in a dark fan.

"Cold?"

"Good cold," Koh said. She ducked under and came up blowing water. She grinned.

Three of the younger children stood at the bank, watching. The smallest one — a boy with a round belly and scabbed knees — put one foot in and pulled it back. The other two waited to see what he would do.

Mei waded toward the far bank, where the current slowed and the water pooled behind a line of rocks. Small fish held in the still water there — she could see them, dark shapes, finning against the current, holding position as though pinned to the spot. She crouched until the water reached her chin and held still. The fish drifted closer. One brushed her knee — a flicker, gone.

"What are you doing?" Koh called.

"Watching."

"Watching what?"

"Fish."

Koh made a sound that meant she did not see the point of watching fish when you could be doing something better. She launched herself sideways into the current and let it carry her two body-lengths downstream before she planted her feet and stood up, laughing.

The round-bellied boy was in the water now, up to his ankles, arms stiff at his sides. One of the older girls waded past him and splashed his face. He screamed — short, sharp, more surprise than fear — and sat down in the shallows. The girl kept walking.

Mei watched.

She watched the way the children moved through water — some careful, some reckless, all of them learning the same thing at different speeds. She watched the older girl teach the round-bellied boy without teaching. just walking deeper, looking back and waiting. The boy followed because someone was ahead of him, and that was enough.

The sun was high and the air smelled of warm stone, river weed, and the sharp green of crushed grass where the children had trampled the bank. Somewhere upstream, women were filling water skins. Their voices carried — soft, shapeless, blending with the sound of the river until Mei could not tell where one ended and the other began.

Koh came back, dripping.

"Build something."

"What?"

"A wall. Here." Koh pointed at the rocks where the fish held. "Block the water. Catch the fish."

Mei looked at the rocks. Looked at the gaps between them.

"Too wide."

"Mud," Koh said. She was already bending, scooping dark handfuls from the bottom. She packed mud between two rocks, pressing it flat with her palms. The water cut through it in three heartbeats.

Mei watched the mud dissolve. She picked up a flat stone from the bottom — grey, heavy, smooth on both sides — and set it against the gap. The water pushed. The stone held. She found another. Wedged it beside the first. Koh packed mud around the edges, and this time the mud stayed because the stones took the force.

They worked without talking. Koh brought stones. Mei placed them. She had an eye for which shapes fit which gaps — the flat ones here, the thick ones there, the wedge-shaped piece that locked the others in place. It was like the puzzles the world kept offering her. How bark peeled in strips that could be braided, how bones stacked to hold weight, how flint broke along invisible lines that she could feel before she struck.

The wall grew. Water pooled behind it, deeper, stiller. The fish circled in the shrinking space, bumping the stones, turning, bumping again.

"Now what?" Mei said.

Koh reached in with both hands, fast. Water exploded. She came up empty, laughing, arms wet to the shoulders.

"Slippery."

She tried again. Missed again. The fish scattered to the edges of the pool, pressing themselves against the stone wall as though they could push through it.

Mei put her hand in slowly. Palm open, fingers spread, just below the surface. She held still. The water settled. A fish drifted toward her hand — cautious, finning, its mouth opening and closing in the quiet rhythm of something that did not know it was being watched.

She closed her hand.

The fish was in her fist — thrashing, slick, alive. She could feel its spine flex against her fingers, the pulse of its body, the frantic kick of its tail. She lifted it out of the water and held it up.

Koh stared.

"How."

Mei opened her hand. The fish dropped back into the pool and was gone in a flash of silver.

"Waited," Mei said.

Koh looked at the pool. At her own hands. At Mei.

"Show me."

They spent the rest of the afternoon in the water. Koh caught one fish — a small one, barely longer than her thumb, which she held up like a trophy before it wriggled free. Mei caught three more, each time the same way. Hand in, still, wait, close. Each time the same small shock of something alive against her palm.

The round-bellied boy waded over to watch. He stood chest-deep in the pool, mouth open, staring at Mei's hand in the water. When she caught one and held it up, he reached for it. Mei let him touch it — the wet scales, the gasping mouth, the tail that flicked against his fingers. His eyes went wide. He looked at Mei with an expression she would see many times in her life, on many different faces, though she did not know that yet. The look of someone seeing a thing they cannot do and wanting it.

She let the fish go.

The sun moved. The shadows from the trees reached the water. The women called the children back, and the children went — wet, mud-streaked, carrying nothing. Koh shook water from her hair like a dog and walked up the bank without looking back.

Mei stayed a moment longer. She put her hand on one of the stones she had placed. It was warm from the sun on its top half, cool from the river on its bottom. She could feel both temperatures through her palm, the line where one became the other sharp as a cut.

She pulled the stone free. Water rushed through the gap. The wall held for three heartbeats, then collapsed — stones rolling, mud dissolving, the pool emptying back into the river as though it had never been.

The fish scattered downstream.

Mei climbed the bank. Her feet left wet prints on the warm stone, dark shapes that shrank and faded as she walked. By the time she reached the camp, they were gone.

Koh had saved her a piece of the afternoon's smoked meat. She handed it over without a word. Mei took it and sat beside her, chewing, their shoulders touching. The meat was tough and tasted of ash and fat.

The fire crackled. The People moved around them in the patterns of evening — someone mending a carrying frame, someone scraping a hide, someone nursing a child. The sounds layered over each other. Scraping, murmuring, the pop of green wood, a baby's hiccup.

Koh leaned against her.

"Good day," Koh said.

Mei chewed. Swallowed. The taste of river was still on her lips — stone, green, cold.

"Good day," she said.
