The fire was low. The kind of fire that happened when nobody fed it because nobody needed the light, only the warmth, and the warmth was almost optional.

You were writing. The pen moved in the lamplight — no, in the firelight, there was no lamp — and the scratch of nib on paper was the only sound besides the wind and the occasional shift of Xiǎohú's body in whatever nest of leaves she'd constructed.

"The names," I said.

You didn't look up. The pen paused. Resumed.

"From the other night," I said. "The ones I couldn't remember."

"Yes."

"There will be more."

The pen stopped. You set it down. Looked at me across the fire with the expression of a man who understood that a conversation was beginning and the conversation was not casual.

"After you leave," I said. "After — whatever happens. Whether I succeed or don't. There will be more names. More faces. More people I watched exist and then watched stop existing. More dead. And if I don't succeed — if the wall holds — then it continues. Indefinitely. The names accumulate. The faces stack. The archive grows and grows and the archivist can't die and can't stop recording."

You folded your hands over the notebook. The gesture of a man who was listening with his whole body.

"That's not a question," you said.

"No."

Silence. The fire shifted. A log collapsed inward, the structural failure of wood that had been consumed from the inside, and the brief flare of sparks illuminated your face — the lines around your eyes, the grey at your temples, the evidence of time written on a surface that time was allowed to write on.

"You'll die," I said.

Flat. I was adopting your register for the sentences that were too heavy for any other delivery.

"Yes," you said. "Eventually."

"And I'll remember your name. Your face. The way you hold the pen. The sound of your voice when you're thinking before you speak. I'll remember the notebook and the fire and this mountain and I'll remember it for — " The word wouldn't form. The duration was too large for language.

"A long time," you said.

"A long time. It will fade but you'll be there."

You picked up the pen. Turned it in your fingers. Not writing — the fidget of a man processing something.

"Is that a complaint?" you asked.

The question was so dry, so stripped of sympathy, that I almost laughed. Almost.

"No."

"Then what is it?"

I looked at the fire. The embers were arranging themselves in patterns that might have been mine or might have been physics. I couldn't tell anymore. Didn't matter.

"At the beginning it was — good." The word was insufficient. "The first few thousand years. Nothing could touch me. Nothing could hurt me. The world was enormous and I could walk through all of it and take as long as I wanted because time was mine. An infinite resource. I was untouchable."

You didn't interrupt.

"Then the faces started accumulating. People I'd known. People I'd — not loved, not exactly, but inhabited the same years with. They died. All of them. And then their children died. And their children's children. And eventually the names lost their faces. Became impressions. The smell of a room. A laugh I could hear but couldn't attach to a mouth."

"So every connection— " you said.

"Cut." The word was small and absolute. "Every single one. By time or by violence. Either they died or I had to leave before they noticed I didn't age. Either way — gone. All of them. Always."

The fire didn't move. The air didn't move. The stillness of a mountain listening.

"And the repetition. That was — " I exhaled. "Humans make the same mistakes. The same decisions. The same wars for the same reasons. After enough time you know what happens next. You know how the empire ends before the emperor is crowned. You know the rebellion will fail because the last twelve rebellions failed in exactly the same way. The reasons stop mattering. The outcomes stop surprising. Everything becomes — "

"Predictable," you said.

"Inevitable. Empires built over generations. Beautiful, complex, layered. And I'd watch them and know — in a thousand years this will be rubble. That river will change course. That city will be farmland. The knowledge made everything temporary. Made everything pointless except the watching."

I watched the embers. Red fading to grey at the edges, the slow death of heat.

"And then you go numb." I said it without looking at you. "If everything is temporary — if every face is already a memory before you've finished looking at it — then nothing has weight. Nothing matters. Lives become — interchangeable. Expendable. What difference does it make if someone dies today or in sixty years? From where I stood it was the same blink of an eye."

The silence was different now. Denser.

"That was my darkest time," I said. "The time when I stopped — caring. When I let them use me. Point me at a thing and I'd destroy it. People. Armies. Cities. I was a weapon. A very effective one. Because nothing mattered and nothing hurt and people were just — bodies. Temporary arrangements of temporary matter doing temporary things."

You didn't write. Your hands were still. The stillness of a man hearing something he'd suspected but hadn't been told.

"No morality. No hesitation. No distinction between an army or a village. They were all going to die anyway. I just — accelerated the schedule."

"For how long?" you asked. Your voice was level. The factual register. No judgment in it and no absolution.

"I don't know. I didn't care and didn't count. Long enough to forget what it felt like to refuse."

"What changed?"

"I'm not sure." The honest answer. The insufficient one. "Something — cracked. A face. A person who was about to die and I saw — I don't remember. But I stopped. I killed the man who was pointing me and walked away. And I kept walking."

"Wandering."

"The wandering. Millennia of it. Looking for the thing that would make it end. Looking for mortality like it was a place I could walk to if I just walked far enough."

"Blessing to curse," I said. "That's the trajectory. That's what immortality is. A thing that starts as freedom then becomes a weapon and later becomes a prison. By the time you understand its cost, it is already — " I gestured at nothing. At everything. At the accumulated weight of millennia spent watching the same story repeat with different names in the margins.

You nodded. The single nod that meant you had received information, filed it and would not offer comfort because comfort was not what was being requested.

"Lin Feiyun," you said.

I looked at you.

"The woman who taught you to grind paste. From the other night. You said the name and your voice — " You made a small gesture. "The voice was different for that one."

"She was kind."

"And dead."

"And dead. For thousands of years. And I still know her name and what her hands looked like when she was working. I still remember the smell of the room and the way she hummed. Thousands of years and the memory hasn't faded. Hasn't blurred. Hasn't done what memories are supposed to do."

"Because your body doesn't degrade."

"It does, actually. Most of it. Most names are gone. Most faces. Thousands of years of people I knew and all that's left is — residue. A feeling without a source. The weight of someone without the someone."

"But not all."

"Not all. Some stay. Complete. Vivid. Lin Feiyun is one of them."

"Why her?"

"Because she was the first person who was kind to me for no reason." I said it and knew it was true. "That's what survives. The firsts. The things that were unexpected. The moments that didn't fit the pattern. Everything else — the routine, the repetition, the millions of sunrises — that degrades. But the first time someone was kind. The first time I killed someone and felt nothing. The first — " I stopped. "Those stay. Sharp. Permanent. Whether I want them to or not."

The fire was embers now. The darkness had expanded to fill the space the light had occupied. Your face was shadow and suggestion — the geometry of a person rather than the detail.

"So, will I stay or fade," you said.

"You helped me understand that I needed to change. That I needed to stop being the thing I'd been for millennia. You were the first person who made me feel like change was possible. Like I could be something other than a weapon or a prisoner. So, yes. I think you'll stay."

You were quiet for a long time. The quiet that preceded the thing you were actually going to say.

"I'll be one of the names," you said. "Xu Ran. Filed. Stored. Retrievable. Permanent because you're permanent. Is that — " You paused. The editorial pause. "Is that supposed to frighten me?"

"No."

"Good. Because it doesn't."

You picked up the pen. The fire died. The last ember went grey.

I couldn't see your face anymore. Just the dark shape of you against the darker shape of the mountain. But I could hear the sound of you settling — the adjustment of a body finding its sleeping position, the small reorganization of limbs and weight that preceded rest.

"Good night," you said.

The mountain was quiet. The stars were visible. Somewhere below the treeline, a bird sang once and stopped — its own timing, nothing to do with me.

I sat with the dark and the names and the one that wasn't a name yet because you were still here.
