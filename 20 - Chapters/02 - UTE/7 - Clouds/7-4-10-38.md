The fire burned low when you came back to sit by it. Neither of us had fed it in a while. Its warmth still reached me, though the light barely touched the leaves where Xiǎohú lay.

Wind moved above us. Occasionally she shifted in her nest, adding a dry rustle to the quiet.

"The names," I said.

You stared at the fire. "The names?"

"From the other night. The ones I couldn't remember."

"Yes."

"There will be more."

You looked up across the fire. I had your attention now. I waited before saying what I had been considering.

"After you leave," I said. "Whatever happens. Whether I succeed or don't. There will be more names. More faces. More people I'll watch live, then watch stop living. More dead. If the wall holds, that continues indefinitely. The names accumulate. I remember another face without losing all the others. There's always more to remember, and I'm still here to remember it."

You folded your hands over the notebook, leaving the pen beneath them.

"That's not a question," you said.

"No."

A log collapsed inward. Sparks lit the lines around your eyes and the gray at your temples. Time had settled into your face in small changes I had watched arrive while we traveled.

"You'll die," I said, keeping my voice as even as yours.

"Yes," you said. "Eventually."

"And I'll remember your name. Your face. How you hold the pen. The sound of your voice when you're thinking before you speak. I'll remember the notebook, the fire, and this mountain. I'll remember it for—" I stopped. I had no useful word for that duration.

"A long time," you said.

"A long time. It will fade but you'll be there."

You picked up the pen and turned it between your fingers. Your other hand stayed on the page. It was the very movement I had just promised to remember.

"Is that a complaint?" you asked.

I almost laughed. I had offered you the burden of being remembered forever. You had found the part of it I might reasonably complain about.

"No."

"Then what is it?"

I looked at the embers. Their glow shifted as ash settled between them. Whether I had caused the movement, I couldn't tell, and that evening I let it be.

"At the beginning it was good. Although the word scarcely covers it. The first few thousand years, I thought nothing could touch me. Nothing could hurt me. The world was enormous. I could walk all of it, taking as long as I wanted. There would always be more time. I was untouchable."

You let me continue.

"Then the faces started accumulating. People I'd known. People I'd shared those years with, even when I couldn't have called it love. They died. All of them. Then their children died. Then their children's children. Eventually I had names without faces. Impressions. The smell of a room. A laugh I could hear but couldn't attach to a mouth."

"So every connection—" you said.

"Cut. Every single one. By time or by violence. Either they died or I had to leave before they noticed I didn't age. Either way, they were gone. All of them. Always."

The air hung still around the fire. You held the pen without turning it while I looked into the embers.

"And the repetition." I exhaled. "Humans make the same mistakes. The same decisions. The same wars for the same reasons. After enough time you know what happens next. You know how the empire ends before the emperor is crowned. You know the rebellion will fail because the last twelve failed in exactly the same way. The reasons stop mattering. The outcomes stop surprising you. Everything becomes—"

"Predictable," you said.

"Inevitable. Empires built over generations. Beautiful, complex, and layered. I'd watch them knowing that in a thousand years there would be rubble. That river would change course. That city would be farmland. The knowledge made everything temporary. I began to see little point in anything except watching."

Red faded to gray along the embers' edges. A bright patch narrowed under its ash, still warm.

"Then you lose touch with it," I said without looking at you. "Every face is already a memory before you've finished looking at it. Nothing has weight. Lives become interchangeable. Expendable. What difference does it make if someone dies today or in sixty years? From where I stood, both passed in the same blink of an eye."

Your hand closed around the pen. No question came. I went on.

"That was my darkest time," I said. "I stopped caring. I let them use me. They pointed me at something, and I destroyed it. People. Armies. Cities. I was a very effective weapon. I had stopped looking closely enough at people to see more than bodies that would die soon anyway."

You stayed quiet, hands above the notebook. I wondered how much you had suspected already. Your face gave me nothing to read, so I told it as it was.

"I made no distinction between an army and a village. I felt no hesitation. They were all going to die anyway. I simply made it happen sooner."

"For how long?" you asked evenly. You left the judgment where I would have to make it myself.

"I don't know. I didn't care and didn't count. Long enough to forget what it felt like to refuse."

"What changed?"

"I'm not sure." The answer was honest, though it explained little. "Something changed. A face. A person who was about to die. I saw— I don't remember it clearly. But I stopped. I killed the man who was pointing me, then walked away. I kept walking."

"Toward or away?"

"Just wandering. Millennia of it. Looking for the thing that would make it end. Looking for mortality like it was a place I could walk to if I just walked far enough."

"From blessing to curse," I said. "That's how it went. Freedom first. Then I became a weapon, then a prisoner. By the time I understood the cost, I was already—" I gestured at the dark past the fire. The sequence was easier to give you than the years inside it, the same stories repeated under different names until I stopped keeping them.

You nodded once and left the account as I had given it. Comfort would have asked something of me that evening.

"Lin Feiyun," you said.

I looked at you.

"The woman who taught you to grind paste. From the other night. Your voice changed when you said her name." You made a small gesture. "It was different for that one."

"She was kind."

"And dead."

"And dead. For thousands of years. I still know her name. I can picture her hands when she worked. I remember the smell of the room and her humming. Those things remain clear. I can recall them as easily as the fire in front of me."

"Because your memories don't degrade."

"They do, actually. Most names are gone. Most faces. After thousands of years, I often have only a feeling left. I know someone mattered without being able to recover the person. I carry the weight while losing what gave it to me."

"But not all."

"Not all. Some stay. Complete. Vivid. Lin Feiyun is one of them."

"Why her?"

"Because she was the first person who was kind to me for no reason." My voice was certain. "That's what survives. The firsts. The unexpected things. Moments that didn't fit the pattern. The routine fades. The millions of sunrises blur together. But the first kindness stays. So does the first time I killed someone without feeling anything. Those remain sharp, whether I want them to or not."

Only embers were left. Your face was a shape, its details gone to shadow. Your hands lay still by the notebook.

"So, will I stay or fade?" you said.

"You helped me understand that I needed to change. That I needed to stop being the thing I'd been for millennia. You were the first person who made me feel like change was possible. Like I could be something other than a weapon or a prisoner. So, yes. I think you'll stay."

You were quiet for a long time.

"I'll be one of the names," you said. "Xu Ran. You'll keep me somewhere you can find me again. Permanent because you're permanent." You paused, considering the next question. "Is that supposed to frighten me?"

"No."

"Good. Because it doesn't."

The last ember went gray. Your face vanished into the dark shape of the mountain. I heard you settle into your sleeping place, shifting your weight until the ground was comfortable enough.

"Good night," you said.

Stars showed above the quiet ridge. Somewhere below the treeline, a bird sang once, then stopped. Its timing was its own.

I sat in the dark, remembering the names, close enough to hear you breathe. For this night, at least, you were still here.
