The fire is low. The kind of fire that happens when nobody feeds it because nobody needs the light, only the warmth, and the warmth is almost optional.

Xu Ran returns and sits by the fire. The wind and the occasional shift of Xiǎohú's body in whatever nest of leaves she has constructed are the only sounds.

"The names," Yun says.

He doesn't look up, sunken in thoughts. "The names?" he says.

"From the other night," she says. "The ones I couldn't remember."

"Yes."

"There will be more."

Xu Ran looks at her across the fire with the expression of a man who understands that a conversation is beginning and the conversation is not casual.

"After you leave," she says. "After — whatever happens. Whether I succeed or don't. There will be more names. More faces. More people I watched exist and then watched stop existing. More dead. And if I don't succeed — if the wall holds — then it continues. Indefinitely. The names accumulate. The faces stack. The archive grows and grows and the archivist can't die and can't stop recording."

He folds his hands over the notebook. The gesture of a man who is listening with his whole body.

"That's not a question," he says.

"No."

Silence. The fire shifts. A log collapses inward, the structural failure of wood consumed from the inside, and the brief flare of sparks illuminates his face — the lines around his eyes, the grey at his temples, the evidence of time written on a surface that time is allowed to write on.

"You'll die," she says.

Flat. She is adopting his register for the sentences that are too heavy for any other delivery.

"Yes," he says. "Eventually."

"And I'll remember your name. Your face. The way you hold the pen. The sound of your voice when you're thinking before you speak. I'll remember the notebook and the fire and this mountain. And I'll remember it for — " The word doesn't form. The duration too large for language.

"A long time," he says.

"A long time. It will fade but you'll be there."

He picks up the pen. Turns it in his fingers. Not writing — the fidget of a man processing something.

"Is that a complaint?" he asks.

The question is so dry, so stripped of sympathy, that she almost laughs. Almost.

"No."

"Then what is it?"

She looks at the fire. The embers are arranging themselves in patterns that might be hers or might be physics. She can't tell anymore. It doesn't matter.

"At the beginning it was — good. Although the word is insufficient. The first few thousand years. Nothing could touch me. Nothing could hurt me. The world was enormous and I could walk through all of it and take as long as I wanted because time was mine. An infinite resource. I was untouchable."

He doesn't interrupt.

"Then the faces started accumulating. People I'd known. People I'd — not loved, not exactly, but inhabited the same years with. They died. All of them. And then their children died. And their children's children. And eventually the names lost their faces. Became impressions. The smell of a room. A laugh I could hear but couldn't attach to a mouth."

"So every connection— " he says.

"Cut." The word is small and absolute. "Every single one. By time or by violence. Either they died or I had to leave before they noticed I didn't age. Either way — gone. All of them. Always."

The fire doesn't move. The air doesn't move. The stillness of a mountain listening.

"And the repetition. That was — " She exhales. "Humans make the same mistakes. The same decisions. The same wars for the same reasons. After enough time you know what happens next. You know how the empire ends before the emperor is crowned. You know the rebellion will fail because the last twelve rebellions failed in exactly the same way. The reasons stop mattering. The outcomes stop surprising. Everything becomes — "

"Predictable," he says.

"Inevitable. Empires built over generations. Beautiful, complex, layered. And I'd watch them and know — in a thousand years this will be rubble. That river will change course. That city will be farmland. The knowledge made everything temporary. Made everything pointless except the watching."

She watches the embers. Red fading to grey at the edges, the slow death of heat.

"And then you go numb." She says it without looking at him. "If everything is temporary — if every face is already a memory before you've finished looking at it — then nothing has weight. Nothing matters. Lives become — interchangeable. Expendable. What difference does it make if someone dies today or in sixty years? From where I stood it was the same blink of an eye."

The silence is different now. Denser.

"That was my darkest time," she says. "The time when I stopped caring. When I let them use me. Point me at a thing and I'd destroy it. People. Armies. Cities. I was a weapon. A very effective one. Because nothing mattered and nothing hurt and people were just — bodies. Temporary arrangements of temporary matter doing temporary things."

Xu Ran looks at her in silence. Listening. His hands are still. The stillness of a man hearing something he suspected but hadn't been told.

"No morality. No hesitation. No distinction between an army or a village. They were all going to die anyway. I just — accelerated the schedule."

"For how long?" he asks. His voice is level. The factual register. No judgment in it and no absolution.

"I don't know. I didn't care and didn't count. Long enough to forget what it felt like to refuse."

"What changed?"

"I'm not sure." The honest answer. The insufficient one. "Something — cracked. A face. A person who was about to die and I saw — I don't remember. But I stopped. I killed the man who was pointing me and walked away. I kept walking."

"Towards or away?"

"Just wandering. Millennia of it. Looking for the thing that would make it end. Looking for mortality like it was a place I could walk to if I just walked far enough."

"From Blessing to curse," she says. "That's the trajectory. That's what immortality is. A thing that starts as freedom then becomes a weapon and later becomes a prison. By the time you understand its cost, it is already — " She gestures at nothing. At everything. At the accumulated weight of millennia spent watching the same story repeat with different names in the margins.

He nods. The single nod that means he has received information, filed it and won't offer comfort because comfort is not what is being requested.

"Lin Feiyun," he says.

She looks at him.

"The woman who taught you to grind paste. From the other night. You said the name and your voice — " He makes a small gesture. "The voice was different for that one."

"She was kind."

"And dead."

"And dead. For thousands of years. And I still know her name and what her hands looked like when she was working. I still remember the smell of the room and the way she hummed. Thousands of years and the memory hasn't faded. Hasn't blurred. Hasn't done what memories are supposed to do."

"Because your memories don't degrade."

"They do, actually. Most of it. Most names are gone. Most faces. Thousands of years of people I knew and all that's left is — residue. A feeling without a source. The weight of someone without the someone."

"But not all."

"Not all. Some stay. Complete. Vivid. Lin Feiyun is one of them."

"Why her?"

"Because she was the first person who was kind to me for no reason." She says it and knows it is true. "That's what survives. The firsts. The things that were unexpected. The moments that didn't fit the pattern. Everything else — the routine, the repetition, the millions of sunrises — that degrades. But the first time someone was kind. The first time I killed someone and felt nothing. The first — " She stops. "Those stay. Sharp. Permanent. Whether I want them to or not."

The fire is embers now. The darkness has expanded to fill the space the light occupied. His face is shadow and suggestion — the geometry of a person rather than the detail.

"So, will I stay or fade," he says.

"You helped me understand that I needed to change. That I needed to stop being the thing I'd been for millennia. You were the first person who made me feel like change was possible. Like I could be something other than a weapon or a prisoner. So, yes. I think you'll stay."

He is quiet for a long time. 

"I'll be one of the names," he says. "Xu Ran. Filed. Stored. Retrievable. Permanent because you're permanent. Is that — " He pauses. The editorial pause. "Is that supposed to frighten me?"

"No."

"Good. Because it doesn't."

The fire dies. The last ember goes grey.

She can't see his face anymore. Just the dark shape of him against the darker shape of the mountain. But she can hear the sound of him settling — the adjustment of a body finding its sleeping position, the small reorganization of limbs and weight that precedes rest.

"Good night," he says.

The mountain is quiet. The stars are visible. Somewhere below the treeline, a bird sings once and stops — its own timing, nothing to do with her.

She sits with the dark and the names and the one that isn't a name yet because he is still here.
