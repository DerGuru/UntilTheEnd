You had been watching me all morning.

Small bricks. "This leaf could fall." It fell. "This water could cool." It cooled. "This stone could split." It split. Each one a claim against the natural order, each one effortless — the easy bricks, the ones that didn't touch the self. The mirror question from yesterday still had no answer. But the bricks were accumulating.

At some point you stopped writing. Set the pen down and leaned your back against the boulder with your legs stretched and your hands resting. Your face set in the blankness that indicated maximum internal activity.

"You said, that you shape the world like clay," you said.

"No." The correction was important — the distinction that lived at the center of everything. "The world was — is — clay. I just didn't know I was the one with hands."

You tried for yourself. Of course you tried. The scholar's imperative — the need to test, to verify, to subject every phenomenon to the acid of empirical inquiry. You turned to a stone near your feet. Small, grey, ordinary. You stared at it with the concentrated intensity of a man applying every cognitive resource to a single, exact outcome.

Nothing happened.

You concentrated harder. Your jaw tightened. Your hands clenched on your knees. The stone — roundish, palm-sized, grey — remained exactly where it was, doing exactly what stones did. Nothing.

You leaned back.

"It was worth a try,  Maybe I have hands, too." You said.

The observation was analytical. Not "unfair" or the emotional response to inequity. A clinical filing of a data point. The capacity to shape reality through conviction was not a universal faculty that anyone could develop. It was deliberate. Singular. The result of a set of conditions that no one else had experienced because no one else had lived long enough to accumulate.

You looked at me. The processing-face shifted into the consideration-face — subtly different, the same way a knife's edge and a scalpel's edge are subtly different. Both sharp. Different purposes.

"If the world was clay," you said. "And you didn't know you had hands. What have you been doing?"

"Unconsciously shaping."

"Everything?"

"Everything within range."

"What's the range?"

I didn't know. I didn't even want think about it. The question's implications were too large for a mountainside conversation on a afternoon in spring. But if the world was clay. And if I had been shaping it for millennia. And if I hadn't known — then every tree, every river, every weather pattern, every geological feature within some unknowable radius might have been — adjusted. Shaped by the passive pressure of an unconscious conviction so massive it bent reality by existing.

"The implication," you said. Slowly. Choosing words the way a surgeon chose instruments. "If you are the only one. And the world is clay. And your hands have been working for thousands of years —"

"I know."

"— then nothing we see is untouched."

"I know."

"Every sunrise. Every rainfall. The growth patterns of forests, the courses of rivers, the—"

"I KNOW."

The sharpness surprised me. My voice — louder than intended. Edged. The calm of training washed away. But the implication was too large and too true. And saying it out loud made it real in a way that knowing it privately didn't.

You stopped and looked at me. Your expression recalibrated. A softening around the eyes, a loosening of the jaw, the physical signals of a man who recognized he had pushed past the productive boundary.

"Sorry," you said.

"Don't be."

"The—"

"It's accurate."

"Accurate things can still be too much."

We sat. The clearing. The split stone near my feet. The leaf, on the ground where it had fallen. The stream below us, audible but not visible. The ordinary landscape of a morning that had contained too many implications.

"What does it feel like?" you asked. Quieter now. "When you — shape?"

"It's like remembering. Like knowing something you already knew. The leaf floats — and you remember that leaves float. The water rises — and you remember that water rises. It's — correcting the world. As if it had been doing it wrong and you're reminding it."

"Reminding the world."

"Yes."

You wrote that down. The pen moved with the slow, careful strokes of a man recording something that frightened you and fascinated you in equal measure.

Xiǎohú emerged from behind the boulder where she had been hiding during the training — the tactical withdrawal of an animal that preferred not to be within range of reality corrections — and trotted across the clearing to sit equidistant from both of us. The amber eyes moved from face to face. Whatever she assessed, she kept.

We sat with the implication. The world was clay. I had been working on it for millennia. The shapes I had made — unknowable, uncountable, the invisible fingerprints of an unconscious mind on every surface of a reality.

The clouds moved overhead. Formed, dissolved, reformed. The only things in the sky without pretense.
