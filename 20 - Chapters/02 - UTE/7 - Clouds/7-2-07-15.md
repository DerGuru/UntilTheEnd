You sat beside the boulder with your notebook while I worked through small changes. "This leaf could fall." It fell. "This water could cool." It cooled. "This stone could split." It split. Each alteration came easily so long as it was outside me. Yesterday's question about a mirror still had no answer. I practiced anyway.

At some point you stopped writing. You set the pen down, then leaned against the boulder with your legs stretched out. Your hands lay still by the notebook. I had learned to wait when you looked like that.

"You said that you shape the world like clay."

"No." I looked at the split stone, searching for words that kept the distinction clear. "The world is clay. It always was. I just didn't know I was the one with hands."

You tried it yourself. I had expected that sooner or later. You turned to a small gray stone near your feet, leaving the notebook where it lay. You stared at it with the same attention you had given my attempts.

Nothing happened.

You concentrated harder. Your jaw tightened; your hands clenched on your knees. The roundish stone was small enough to fit in your palm. It lay on the ground through all of it.

You leaned back.

"It was worth a try. Maybe I have hands too," you said.

You eyed the stone once more, then left it alone. I had acquired this capacity through conditions neither of us could reproduce for you, an accumulation so long that even I could scarcely measure it. Your attempt had given us one small piece of evidence, hardly an explanation. You accepted that much.

You turned back to me. Your brow eased, but your attention was as close as it had been on the stone. I waited for the next question.

"If the world was clay," you said. "And you didn't know you had hands. What have you been doing?"

"Unconsciously shaping."

"Everything?"

"Everything within range."

"What's the range?"

I didn't know, and I was reluctant to consider it. Trees stood past the clearing; a river ran somewhere below us. If I had been shaping things for millennia without knowing, either might bear the effects. So might the weather above this mountainside. In every direction I found no boundary I trusted.

"The implication," you said slowly. "If you are the only one, if the world is clay, and if your hands have been working for thousands of years—"

"I know."

"— then nothing we see is untouched."

"I know."

"Every sunrise. Every rainfall. The growth patterns of forests, the courses of rivers, the—"

"I KNOW."

My voice came out louder than I intended, its edge ringing across the clearing. I had been able to look at a tree and keep the possibility at a distance. Hearing you name everything around us closed that distance.

You stopped. Your jaw loosened, and the question you had been forming went unspoken. I let my hands settle on my knees.

"Sorry," you said.

"Don't be."

"The—"

"It's accurate."

"Accurate things can still be too much."

We sat in the clearing. The split stone lay near my feet, the leaf on the ground where it had fallen. Below us the stream ran out of sight. I tried to hear it as simply running water.

"What does it feel like?" you asked more quietly. "When you shape?"

"It's like remembering. Like knowing something you already knew. The leaf floats, and you remember that leaves float. The water rises, and you remember that water rises. It feels like correcting the world. As if it had been doing it wrong, so you're reminding it."

"Reminding the world."

"Yes."

You wrote that down in slow, careful strokes, glancing up between phrases at the objects around my feet. Your questions could wait.

Xiǎohú came out from behind the boulder where she had spent the training and trotted across the clearing to sit halfway between us. Her amber eyes went from your face to mine. She tucked her paws under her.

I looked past her to the trees. I had been working on this world for millennia. I could neither count the changes nor recognize all the shapes I might have left behind.

Overhead a cloud thinned in the wind, and its edge disappeared.
