The rice burns.

She stands at the stove, watching the pot the way she has learned to watch pots — with the patient attention of a woman who knows that inattention has consequences — and still the rice at the bottom turns brown, then dark. The smell reaches her before the color does. Familiar. Warm. The specific char of rice that has been left too long at too high a heat, a smell she knows the way you know a melody you haven't heard in years.

Xu Ran.

His burned rice. The mediocre consistency that she endured for months of mountain walking, that she complained about and ate and complained about again. He had burned rice the way other people breathed — reliably, consistently, without apparent effort or concern. Not because he couldn't cook. He demonstrated that once, at the end, the one meal that proved he had been choosing not to. But the burning was his default, his culinary signature, the expression of a man who understood the process but could not be bothered with the patience.

She has made his mistake. The water ratio is wrong. The heat too high. The exact same parametric error that produces burned-bottom-adequate-top, the geological stratification of overcooked and underdone that he perfected and she has now inherited.

She eats it. Standing. The burned part is crunchy. The adequate part is adequate. The overall experience is — his. She is eating Xu Ran's rice, in a kitchen he never entered, years after he closed a notebook and walked away. And the echo is not painful.

That is new. Not-painful. The memory of the man who burned rice arrives and sits beside her and does not hurt. It is warm. The particular warmth of a thing remembered with fondness — not the sharp fondness that cuts, but the softer kind, the kind that time produces when time is allowed to work on memories the way it works on everything else.

She sets the bowl down. The bowl is empty. The pot is burnt. She scrubs the carbon from the iron with patient, circular strokes, the thinking-without-thinking that hands perform when they are occupied and the mind is free to wander.

She thinks about collecting.

Not objects. Memories. The conscious decision to notice and retain. Before, memories happened to her — arrived without consent, accumulated like sediment in a river that never stopped flowing. Millennia of sediment. Millennia of arrivals she never asked for. Now she chooses. Looks at a moment and decides: this one.

The light through the kitchen window at this time of day — late afternoon, golden, falling at the angle that makes dust visible and everything it touches look warmer than it is. The way the light lands on the counter and illuminates the grain of the wood, the small imperfections, the ring-stain from a cup she set down too many times in the same place. This one.

The sound of the Counter's voice from the lane, distant, counting. The numerical narration of a child who treats the world as arithmetic and solves it constantly, her voice rising and falling with the cadence of someone who has found her purpose and does not need anyone to confirm it. This one.

The smell of the plum tree's blossoms in spring. The sweet-light scent that the flowers produce for the brief weeks of their display, delicate and temporary and all the more valuable for the temporary. This one.

Collecting. Not the passive accumulation of millennia, but the active curation of a life. The conscious decision to notice the moments as they occur, to look at them clearly and say: yes, you. I see you. I am keeping you.

She cooks dinner. Better than the rice. Bean stew, seasoned with ginger from the edge of the garden where the ginger grows in a disorganized tangle that she has stopped trying to organize. The stew is acceptable. Genuinely acceptable. The kind of meal that would not impress anyone but would not disappoint them either, and that middle ground feels like a victory she did not know she was fighting for.

She eats. At the table. Sitting. The candle is lit. The stew is warm. The evening is arriving with the patience that mountain evenings have — slow, unhurried, the sky giving up its colors one at a time, from gold to copper to the deep blue that exists for ten minutes and never repeats exactly.

Xiǎohú is under the table, in the position of an animal that has learned that human meals sometimes produce floor-level dividends. The optimistic proximity of hope. She does not drop anything. Xiǎohú's patience is admirable and unrewarded.

Loneliness is different now. She can feel the difference the way you feel a change in weather — not all at once, but in the shifted quality of the air. The loneliness of before was the loneliness of a woman who could not connect, who was too different, too old, too permanent for the temporary world around her. This loneliness is the loneliness of a woman who is between connections. Interval. Pause. The space between that lets the light through, the opening that makes the walls meaningful.

She sits at the table. Alone. In the quiet house. The candle burns. The stew cools. Outside, the last of the evening colors drains from the sky and the first stars appear — or the first ones she notices, which is a different thing.

She is alone the way a room is empty. Available.

This, she thinks. This one, too.

She collects the moment and adds it to the others.
