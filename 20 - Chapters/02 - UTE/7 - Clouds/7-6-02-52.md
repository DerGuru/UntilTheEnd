Evening arrives and she spends it in her new home. 

It is funny, how that word had changed its meanings over the years, she muses.

The candle on the table produces a circle of light that is smaller than she expected — the radius of visibility that one wick achieves when one wick is all that is available instead of a fire on the ground. The table smells of old wood. Cedar, maybe. Or oak. She has known the difference for centuries. It does not matter.

The candle has burned to its last third. The wax pools around the wick, feeding the flame that is — finite. The flame will end. The wax will end. The light will end.

The thought arrives before she can stop it. Not the candle's ending. Hers.

She lets it be.

This is new. The thought is there — and she does not wall it off. Does not suppress and bury it under millennia of practiced numbness. The fear of ending sits in the room like the smell of old wood. 

She sits at the table. In the candlelight. With it.

The candle flickers. Wind from the window — the frame does not seal properly, the draft entering through the gap between wood and wall. The flame bends. Recovers. The shadow on the wall moves and returns.

She breathes. It breathes. The candle breathes.

A knock.

The door. Someone is at the door.

She opens it.

A woman. Short and wide. The typical body that a village life produces — built by labor rather than exercise, shaped by function rather than intention. She holds a pot that steams.

"I thought you might need —" The woman holds the pot higher. Rice. Real rice — not the grain Xu Ran had prepared for the last days of his companionship. The lid is slightly askew, the steam escaping in the thin curl that indicates fresh cooking, recent heat, food that was is being shared with another. 

"Leftovers from dinner."

"Thank you."

"You're thin."

The woman's observation is delivered without preliminary, without softening, without the social padding that urban interaction requires. Direct. Unasked. This is how village neighbors are.

The woman notices Xiǎohú on the bed. Her eyebrows rise — mild surprise, the moderate elevation that indicates curiosity without judgment. "Pretty animal." She sets the pot on the table. "Eat." Not a suggestion.

She leaves. The door closes. The pot sits on the table beside the candle, the steam rising into the circle of light.

Yun eats.

The rice tastes like other-people's-rice. Competent. Seasoned. Made by hands that understand rice the way her hands once understood medicinal paste — through repetition, through practice and the accumulated skill of doing one thing often enough that it becomes excellent without becoming remarkable.

She eats slowly. The jaw works. The tongue tastes. The throat swallows. Her body accepts the nutrition with the quiet compliance of an organism performing its function.

The candle burns lower. The pot empties — mostly. She washes it, dries it, places a handful of dried herbs inside. The ones she carries without thinking — chamomile, wild mint, the residue of centuries of knowing which plants help. You do not return a pot empty.

The fear is still there.

She sits in the half-dark — the candle reduced to its last centimeter, the light contracting, the circle shrinking — and it is present. In the room. In the air.

But — quieter? Or has she just grown accustomed? The distinction is unclear. Perhaps there is no distinction. Perhaps growing accustomed is getting quieter? 

She sits with it, lets it be, and the letting-be is — something.

Something between victory and acceptance that does not have a word yet but might develop one if given enough time.

Having fear is something other than being in fear. The distinction is thin. The distinction is real. The distinction is the space in which a woman sits at a table eating a neighbor's rice while the thing that has defined her for millennia sits in the room and she lets it stay.

The candle goes out.

The dark is familiar now.

She goes to bed.
