By evening she is sitting at the table in her new home. The word has meant so many different things over the years. Here it means one room with a door she can close.

The candle makes a smaller circle of light than she expects. She has grown accustomed to a fire on the ground; one wick leaves most of the room in shadow. The table smells of old wood. Cedar, perhaps. Or oak. She has known how to tell the difference for centuries. Tonight she lets the question lie.

The candle has burned to its last third. Wax pools around the wick, feeding the finite flame. Soon the wax will be gone. The light will end with it.

Watching the small flame brings her own ending to mind.

She lets it be.

The thought stays where she can look at it. She has spent millennia turning away from this part of herself. Tonight the fear of ending is as plain as the smell of old wood. She stays at the table with it.

She sits at the table. In the candlelight. With it.

Wind enters through a gap at the window frame. The flame bends in the draft, then recovers. Its shadow shifts on the wall and returns to its shape.

She follows the movement through a breath of her own.

A knock.

The door. Someone is at the door.

She opens it.

A short, broad woman stands outside with a steaming pot. Her arms bear the strength of daily work. She holds the weight easily.

"I thought you might need—" The woman holds the pot higher. Rice, the kind Xu Ran had run out of before their last days together. The lid sits askew. A thin curl of steam escapes, carrying the smell of freshly cooked food.

"Leftovers from dinner."

"Thank you."

"You're thin."

The observation arrives as directly as the pot. Yun remembers neighbors who could assess a person's health from a doorway, then regard the assessment as permission to do something about it.

The woman's eyebrows lift at the sight of Xiǎohú on the bed. "Pretty animal." She sets the pot on the table. "Eat."

She leaves. The door closes. The pot sits on the table next to the candle, the steam rising into the circle of light.

Yun eats.

The rice tastes like other people's rice. Competent. Seasoned. Someone has made it often enough to get it right without much attention. Yun remembers grinding medicinal paste, the pressure under her hands. Practice could make a thing excellent without making it remarkable.

She eats slowly, each mouthful held long enough to taste the seasoning. The grains separate easily. She swallows and reaches for more while the pot is still warm.

The candle burns lower as she finishes most of the rice. She washes the pot, dries it, and places a handful of herbs inside. Chamomile with wild mint: plants she has carried for so long that finding them has become habit. A borrowed pot should go back with something in it.

The fear is still there.

She sits in the half-dark with the candle burned down to a fingerbreadth. Its light reaches less of the table now. The fear is close enough to name.

Perhaps it is quieter. Perhaps she has grown used to sitting with it. She weighs the difference over the pot of herbs. Either would make staying easier.

She lets the feeling be. The practice is unfamiliar, somewhere between endurance and acceptance. Given time, she may find a better word for it.

Having fear differs from living entirely inside it. The distinction is thin but real enough for this evening. She has eaten a neighbor's rice, prepared something to return, and sat at the table with the feeling that defined her.

The candle goes out.

The dark is familiar now.

She goes to bed.
