At dusk, flames moved under the rice pot while you wrote next to me. Xiǎohú lay nearby with her tawny eyes narrowed to slits. One ear turned toward sounds the fire drowned out for me.

The changing light brought back other fires. A face caught in the orange glow, then another, then a name I had not spoken in centuries. I stared at the flames while more came.

Some names reached me clearly before I could place the face. Others brought a hand, a voice, or a movement I knew at once. They had been out of reach for so long. Now I could scarcely hold one memory apart from the next.

Lin Feiyun. The woman who had taught me to grind medicinal paste. Her hands came back to me, grinding in slow circles. Her death did not. I only knew she was gone.

Zhang Wei, another Wei entirely. I had passed through his village during the second century or the third, in a reckoning I could no longer place. A birthmark covered half his face. He had offered me a piece of fruit; I had eaten it. The name had outlasted whatever happened to him afterward.

Faster.

A merchant in a river town followed him. Then a cook in a traveling company, then a soldier who had deserted to live as a woodcutter. He had made beautiful furniture. The grain of the wood still showed under his hands. Lives I had lost count of came back in fragments, with too little space between them to turn away.

You looked up from your writing with a question on your face. Eyebrows raised, mouth open.

I could not answer. The faces overlapped until I could barely separate them: the cook's burned hands, the soldier's quiet eyes, the child's birthmark, and the woman's grinding motion. I reached for one face. Another took its place.

"Yun."

Your voice said my name. Your face cleared beyond the fire, where you had been sitting all along.

"I can't—" I lost the rest of the sentence. Air shifted against my face. Out past the fire, grass was rising from the ground.

With each breath, it grew, browned, and fell back. The rhythm followed the changes in me. I recognized someone I had thought lost to memory; for an instant they seemed close enough to meet again. Then I remembered how long they had been dead.

Green shoots rose in the firelight. Their color faded as I drew another breath. They collapsed against the soil, leaving room for fresh growth in the same patches. It happened again, close enough that the tips cleared the old blades. The timing matched my breath too closely to mistake.

Your pen moved fast, the characters dense and urgent.

The names continued. I remembered a fisherman, then two sisters who ran a tea house. An old man had carved jade with shaking hands, leaving fine smooth lines in the stone. A woman had sung to horses because she believed they understood her. She was gone. So were the horses. Her singing came back without the song. Somewhere the jade still held the lines the old man had cut.

My breathing grew ragged. Small flowers around the campsite began to open, then wilt. The changes had spread beyond the grass nearest me. Petals folded as I struggled to draw a full breath; fresh ones opened when I let it go.

"Slow your breathing," you said.

I did. Air entered my throat, and I let it go more slowly. After a few breaths the pace eased. The grass changed more slowly with it. Flowers held their petals open longer before wilting.

Almost.

Names still came, but they separated now. A pause opened between one face and the next. I looked at you across the fire.

"What happened?" you asked.

"Names and places that don't exist anymore," I said. "Cities. Villages. Whole—" I gestured past the fire. Streets where later roads had run, settlements replaced by fields. I could scarcely tell you where one had ended and another begun.

Xiǎohú's eyes were fully open. Her gaze followed the changing grass, the flowers, and the irregular movement of the flames. The air was still, yet the fire shifted with each change in my breath.

"I don't know all the names," I said. "I knew them once. Some of them. But—"

"But what?"

"Somehow that's worse."

"Worse how?"

"The ones I can't remember. The ones I can't picture. I have names without faces or stories. I know they mattered. I know they're gone. But the ones that are—" I stopped, trying to recover one of those faces. I had lost the person long ago. Now even what I had kept of them was going.

You let the unfinished sentence stand.

The grass around the fire went brown at once. A circle two paces across lay still after all that growth. The last green tips darkened.

The flowers were closed. The fire was low.

I stopped talking. The names grew harder to hear over the crackle of the fire. Some were still within reach. I let them fade.

You set down your pen.

"That's enough for today," you said.

I said nothing. The fire dwindled. I sat with my hands in my lap, tired enough to let the next name pass without reaching after it.

The grass would regrow. Some of it.

Some of it wouldn't.
