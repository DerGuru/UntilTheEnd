Memories came at dusk. The liquidity of the past when the dam breaks — all of them surfacing simultaneously, the complete archive of a life measured in millennia deciding that now was the moment to empty itself.

We were sitting by the evening fire. The rice was cooking. You were writing. The daily ritual, the documentation, the obsessive recording that was both your purpose and your anchor. Xiǎohú was pretending to sleep, tawny eyes cracked to slits, one ear tracking sounds the rest of us couldn't hear.

I was looking at the fire.

That was a mistake. The flames, the color, the movement, the way light danced on surfaces and made shadows leap. They were the connective tissue to everything that had existed in the temperature range between cold and too hot. The fire was a door. I opened it.

Names poured in. Faces followed, the visual reconstruction lagging behind the verbal like subtitles lagged behind speech. Names I hadn't spoken in centuries. Names I hadn't thought in longer. Names that had been filed in the mental archive where the mind stores things it can't use and can't discard. The dead.

Lin Feiyun. The woman who had taught me to grind medicinal paste. Dead. How? When? The details were gone but the name was permanent.

Zhang Wei — another Wei entirely, a child in a village I'd passed through during the second century or the third, a boy with a birthmark that covered half his face like a map of a country that didn't exist. He'd offered me a piece of fruit. I'd eaten it. He'd died of something. Later. The specifics erased by time but the name remaining.

Faster.

A merchant in a river town. A cook in a traveling company. A soldier who had deserted, lived as a woodcutter and made furniture that was beautiful. All dead. All gone. All stored. Filed. Retained by a mind that couldn't forget because the body wouldn't let it, the immortal cruelty of a memory that expanded rather than compressed, that accumulated rather than pruned.

You looked up from your writing with a question on your face. Eyebrows raised, mouth slightly open.

I didn't answer because the answer was still arriving, the count still incrementing, the flood still flowing. Faces now, thousands of them, overlapping, translucent, the visual congestion of too many images competing for the same mental space. The cook's burned hands, the soldier's quiet eyes, the child's birthmark, the woman's grinding motion and —

"Yun."

Your voice was anchor. The sound that cut through the noise, the combination of vowel and consonant that my mind had been trained to recognize as important, as present, as now.

"I can't — " The sentence fractured. The air fractured. Around us, the grass was — breathing.

That was the only word for it. The grass around the fire was growing and dying and growing and dying in rhythm with my breathing, A botanical oscillation of a landscape responding to the emotional frequency of a woman whose feelings were alternating between grief and the feeling you have, when you meet an old friend after years apart. Between then and now.

The grass grew. Green shoots rising impossibly fast, reaching toward a sun that wasn't there, the nighttime growth of vegetation powered by me rather than photosynthesis. Then the shoots browned, collapsed and returned to the soil they'd risen from. Just to start growing again. The cycle was visible, organic, and it matched the rate of my breathing with a precision that was the no coincidence.

Your pen moved. You were writing fast, the characters appearing dense and urgent. 

The names continued. A fisherman. Two sisters who ran a tea house. An old man who carved jade with hands that shook and produced work that didn't. A woman who sang to horses because she believed they understood, maybe they did. She was dead. The horses were dead. The songs were dead. The jade had survived, because jade was stone. Stone outlasted everything except time and time outlasted everything.

My breathing was ragged. The grass still responded. Grow. Die. Grow. Die. The cycle around me was expanding — not just grass now but the small flowers that dotted the campsite, opening and closing, blooming and wilting, the floral equivalent of hyperventilation.

"Slow your breathing," you said.

Control the automatic. Override the autonomous. Management of a force that wasn't designed for management but demanded it anyway.

The breathing slowed, marginally, the modest deceleration of a body negotiating with its own reflexes. The grass cycle slowed with it. The flowers opened and closed and opened at a pace that was almost natural.

Almost.

The names were still coming. Quieter now. The aftermath of a flood, the receding water revealing the landscape it had covered, the inventory of damage that followed inundation.

"What happened?" you asked.

"Names and places that don't exist anymore," I said. "Cities. Villages. Whole —" I gestured at nothing. At everything. At the spatial absence that represented a geography that had been replaced by other geography, the palimpsest of landscape that only immortals could read.

Xiǎohú's eyes were fully open now. The amber watched the grass breathing, the flowers cycling and the fire flickering in patterns that weren't wind-patterns but grief-patterns, the irregular rhythm of a flame responding to an emotion rather than an atmosphere.

"I don't know all the names," I said. "I knew them once. Some of them. But —"

"But what?"

"Somehow that's worse."

"Worse how?"

"The ones I can't remember. The ones I can't picture. The ones that are just names without faces, without stories, without anything except the fact that they existed and mattered and are now gone. The ones that are — " I couldn't find the words to describe it. The horror of partial forgetting. It made you feel like you were losing them all over again.

You understood. 

The grass died. All of it. A circle around the fire, two meters in diameter, going brown simultaneously, the synchronized botanical death of vegetation that had exhausted itself cycling between life and death at the frequency of a woman's breathing.

The flowers were closed. The fire was low.

I stopped talking. The names retreated — not gone, never gone, just receding to the depth where they lived between summonings.

You set down your pen.

"That's enough for today," you said.

I agreed. Not with words. With silence. The silence of a woman who had just reviewed the inventory of her losses, found it uncountable and was now sitting by a fire that was dying because she was tired. 

The grass would regrow. Some of it.

Some of it wouldn't.
