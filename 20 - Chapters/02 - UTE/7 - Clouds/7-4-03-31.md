The next day. Again. I sat in the same place per your suggestion. Your rationale being that variable reduction improved diagnosis, that if the location was constant then any change in result could be attributed only to the process rather than the position. Scientific thinking applied to something that wasn't science. Your specialty.

*I am mortal.*

I sent it deeper. Volume was irrelevant in the interior, where sound was a metaphor and force was measured in intention. I sent it stronger in the sense of more committed, more deliberate, the increased investment of a woman who had failed yesterday and was applying yesterday's failure as today's instruction.

***I** am mortal. I **am** mortal. I am **mortal**.*

The emphasis shifted like weight on a scale, the stress moving from word to word, testing each syllable's load-bearing capacity, searching for the arrangement that would penetrate the wall.

Something small happened.

Less than breakthrough — the word was too large, too architecturally definitive for what actually occurred. Something. Behind the wall. An auditory impression of a noise heard through insulation — muffled, indistinct, present but not clear. Like an echo. The acoustic evidence of space beyond the barrier, the proof that the wall had another side and the far side contained something.

I opened my eyes. The clearing was unchanged. The stream ran. The birds were silent. Had they been silent before? I couldn't remember. The uncertainty of a woman whose attention had been interior. 

"Something happened," I said.

Your pen was moving before I finished the word. You'd been watching — the sustained observation of a diagnostician monitoring for symptom change, the visual equivalent of a stethoscope pressed to the patient's surface.

"Describe it," you said.

"An echo. Behind — behind everything. Like a sound on the other side of something thick."

"Duration?"

"Brief. Half a breath."

You wrote. The characters were small, precise, the calligraphic density of data collection. You noted the duration. You noted the conditions. You noted the time of day, the position of my body, the expression on my face during the attempt.

"Could it have been environmental?" you asked. "Something in the clearing? The stream? The birds?"

I looked. The clearing. The stream. The grass. The — had the leaves moved? The leaves on the tree nearest my position were — still. The frozen stillness of objects arrested mid-motion. As though the air around them had thickened for a fraction of the duration of my attempt and the leaves had been caught in the thickening.

"I don't think so," I said. "Maybe. The leaves. But that would haven sounded differently. This was internal."

You looked. You noted.

"Again?" you said.

"Not today."

The exhaustion was different from the external-warping exhaustion. That had been like the muscular depletion of a body expending energy on something that shouldn't require energy. This was — deeper. Identity-deep. The fatigue of a self that had been asked to look at itself and had found the looking more expensive than the doing.

We ate. More of the not-rice. The default meal that characterized our travel — the culinary baseline beneath which your cooking could not descend and above which it could not rise. I ate mechanically. The jaw worked. The tongue positioned. The body performed its feeding function without the mind's active participation.

"What could that echo mean?" I asked.

"What does an echo require?" you asked.

"Space. On the other side."

"And what does hearing it require?"

"Permeability."

"So we have two findings. There is something on the other side of the wall. And the wall isn't absolute. It's permeable to some degree."

The logic was — reasonable. There was something on the other side. Something that had been inaccessible before but was now — present in some form, however faint. Also, if the wall had been truly impenetrable, truly total, the echo should have been impossible. The echo was evidence of imperfection — a crack, a thin spot, a vulnerability in the structure that the structure's designer hadn't intended. Or had they? Or had it become permeable over the last months? Was I succeeding, but very slowly?

I was the structure's designer.

The fact was recursive and exhausting. I had built the wall, I was the wall, I was trying to breach the wall. The wall's weakness was my weakness, my weakness the tool I needed to exploit it, and the circularity of the whole structure was the kind of logical topology that made the mind tired just from tracing it.

"Symptom," you said. You were writing again. "Diagnosis. Treatment." You paused. Looked at me. "Look at it as any healer would. Except the patient and the disease are the same thing."

"Is that supposed to help?"

"It's supposed to structure. We already have established the first two. Treatment— "

"That's the part I need."

"That comes later."

The afternoon arrived. The sun moved. The clouds — mine? not mine? — drifted. The atmospheric indifference of a sky that had no opinion about the struggle taking place beneath it.

I washed the bowls. A human procedure that anchored the extraordinary in the mundane, that placed the impossible task of self-transformation in the context of dirty dishes and cold stream water.

Tomorrow I would try again. The echo would be there or it wouldn't. The wall would crack or it wouldn't. The self would budge or it wouldn't. Given time it may.

Something had passed through today. Something had echoed.

The wall wasn't absolute.

That was — something. 

That was a start.
