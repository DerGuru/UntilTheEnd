The rice was gone. The moment when you tilted the sack and nothing came out was a statement. You folded the empty cloth, tucked it into the pack with the same precision you applied to everything and said: "We need food."

We. The word that included me in the practical apparatus of survival despite the fact that my survival required nothing. I didn't need food. My body didn't degrade. The hunger I occasionally felt was aesthetic, not biological — the ghost-appetite of a body that remembered eating the way an amputee remembered a limb.

And while Xiǎohú could tend for herself, you needed food. And the practical reality of a mountain trail with two living creatures who required caloric input meant that someone needed to hunt.

"I'll go hunting," I said.

You looked at me. The look that assessed whether an offer was genuine or performative, whether the woman who hadn't participated in meal preparation for weeks was now volunteering for the most active form of food acquisition.

"Rabbit would be good," you said. "If you can find one."

I would find one. Qi mapped the living things within range — the warm, as I'd called it once, before I had the word. Two rabbits in the undergrowth sixty steps east. A family of marmots in a burrow below the trail. Birds. Insects. The thermal atlas of mountain life, spread before my perception like a map.

I took nothing. No weapon. No snare. No sharpened stick.

The trail east was steep and quiet. The undergrowth thickened where water seeped from a rock face — moss, ferns, the tangled vegetation that small animals used as cover. I stopped. Breathed. The  rabbit was thirty steps ahead, under a juniper root. Still. Ears flat. It knew I was there — or it knew something was there. 

I waited.

The rabbit waited.

The memory came uninvited. The river. The first fish. Small, barely a meal. Her hands — my hands, the same hands, smaller then — holding a thrashing body against stone. The sound it made. The way the thrashing stopped. The taste of river and blood. The girl who had eaten it raw because cooking required fire and fire required knowledge she hadn't yet acquired.

That girl had killed without thought. Without guilt. The fish was food. The rabbit was food. The marmot was food. The mountain provided and she took. Taking was as natural as breathing, as uncomplicated as thirst.

I moved forward. Slowly. The rabbit's ears twitched. Its body tensed — the coiled potential of a prey animal calculating the distance to the nearest exit. But the exits were mapped. I knew where it would run before it decided to run. The warm showed me the trajectory like a line drawn in the air.

It ran.

I moved. Not fast — not the cultivator's speed, not the power that made physics optional. Just the body's competence. The knowledge of angles and ground and the mechanical advantage of a being that had been catching things for longer than this species had existed. My hand closed around warm fur. The rabbit thrashed. Its heart was a machine, hammering against my palm, the biological panic of a creature that understood what had happened before the understanding could form in any brain it possessed.

I held it.

The memory again. Stronger. The fish on the stone. The sound. The girl's face — my face, young, thin, expressionless. She hadn't hesitated. She'd struck the head against rock, ate and moved on. Moving-on had been the simplest thing in the world because survival was simple. Kill. Eat. Walk. Sleep. Repeat.

When had it stopped being simple?

The rabbit thrashed. Its claws scratched at my wrist — shallow, meaningless, the futile resistance of a body fighting a conclusion that was already written. I could feel its heartbeat through the fur. Fast. Faster than fast. The metabolic overdrive of an animal that was spending its last calories on the hope of escape.

I broke its neck. Quick. Clean. The way the hunters had done it. The way I had done it a thousand times in the centuries when hunting was routine, when the kill was breakfast, when the warm was a tool and the tool's purpose was survival and survival's mechanics were clear.

The body went limp. The heartbeat stopped. The warmth stayed — briefly, the residual heat of a life that had been here a moment ago and was now not.

I held a dead rabbit.

Three days ago I had killed a forest. Accidentally. Emotionally. A hundred trees, a bird and three eggs, erased by grief I hadn't chosen to feel. That had been unbearable. The guilt had been a circle with no exit.

This was different.

This was a rabbit I had chosen to kill. Deliberately. With my hands. For a reason. You needed to eat. The reason was sufficient. The reason was the oldest reason that any living thing had ever killed another living thing.

I carried it back down the trail. The weight was negligible — a kilogram, maybe less. The fur was soft against my forearm. The eyes were half-closed, the lids settling into the expression that dead things wore, the relaxation that followed the final tension.

You were writing when I returned. You looked up. Saw the rabbit. Nodded.

"Thank you," you said.

Two words. Normal. The exchange of a man receiving food from a woman who had procured it. No ceremony. No significance beyond the practical. A rabbit, acquired, about to be prepared, about to become calories in a body that needed them.

I cleaned it myself. My hands remembered — not elegant, not gentle, but adequate. The knife moved. The fur separated from the flesh. The interior became exterior. The process was ancient, universal, the same sequence of actions that every human who had ever eaten meat had performed or witnessed or benefited from.

The smell was iron and grass. The same smell as the river. As the fish. As every kill that had ever fed a hungry body on a mountain where nothing grew fast enough to sustain a life that moved.

Xiǎohú appeared. Sat near the work. Her amber eyes tracked my hands with the focused attention of an animal that understood exactly what was happening and approved.

I had killed something. Deliberately. The kill had been contained, intentional, bound by the act itself and nothing else.

The fire was catching. The meat would cook. The evening would come.
