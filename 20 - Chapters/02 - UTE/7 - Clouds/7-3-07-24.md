You tilted the rice sack over the pot. Nothing came out. After a moment you folded the empty cloth, tucked it into your pack, and looked at me.

"We need food."

We. You still included me in the work of staying alive. I had no need of the rice; going without it would leave my body unchanged. Sometimes I wanted food, remembering its warmth in my hands or the taste of a meal. You had to eat whether you wanted to or not.

Xiǎohú could fend for herself. You would need something more substantial than the greens along the trail. I looked toward the undergrowth where an animal might shelter.

"I'll go hunting," I said.

You studied me. After so many days of leaving meals to you, I had only just begun helping with the pot. I waited until you accepted that I meant it.

"Rabbit would be good," you said. "If you can find one."

I could find one. Qi revealed living things around us, the warm I had sensed long before I knew its name. Two rabbits crouched in undergrowth sixty steps east. Below the trail, a family of marmots occupied a burrow. Smaller presences fluttered in the trees or stirred among the leaves.

I left with my hands empty. They would be enough for a rabbit.

The trail east was steep and quiet. Water seeped from a rock face into thick moss, ferns, and tangled undergrowth. I stopped to breathe. Thirty steps ahead, a rabbit crouched under a juniper root with its ears flat. It had gone still at my approach.

I waited.

The rabbit waited.

The rabbit's warmth brought back the first fish I had caught. I remembered my smaller hands holding its thrashing body against river stone. It had been barely a meal. The last slap against the rock came back, then the taste of river and blood. I had eaten it raw, before I knew how to make a cooking fire.

Food had seemed simple to that girl. She had caught what she could, eaten it, and continued until she needed more. I remembered the hunger that made a fish enough to fill all my attention.

I moved forward slowly. The rabbit's ears twitched; its body tightened under the root. Through the warm, its weight shifted toward one of the gaps it might run through.

It ran.

I stepped into its path. I had caught animals on ground like this for longer than I could count, learning where to place a foot before reaching. My hand closed around warm fur. The rabbit thrashed against my grip. Its heart hammered through its ribs into my palm.

I held it.

The fish returned more clearly. I remembered striking its head against the rock. My young face had been intent on the food before me. Once I had eaten, I had moved on. The next need would come soon enough: somewhere to walk, a place to sleep, and another meal when I woke.

When had it stopped being simple?

The rabbit thrashed again. Claws scratched shallow lines across my wrist. Its heartbeat raced through the fur while I adjusted my grip. Warmth pressed against my palm with each movement.

I broke its neck quickly, as hunters had taught me long ago. I had made that movement a thousand times when hunting was an ordinary part of the morning. The warm had guided me toward food; my hands had done the rest.

Its body went limp. The heartbeat stopped, though warmth lingered against my palm. I held it while that difference became clear.

I held a dead rabbit.

Three days ago I had killed a forest. A hundred trees, a bird, and three eggs had been destroyed while I thought of Wei. I had knelt beside the nest unable to account for what my feeling had done.

Here I could account for each movement. I had found a rabbit, caught it, and killed it because you needed food. Its weight sat in my hands. I could carry it back to you knowing why I had taken this life.

I carried it down the trail. It was a small rabbit, light enough to rest along one forearm, scarcely a burden. Its fur was soft against my skin. The eyes were half-closed now, the tension gone from the small body.

You were writing when I returned. You looked up at the rabbit, then nodded.

"Thank you," you said.

You said it as you would for a filled bowl. I had brought food back to camp, and once cooked it would give you the strength to walk farther tomorrow.

I cleaned it myself. My hands still knew the work, though I had done it more neatly when I hunted every day. The knife separated the skin from the flesh. I worked through the sequence, preparing what we could cook over the fire.

The smell was iron and grass. It brought back the river again, then other mornings when I had prepared a catch. Up here, plants grew too slowly to feed a traveler on their own.

Xiǎohú sat near my work. Her amber eyes followed the knife, then the meat beside it. She was still there when I finished.

I had chosen this kill. The ground around me was as it had been when I arrived. I could set the knife down, then turn toward the next part of the work.

The fire was catching. The meat would cook. The evening would come.
