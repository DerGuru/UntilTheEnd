The trail reached a ridgeline, following the high stone between two slopes. From there, forests spread below us on either side. Rivers narrowed to pale lines among them. I walked slowly, looking down at the places where trees gathered thickest.

"Tell me about the silence," you said.

You had waited days to ask. Even now you watched the trail, matching my pace rather than stopping me. You had chosen the moment carefully.

I knew which silence you meant.

The silence after Wei died. I remembered his weight in my arms while cold air crossed the ridge. He had come so close to me that remembering those last moments still changed how I held my hands.

Everything around us had stopped.

The birds had stopped singing.

The wind had stopped blowing.

Leaves had fallen together from every tree in sight. I had never measured how far that silence reached. I had been holding a dead boy while it happened, watching the air fill with falling leaves.

"I thought the world was grieving with me," I said.

You walked beside me without answering. Your pen stayed in your hand, unused. Your steps kept pace with mine while I tried to continue.

"The birds. The wind. The leaves. Everything stopped, and I thought... it felt as if the world understood. That it was honoring what I'd lost. That the silence was respite. That it was appropriate."

The last word hung in the mountain air. At the time, I had accepted that silence as something given to me. I kept my eyes on the trail.

"It wasn't the world," I said.

"No."

"It was me."

"It was."

Saying it slowed me. I had followed the possibility through the fog, the trees, and the grass beside our fire. Now I had named the same thing in the moment I least wanted to revisit.

"When Wei died, I killed the world around me."

I remembered the missing birdsong, the wind falling still, and the leaves severed from their branches. Even the insects had gone quiet. My grief had reached past the body I held, changing things at a distance I could no longer recover. I had mistaken the stillness for understanding.

"You killed the world around you," you said.

You repeated my words quietly. Hearing them in your voice left me with little room to retreat from what I had said.

"Not intentionally," I said.

"No," you agreed. "Not intentionally."

You waited a few steps before continuing.

"But you did."

We walked in the quiet of the ridge. Air moved over the stone. I listened to it without trying to speak.

Clouds moved above us. Their edges shifted, and I tried again to tell weather from something I might be doing. I found no reassuring boundary between the two.

The nest came back to me: three broken eggs, the bird beside them. Only a few days had passed, but I was already having trouble placing them. I remembered that small circle as clearly as I remembered the leaves falling around Wei.

In both places, my feeling had reached things outside me. I had stopped the birdsong around the boy. I had stilled the wind. All that silence had come from me while I held him, affecting lives I had scarcely noticed.

"How many times?" I asked. "How often have I done it without knowing?"

"There's no way to answer that."

"Try."

"Every strong emotion you've ever felt," you said. "Every moment of grief, anger, joy, and terror. For millennia. Without control. Without awareness. Every time you felt something strongly enough, the world around you changed. Trees grew or died. Weather shifted. Animals fled or didn't flee in time."

You paused.

"That's not an answer," you said. "But it's a framework."

I looked down at the forests. You had given me no number, but the extent was plain. I had crossed so much country, carrying each loss into another place, and trusted the weather around me to belong to the mountains.

From this height, whole stretches of woodland lay open at once. Somewhere in that green were nests too small to pick out from the ridge. I looked straight at them without knowing what was there.

I sat down on the ridgeline. Sun had warmed the stone under me. I pressed my palm against it and attended to the roughness. That much was here, within reach, while the distance below was hard to look at.

Xiǎohú sat next to me, her amber eyes on the valley. Her flank rested against my leg. I let her.

You sat on my other side with the notebook open. You held the pen, looked at the valley, and waited.

The clouds drifted west, white against blue. Whether the wind had carried them there or I had changed their course, I still could not tell.
