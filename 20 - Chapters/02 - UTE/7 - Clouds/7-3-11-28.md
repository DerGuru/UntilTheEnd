I ate. For the first time since — I didn't count. Counting required acknowledging the duration and the duration was the kind of number that made the fact worse. Days. Enough days that the body should have protested, should have collapsed, should have done the things that bodies did when denied sustenance. My body didn't do those things. My body persisted because persistence was what it did, the biological stubbornness of a body that refused to degrade regardless of input.

One bite.

The not-rice was burned. Subtly — the bottom-of-the-pot burn that your cooking consistently produced, the tonal shift from white to amber that indicated a man who understood the procedure but misjudged the timing, who knew the proportions but fumbled the patience, who had mastered the chemistry and missed the art.

It tasted almost like burned rice.

The texture was the chewiness that overcooking produced when combined with under-stirring, the grains sticking to each other, to the pot and to the memory of what it was supposed to be. The flavor was smoke, starch and the aftertaste that distinguished edible from optimal, the culinary territory between hunger and satisfaction where most of your cooking resided.

I chewed.

The muscular activity of a jaw. The teeth grinding, the tongue positioning, the salivary response that the body produced regardless of the mind's participation. The body ate because the body was offered food. The mind watched the process from a distance, the internal observation of a self watching itself perform a function it hadn't performed in days.

You said nothing.

This was important. The silence. The nothing-silence. The absence of comment. The particular you-communication-mode that treated an event as being exactly as significant as the event was.

A woman eating rice. Plain and unstaged. The kind of event that happened everywhere, all the time, to everyone who was alive, hungry and in proximity to food.

Xiǎohú was eating too. Not the not-rice — something she had caught while we weren't watching, something small, furred and now sticking partly out of her mouth. Her tawny gaze blinked at me from beside the fire. Organisms ingesting. Standard procedure.

I took a second bite. The burned flavor was consistent. The texture was consistent. Everything about your rice was consistent. Reliably mediocre, dependably edible, the culinary middle that represented adequacy rather than ambition.

The fire was almost out. The darkness had settled — the mountain shadows merged into one continuous dark, the sky above the ridgeline holding the last grey before full night. The clouds were moving west. Were they mine? Were they weather? The question had become a background process, the persistent uncertainty surfacing every time I observed them now.

I didn't know. I decided not to know. The decision was temporary, insufficient but enough.

The bowl was not empty. Three more bites, maybe four, remaining. The partial consumption that indicated a beginning rather than completion. 

I set the bowl down again.

The guilt was there. Still. The circle was there. Still. The eggs, the dead bird, Wei's silence and the thousand faces — all of it, still present, still orbiting, still the mental content that occupied the space between what I was and what I'd done.

But I'd eaten the not-rice.

And the not-rice had been burned.

And the burn had been natural — a man's imperfect cooking. Ordinary. The fire had been fire. The pot had been pot. The heat had been fire doing what fire does. The not-rice had burned because you weren't careful and your relationship with culinary art was respectful but not intimate.

Normal. The word was foreign. The taste of the word in the mind was strange, the unfamiliarity of a concept that had been absent so long it required reintroduction. Normal. A woman eating burned not-rice cooked by a man she was traveling with. Normal.

Wind came up and rekindled the fire. The embers glowed again. The mountain continued its patient existence around us, the mineral stubbornness of a landmass that had been here before anything alive and would be here after.

You cleaned the pot. The post-meal procedure: scrubbing with grass and water, the practical domesticity that treated the pot as an object rather than a vessel of meaning. You packed it. You stirred the embers. You unrolled your sleeping mat.

"Tomorrow," you said.

I lay down. The ground was hard and the stars were appearing behind the clouds — behind the clouds that might have been mine, behind the clouds that I was choosing not to investigate.

The bowl beside me. Half-eaten. Started.

I closed my eyes.

For the first time in days, nothing growing. Nothing dying. The grass around the campsite maintaining its usual relationship with night.

Quiet. Real quiet. Mountain quiet.

I fell asleep thinking about the not-rice.

You burned the not-rice every time and the consistency was almost admirable. I ate it anyway. That was the point, wasn't it.
