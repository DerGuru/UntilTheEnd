Yun stands in the overgrown space behind the house and looks at what needs doing. The weeds are thick — the aggressive vegetation of neglected soil, plants that have filled every available centimeter in the absence of human intervention. Stones. Debris. The remains of whatever the previous tenant grew, long since returned to the undifferentiated organic matter that all cultivation becomes without maintenance.

She borrows a shovel from the landlord and starts to dig. The shovel is iron, wood and rust in the places where iron meets moisture without intervention. The handle is smooth from use. The earth is cool. Damp. When she lifts it, the underside is darker — the color that distinguishes surface from depth.

She makes rows. Approximate. The imprecise geometry of a gardener who knows the principle but not the practice, who understands that seeds require spacing but is uncertain about the optimal distance. The rows are — adequate.

She plants some seeds from the market. She bought them two days ago — a small paper packet, the folded paper that contains possibilities she cannot see. She does not know exactly what will grow. She could know — the reality-sensing capacity that her power provides would identify the seed type, predict the growth pattern and forecast the harvest with precision that no farmer could match. And she could have it in a blink.

She doesn't.

She places the seeds in the rows. One by one. The manual labor of planting — fingers in soil, seed in hole, soil over seed. The gesture is old. A human action that predates civilization. That's all there is to it. The seed is in the ground. The rest is waiting. The sufficiency is — the whole point.

Planting. Waiting. Letting.

The parallel is obvious. She lets it lie because dwelling is a form of pushing and pushing is the thing she has set aside.

"... Water?"

A voice brings her out of her reverie. The fence between her garden and the neighbor's is low — three boards, decorative rather than functional, the architectural gesture that says "this is mine" without adding "and you cannot enter." A man stands at the fence. Thin. The angular thinness of a body that works more than it eats, the caloric deficit recorded in the angles of jaw, shoulder and wrist.

"My well runs slow this time of year," he says. He gestures at his own plot — smaller than hers, more maintained, but dry. The soil is pale. The plants are — trying. Vegetation that wants to live but lacks the water to thrive.

She nods. "Take what you need."

He does. Her well — the shared well, technically, the communal water source that serves this end of the village — produces enough. He fills a bucket. Carries it to his plot and pours. The water darkens the pale soil immediately, the way that water darkens earth.

"Thank you," he says.

The exchange is — small. Human-scaled. It requires no power, no warping, no supernatural capacity of any kind. A woman with water shares with a man without water. The simplest exchange that human community facilitates.

She kneels in the dirt. Her knees are damp. The earth is under her nails. The seeds are in the ground. The sun is overhead, the clouds present, and the village noises — children, roosters, the thin man's bucket clanking — are the acoustic evidence of a world operating without her intervention.

She lets the seeds be. It'll grow at its own pace. She lets the garden produce what it will.

She waits. Her active choice to step back, restraint that is harder than action because action has momentum and restraint has only intention. She waits with confidence that things will happen when things happen and the timing is not hers to determine.

Xiǎohú watches from the doorstep. Eyes on the garden. On the woman in the dirt. On the human comedy of someone who can reshape reality choosing instead to use a shovel.

She washes her hands at the well. The water is cold. The earth dissolves from her nails slowly, reluctantly.

She dries her hands on her clothes.

The seeds are in the ground.

Enough for today.
