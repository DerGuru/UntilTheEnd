By evening, you had cooked another pot of rice. That morning you had pressed clay into the crack with a flat stone; the repair had held through cooking. We sat in the fire's warmth with our bowls after another day of failed attempts.

"Stop forcing it," you said.

"What else am I supposed to do?" I said.

"You're fighting yourself."

"Against whom else should I fight?"

"That is exactly the problem."

Five words, spoken evenly. I waited for you to add something. You took another mouthful instead, leaving me with what you had already said.

The fire shifted. A log settled, sending sparks upward. They lasted a heartbeat, then fell as dark dust. The speed of their fading held my attention longer than the sparks themselves had lasted.

"Explain," I said.

"You're fighting yourself. The enemy is you. The battlefield is you. The weapon is you. When you win, you lose. When you lose, you win. The combat is the problem, not the opponent."

You ate between sentences, lifting your chopsticks whenever you paused. You had managed to keep thinking without sacrificing your dinner. I considered the difficulty of what you were proposing.

"You're saying I should stop."

"I'm saying the *fighting* is what's preventing the change. You tried to believe yourself mortal. Your body responded by cracking stones and bending trees. The *trying* was a form of conviction. Conviction changes reality. Yours was: *I am doing battle with my own immortality.* And what did reality become? A battle."

Air passed between the logs, feeding flames along their blackened edges. With dry wood it would burn higher, and once the wood was gone it would dwindle to embers. We had warmed ourselves at it each evening, relying on the same process.

"You're telling me to be a fire," I said.

"I'm telling you to stop being a siege."

The image was absurd enough to make me look up. I had been laying siege to a certainty I had built myself. Each assault began with the conviction that something in me had to be overcome. If you were right, the harder I pressed, the more there was to press against.

"You fight against yourself," you repeated. "And the fighting is itself an act of conviction. The conviction says: there is something to fight. There is an enemy. There is resistance. Reality responds to that. It gives you the enemy, the resistance, and the wall. You're building what you're trying to break."

"That's—"

"Absurd? Yes. So is everything about you."

You almost smiled. It lasted an instant before your face settled again. Even now, with so little going well, you could find something ridiculous in me.

"Then what?" I said.

You set down your chopsticks and rested your hands next to the bowl. Flames moved between us, casting wavering shadows over your face. I waited until you had finished swallowing.

"Stop fighting. Stop trying. Stop directing conviction at the problem like a weapon. If the fighting is the problem, then the solution is the absence of fighting. Not surrender. The absence of combat. Not losing. The absence of the game."

"That's the same thing I learned at the river. Letting go."

"Is it?"

"Isn't it?"

"At the river you learned that letting go exists. That it's different from willing. That it's the open hand, not the fist. But you haven't *done* it. You've understood it. Understanding is not doing."

You were right. I sat with the bowl against my palms and the fire on my knees. Overhead, thick clouds hid the stars. I had understood the difference at the river, and here I was asking you to explain it again.

"Understanding is not doing," I repeated.

"You understand perfectly how to let go. But for some reason, you cannot do it. Because doing is itself a form of will. Will is like a grip, which is what you're trying to release. The paradox is the answer."

"The paradox is the problem."

"Same thing."

We looked at each other across the fire. You could describe what happened, but you could not do it for me. I could change so much, yet I could not reach myself. The two of us had arrived at another answer we could hardly use. At least we had warm rice. Xiǎohú slept at the edge of the dark, and clouds passed overhead.

"Tomorrow," I said.

"Tomorrow you won't force it."

"I'll try."

"Trying is forcing."

"Then I won't try."

"Better."

You picked up your chopsticks and went back to eating. The flames settled along the log while you finished the bowl.

You were right. Understanding is not doing. It was the truest thing anyone has ever told me with their mouth full.

For once, I left the question where it was and sat next to you.
