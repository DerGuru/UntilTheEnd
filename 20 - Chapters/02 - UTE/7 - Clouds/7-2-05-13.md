We walked with three leaves floating at my shoulder. I held them there along the trail, sometimes adding a fourth. The certainty was getting easier to reach. My hands hung at my sides while I held the simple fact that the leaves floated.

They were brown-gold, still carrying autumn's colors although the season was spring. When I let the certainty lapse, a leaf fell and touched the trail without breaking my stride. Overhead, clouds moved west in the direction we were walking. Their shadows crossed the path ahead.

"So you've spent your entire life believing you can't die," you said.

You walked next to me now. Since the ridge, the three steps you used to leave between us were gone. I heard you without turning my head. We had returned to this question many times, examining another detail each time we walked. How could I change something I knew as certainly as I knew the ground under my feet?

"Almost. As far as I can remember, but there are still gaps. Especially around the time when I was in that cave and before."

"And that belief, that certainty, is what makes it true."

"Yes."

"Then why not believe something else?"

I let the leaves fall and looked at you. Your stride did not change. I knew that attentive expression: you had an argument to offer, but you were waiting to see where I would object.

"That's not how it works."

"Why not?"

I walked four steps without answering, then a fifth. A bird called closer than any had in days.

"The birds are coming back," you said, looking up. You noted it as readily as a change in the trail, then turned back to me.

"Why not?" I repeated.

"Let's look at it this way. You can believe a leaf floats. You can believe water rises. You can believe a flower blooms. Each contradicts how the world normally works, yet you do them. Easily, now. Playfully. So why can't you believe one more thing? One more contradiction? I know you aren't supposed to fight it. And I mean that too. Don't fight the existing belief. Believe something else. Just add a new one, as you did with the leaves."

Your suggestion sounded as simple as lifting another leaf. The difference was there, but finding words for it was harder. It reached deeper than any argument I could make.

"Belief isn't chosen," I said.

You waited.

"The leaves float because I know they float. Not because I decide they should. Not because I want them to. I know it as I know that stones fall. The knowing comes before any decision."

"And?"

"And I know I'm immortal. The same way. The same depth. The same certainty. Telling myself I'm mortal is like telling myself things fall upward. I can know it for a leaf. The leaf is outside me, a separate thing. But what holds this body together comes from certainty I have lived with for many millennia."

You considered as we walked across a plateau. The ground was level here, giving us a stretch of easier walking before the next incline.

"You can't decide to believe," you said.

"No. Belief arises. Like a feeling. You can't decide to feel love. You can't decide to feel fear. You can feel them, or not feel them, but the feeling isn't a choice. The belief is the same. It exists or it doesn't. That certainty exists in my flesh, in my qi, and in everything that holds me together. I can't remove it by deciding to, or by adding a smaller belief on top."

"Then what?"

"I need to stop holding to it. Not un-believe, just stop holding. The way you stop gripping. Not choosing to release, just letting go of the choice to hold."

You were quiet for a dozen steps.

"Try something smaller first," you said.

"Smaller?"

"Not 'I am mortal.' That's the whole building. Try a brick. One brick. One small, exact belief. Not the whole structure. A corner of it."

That gave me something I could examine. Most people dismantling a wall would have to work at the mortar around a brick. I could simply declare the wall absent, which had done little to prepare me for a problem requiring patience. I looked down at one finger.

"My finger could bleed," I said.

A possibility. I let the thought be that small. Blood might rise through the skin of this finger. I held the finger out before me, attending to that patch of flesh rather than everything that kept me alive.

My qi shifted. A brief hesitation ran under the skin, in the flow I usually noticed only when I directed it. The possibility held for a moment without the immediate tightening I had come to expect.

One heartbeat. Two.

Then the flow steadied. The certainty returned through the finger, as firm as before. I searched the skin for the brief looseness I had felt.

It had lasted scarcely a heartbeat. Still, something in me had yielded. I lowered my hand slowly.

"Something happened," you said.

"A hesitation."

"From the conviction?"

"From me."

The bird called again, closer this time. Above us, the clouds had begun drifting east while we continued west. A thin, bright seam of blue opened between two formations. It lasted three breaths before the gray closed over it.
