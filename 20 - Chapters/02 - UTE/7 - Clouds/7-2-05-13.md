Walking. Training in motion. The leaves floated.

Not all of them. Three, four at a time, plucked from my attention rather than from the trees. I didn't reach for them. Didn't focus. The belief was becoming easier to access, something beneath will and intention both. The knowing. The leaves float. Not because I say so. Because they do. Because in this arrangement of reality, leaves float and the arrangement is the true one.

Three leaves, hovering at shoulder height, drifting beside me like small, patient escorts. Brown-gold, autumn-touched though the season was spring. They floated because the floating was real and when I stopped knowing, they fell and the falling was also real. Overhead, clouds moved in the same direction we walked — west, slowly, their shadows crossing the trail ahead of us like advance scouts.



"So you've spent your entire life believing you can't die," you said.

You walked beside me now. The three-step distance had closed permanently since the ridge, replaced by the side-by-side arrangement of equals, or of two people who had stopped pretending that proximity was a variable. We talked about this many times. Every time battering another detail of the same problem, the same question. How do you change a belief that is not just faith but a certainty? 

"Almost. As far as I can remember, but there are still gaps in the memory. Especially, around the time when I was in that cave and before."

"And that belief — that certainty — is what makes it true."

"Yes."

"Then why not believe something else."

I let the leaves fall. Looked at you. You were walking with the steady stride of a man making a point, your face set in the arrangement that meant you were deploying logic and expected the logic to be received as provisional.

"That's not how it works."

"Why not?"

Silence. Four steps. Five. A bird called — closer than they'd been in days.

"The birds are coming back," you said, looking up. The way you noted weather or trail conditions — observation filed, conversation continued.

"Why not?" I repeated.

"Lets look at it this way. You can believe a leaf floats. You can believe water rises. You can believe a flower blooms. Each of those is a contradiction of how the world normally works and you do them. Easily, now. Playfully. So why can't you believe one more thing? One more contradiction? I know, you aren't supposed to fight it. And I mean that too. Don't fight the existing belief. Belief something else. Don't try to un-believe the old one. Just add a new one. The way you added the leaf-floating belief."

The logic was seductive. Elegant. Wrong. But wrong in a way that was difficult to articulate because the wrongness lived in the place where logic couldn't reach. The substrate, the marrow, the bone-deep architecture that preceded rational analysis.

"Belief isn't chosen," I said.

You waited.

"The leaves float because I know they float. Not because I decide they should. Not because I want them to. I know it the way I know gravity — not as theory but as fact. The knowing precedes the decision. The knowing is the decision."

"And?"

"And I know I'm immortal. The same way. The same depth. The same certainty. Telling myself I'm mortal is like telling myself gravity goes up. I can know it for a leaf. The leaf is outside me, the leaf is a separate thing, the leaf's physics are not my physics. But my physics — the laws that govern this body, this existence — those laws are written in the certainty that has been accumulating for many many millennia."

You considered. Walking. The trail was gentler here — a plateau between ascents, the mountain providing a brief horizontal stretch before the next incline.

"You can't decide to believe," you said.

"No. Belief arises. Like a feeling. You can't decide to feel love. You can't decide to feel fear. You can feel them, or not feel them, but the feeling isn't a choice. The belief is the same. It exists or it doesn't. And the belief that I am immortal exists — in every cell, in every particle of qi, in the structure of whatever I am. I can't un-believe it by deciding to un-believe it or adding another smaller belief on top."

"Then what?"

"I need to stop holding to it. Not un-believe, just stop holding. The way you stop gripping. Not choosing to release, just letting go of the choice to hold."

You were quiet for a dozen steps.

"Try something smaller first," you said.

"Smaller?"

"Not 'I am mortal.' That's — the whole building. Try a brick. One brick. One small, exact belief. Not the whole structure. A corner of it."

You were right. The logic of dismantlement rather than demolition. You don't bring down a building by declaring it doesn't exist. Well, I could, but that's different. Someone else would need to find a brick, find the mortar around the brick and work the mortar.

"My finger could bleed," I said.

Not my finger will bleed. Not my finger must bleed. Could. The smallest possible opening. The conditional, the hypothetical, the suggestion that the laws governing this body might, under certain circumstances, permit the ordinary function of vulnerability.

My qi — shifted. A momentary hesitation in the grid of certainty that kept my body immortal, the localized flicker of something adjusting to accommodate a new possibility. Like a wall developing a hairline crack, not a structural failure but the acknowledgment that the structure was not, perhaps, absolute.

One heartbeat. Two.

The crack sealed. The qi stabilized. The conviction re-established itself with the efficient speed of a structure that had been maintaining its integrity for millennia and had developed robust protocols for handling anomalies.

But it had been there. The hesitation. The flicker. The opening that lasted a heartbeat and proved that the wall was not welded shut.

"Something happened," you said.

"A hesitation."

"From the conviction?"

"From me."

The bird called again. Closer this time. Above us, the clouds had drifted east while we walked west and a seam of blue opened between two formations, thin and bright, a crack in the overcast that lasted three breaths before the grey closed over it again.
