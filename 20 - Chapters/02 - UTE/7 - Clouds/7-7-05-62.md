Summer.

The heat arrives without negotiation — sudden, complete, the temperature of a season that imposes its conditions and expects compliance. She sweats. Weeding the garden, her body producing moisture to cool itself the way bodies have always done, except hers never needed to. The sweat runs down her temples. She wipes it with her forearm and the wiping is so ordinary, so perfectly unremarkable, that she stands there for a moment holding the hoe and marveling at the fact that she is a person who sweats.

The garden produces things. Small things — green shoots that become plants that become food that becomes meals that become the slow, fumbling infrastructure of a woman learning to feed herself through effort rather than existence. The lettuce is acceptable. The beans are adequate. The tomatoes she planted too close together, the error of a first-time gardener, crowd each other like siblings in a too-small bed, competing for sunlight and soil and producing fruit that is small and hard.

She eats them anyway. They are hers.

The market is twice a week — on the second and fifth day. She goes both days. Loudcumber — the vegetable seller whose volume exceeds his stall, his street, and possibly his province — had begun recognizing her. The recognition is not subtle.

"THE NEW ONE," he announces when she approaches, although she is a regular now for a few months. The announcement carries across three stalls and reaches customers who did not ask to be informed. "What do you want to get today?"

"Everything," she says.

He appears to find this acceptable. He hands her vegetables with an aggressive care, explaining at a volume designed for an audience of two hundred how to select, how to store, how to prepare. He is wrong about some things. He is loudly wrong. But his tomatoes are good and his heart is in approximately the right place, which she supposes is all one can ask of a man who sells vegetables like he's defending a city wall.

At the next stall, two women are talking. The neighbor — the one who brings jars and opinions and invitations to eat with equal frequency — is telling a story. Her hands sculpt the air into the shapes the story requires, the physical grammar of a woman who narrates with her whole body.

"She came down from the sky. Light everywhere. The whole harvest was dying and she said one word — just one — and the fields turned green again. Then she disappeared. Into light. Gone."

The younger woman nods. The nod of someone who enjoys a good story and sees no reason to interrogate it.

She walked, Yun thinks. On her feet. She showed the farmer where the irrigation channel was blocked. He cleared it himself. The fields recovered on their own.

The neighbor continues. The woman from the sky saves another village, another harvest, another generation that would have starved without the intervention of light and a single word. The story grows with each telling, the way stories do — taller, brighter, further from the ground.

Yun picks up her vegetables. Something in her chest is quiet. The stories were never hers. They belonged to the people who told them, who shaped them, who needed the sky and the light and the single word more than they needed the truth. The sky version always wins. She has known this for a thousand tellings.

She lets it go. The way she is learning to let things go — not by pushing them away, but by stopping the push.

She carries the vegetables home and cooks. The cooking is improving. The incremental trajectory of a skill being learned through repetition and failure.

Autumn.

The cooling comes the way the heat came — without asking. One morning the air has an edge to it, a thin sharpness that she feels against her arms when she steps outside, and she stands in the doorway and realizes that the season has changed while she was learning to cook.

There is hunger. The particular hunger that follows physical work in cooling air — sharper than summer hunger, more insistent, the body's way of announcing that the season is changing and the reserves need attention.

Her stomach growls. Still startling, after months. The body's commentary on its own condition — unsolicited, unapologetic, loud in the quiet kitchen.

She eats, while standing. An apple from the market, from Loudcumber, who selected it for her with the same intensity he applies to all customer interactions. The apple is crisp. Sour-sweet. The juice runs along her chin and she wipes it with the back of her hand.

The hunger recedes. Satisfied. The quiet contentment that follows eating when eating is real, when the body got what it needed and the distance between those two things has been closed.

Xiǎohú appears at the kitchen door. Her gaze fixed on the apple core with the calculated interest of an animal assessing whether a thing qualifies as food.

"No," Yun says.

The bright eyes disagree but accept.

Autumn continues. The garden gives its last. She eats. She sleeps every night, the deep recuperative unconsciousness that her body requires because she is a body now and bodies have requirements. She is learning the rhythm of requirements — the feeding and the resting and the maintenance that living demands, the thousand small negotiations between a body and the world it inhabits.

The seasons accumulate. The days stack. Time flows through her and each second departs and does not return, and the departing is not loss.

It is counting.
