Summer heat reaches the garden while she weeds. Sweat runs down her temples. She wipes it with her forearm, then stands holding the hoe. Her body cools itself as other bodies do. The gesture is so ordinary that she has to look at it twice before accepting that it belongs to her.

The garden produces small things she can carry into the kitchen. Shoots become plants; some become food she is learning to prepare. A meal begins in work she did days before, with soil under her nails. The lettuce is acceptable. The beans are adequate. She planted the tomatoes too close together, forgetting how much room they would need. Their stems crowd the bed like siblings given too little space. They compete for sunlight and yield fruit that is small and hard.

She eats them anyway. They are hers.

The market meets on the second and fifth days of each seven-day round. She goes both times. Loudcumber has begun recognizing her. His voice carries beyond his stall, his street, and possibly his province. Recognition receives the same treatment as everything else.

"THE NEW ONE," he announces when she approaches, although she has been a regular for months. Customers three stalls away receive information they did not ask for. "What do you want to get today?"

"Everything," she says.

He finds this acceptable. He hands over vegetables with aggressive care, explaining to an audience of two hundred how to select, store, and prepare them. He is wrong about some things. He is loudly wrong. But his tomatoes are good and his heart is approximately in the right place. That may be all she can ask of a man who sells vegetables as though defending a city wall.

Two women talk at the next stall. The neighbor is telling a story, using her hands to give each part its shape. She brings jars, opinions, and invitations to eat with equal frequency. Her stories receive the same generous attention.

"She came down from the sky. Light everywhere. The whole harvest was dying. She said one word, just one, and the fields turned green again. Then she disappeared. Into light. Gone."

The younger woman nods, following each gesture with evident enjoyment.

She walked, Yun thinks. On her feet. She showed the farmer where the irrigation channel was blocked. He cleared it himself. The fields recovered on their own.

The neighbor continues. Her woman from the sky saves another village, another harvest, and another generation that would otherwise have starved. Each rescue comes with more light. Yun listens to the story climb farther from the ground where she remembers walking.

Yun picks up her vegetables. Something in her chest is quiet. The stories belong to the people telling them, shaped by what those people need. The sky, the light, and the single word will outlast the blocked irrigation channel. She has heard enough versions to know which one wins.

She lets the neighbor continue without correcting her. The effort of holding the story to its facts can end here.

She carries the vegetables home to cook. The food improves with each failed attempt she remembers well enough to avoid. The change is so gradual that she notices it only when a meal repeats an old mistake less completely. There is room to become competent without making competence another revelation.

Autumn.

One morning the air has a thin sharpness against her arms. She stops in the doorway, feeling the change before going out. Autumn has arrived while she was learning to cook.

Hunger follows work in the cooling air. It is sharper than the summer's hunger, insistent enough to bring her back into the kitchen before she has finished everything outside.

Her stomach growls loudly in the quiet kitchen. After months, the sound can still startle her.

She eats standing. An apple from the market, from Loudcumber, who selected it for her with the same intensity he applies to all customer interactions. The apple is crisp. Sour-sweet. The juice runs along her chin and she wipes it with the back of her hand.

The hunger recedes as she eats. A small satisfaction settles in, the satisfaction of giving her body something it required. She lingers at the table with it.

Xiǎohú appears at the kitchen door, her eyes fixed on the apple core.

"No," Yun says.

The fox watches it a moment longer before turning away.

Autumn continues through the last of the garden's produce. She eats what grows. Each night she sleeps deeply enough to recover from the day's work. She is learning that rhythm: food, rest, and the small maintenance by which a person continues. The needs come regularly enough for her to begin anticipating them. A day spent working changes the evening's hunger. A night's rest changes the next morning. One part of living supplies the next.

Days gather into seasons. Each heartbeat passes through her without returning. She feels the departure without calling it loss.

It is counting.
