She packs.

There is not much. The minimal possessions of a woman who has lived in a village for years without accumulating — a few clothes, a bowl, a cup, the chipped ceramic that has become hers through the quiet alchemy of daily use. The chip happened in the autumn, when she set the cup down too hard after the neighbor said something about her garden that was probably true and definitely unwelcome. She likes the chip. It gives the cup a history.

She leaves the bowl. She leaves the cup. She places them on the table — clean, positioned with the deliberate care of objects being left behind for whoever comes next. The table looks like a place setting for a ghost. She adjusts the cup. Adjusts it again. Stops adjusting.

The neighbor is in the doorway before she reaches it. Of course she is. The neighbor has a sense for departures the way some people have a sense for rain — she knows before the sky does.

"I'm going," she says.

The neighbor's face does the thing — the rapid assessment, faster than speech, that takes in the packed bag, the empty shelves, the positioned bowl, and arrives at a conclusion before the mouth can form a question. Then the mouth forms one anyway.

"Where?"

"Home."

"You HAVE a home."

The neighbor gestures — at the house, the garden, the plum tree that is losing its leaves in the slow, dignified shedding that autumn requires. At the village beyond the fence, the rooftops and the smoke and the lane where the Counter walks and counts. At everything.

"A different one," she says.

The neighbor looks at her. The long look of a woman who has shared rice and wrinkle commentary and the particular intimacy of village neighbors who know more about each other than either has chosen to disclose — who know each other not through confession but through proximity, through the thousand small observations that accumulate when you live close enough to hear someone else's kettle whistle.

"Your rice is good now," the neighbor says.

"Thank you."

"Don't get lost."

It is as close to goodbye as the neighbor will come. Affection delivered as instruction. The imperative that says, underneath itself: *the getting-lost would matter. You would be missed.*

She leaves the house. The door closes behind her. The plum tree is there — bark grey, branches bare, the winter-approaching silhouette of a tree that will continue its cycle regardless of who lives in the house beside it. She looks at it for a moment. The tree does not look back. Trees never do. She likes that about trees.

The path out of the village leads uphill. She walks. Slowly — her body is older now, the joints stiffer, the knees negotiating with the incline the way old knees negotiate with all inclines: cautiously, with the deliberate placement of feet that indicates gravity is no longer being taken for granted.

"Will you come back?"

She stops. Turns.

The Counter. Standing on the path behind her. Taller now — eleven, twelve, the half-grown height of a child between childhood and whatever comes after. Her face is serious. The counting face. The face that has applied numerical analysis to wrinkles and lanterns and steps and now applies it to this.

"No," she says.

The word is honest. The Counter deserves honest words — the child who counts everything deserves to be counted upon for truth.

"But you'll remember me," the Counter says. Not a question.

"Your wrinkles," the Counter adds. As though specifying. As though the wrinkles are the important part, which perhaps they are — the counted things, the enumerated evidence of a life that the Counter witnessed and recorded in the only language she trusts. "I'll remember your wrinkles. There were too many."

She looks at the child. The child looks at her. The exchange lasts a breath. Maybe two. She collects it. This one. The last one from this village. The Counter on the path with the serious face and the numbers and the steady certainty that counting is enough.

"Everything," she says. "I'll remember everything."

The Counter nods. Satisfied. She turns and walks back toward the village, counting steps as she goes — the retreating sound of numbers, growing quieter, the census of a familiar world reclaiming its mathematician.

She watches her go. Then turns. Continues uphill.

The path is steep. Her knees protest. Xiǎohú walks beside her — lower to the ground now, or she is higher off it, the relative positioning shifted by years that changed one of them and left the other the same. The amber eyes are steady. Xiǎohú has been walking beside her since before the village, before the mountain, before the name. The walking is what she does.

She thinks of a notebook. Of a man who carried one — worn leather, stained pages, dots where characters should have been. A man who recorded everything because everything deserved recording, who walked away with the recording under his arm and did not look back.

She does not look back.

The village recedes below her. The path narrows. The mountain receives her with the indifference that mountains apply to all travelers — the geological patience of a thing that has been here longer than language and will be here after it. Come or go. The stone does not mind.

She climbs. Old knees. White hair. The visible effort of a woman who is mortal and aging and climbing anyway, because the climbing is what she came to do.

Above her, the clouds are dissolving. The afternoon light breaks through in columns of gold, the intermittent illumination that occurs when cloud cover thins and the world brightens, dims, brightens again with the rhythm of atmospheric change. The light falls on her white hair and her lined face and the path ahead.

She climbs toward the light.
