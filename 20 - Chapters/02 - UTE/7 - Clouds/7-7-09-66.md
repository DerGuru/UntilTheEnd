After the other left, she packs.

There is not much. The minimal possessions of a woman who has lived in a village for years without accumulating much — a few clothes, a bowl, a cup, a pot. 

She places them on the table — clean, positioned with the deliberate care of objects being left behind for whoever comes next. The table looks like a place setting for a ghost. She adjusts the cup. Adjusts it again. Stops adjusting.

The neighbor is in the doorway before she reaches it. Of course she is. The neighbor has something of a sixth sense for anything unusual, like departures. Like some people have a sense for rain — she knows before the sky does.

"I'm going," Yun says.

"Where?"

"Home."

"But you have a home."

The neighbor gestures — at the house, the garden, the plum tree that is already blank in the late autumn weather. At the village beyond the fence, the rooftops and the smoke and the lane where the Counter walks and counts. At everything.

"A different one."

The neighbor looks at her. The long look of a woman who has shared rice and the particular intimacy of village neighbors who know more about each other than either has chosen to disclose — who know each other not through confession but through proximity, through the thousand small observations that accumulate when you live close enough to hear someone else's kettle whistle.

"Don't get lost."

It is as close to goodbye as the neighbor will come. Affection delivered as instruction. 

She leaves the house. The door closes behind her. The plum tree is there — bark grey, branches bare, the winter-approaching silhouette of a tree that will continue its cycle regardless of who lives in the house beside it. She looks at it for a moment. The tree does not look back. Trees never do. She likes that about trees.

The path out of the village leads uphill. She walks. Slowly — her body is older now, the joints stiffer, the knees negotiating with the incline. Cautiously, with the deliberate placement of feet that indicates gravity is no longer being taken for granted.

"Will you come back?"

She stops. Turns.

The Counter. Standing on the path behind her. Her face is serious. The counting face. The face that has applied numerical analysis to wrinkles and lanterns and steps and now applies it to this.

"No," Yun replies honestly with warmth in her voice.

"But you'll remember me," the Counter says. Not a question.

She looks at the girl. The counter looks at her. The exchange lasts a breath. Maybe two. She collects it. This one. The last one from this village. The Counter on the path with the serious face and the steady certainty that counting is enough.

"Yes," Yun says. "I'll remember everything."

The Counter nods. Satisfied. She turns and walks back toward the village, counting steps as she goes — the retreating sound of numbers, growing quieter, the census of a familiar world reclaiming its mathematician.

Yun watches her go. Then turns. Continues uphill.

The path is steep. Her knees protest. Xiǎohú walks beside her. Her amber eyes are steady. Xiǎohú, who has been walking beside her since before the village. The walking is what she does.

She thinks of a notebook. Of a man who carried one — worn leather, stained pages, dots where characters should have been. A man who recorded everything because everything deserved recording, who walked away with the recording under his arm and did not look back.

She does not look back.

The village recedes below her. The path narrows. The mountain receives her with the indifference that mountains apply to all travelers. Come or go. The stone does not mind.

She climbs. Old knees. White hair. The visible effort of a woman who is mortal and aging and climbing anyway, because the climbing is what she came to do.

Above her, the clouds are dissolving. The afternoon light breaks through in columns of gold, the intermittent illumination that occurs when cloud cover thins and the world brightens, dims, brightens again in their rhythm. The light falls on her white hair, her lined face and the path ahead.

She climbs toward the light.
