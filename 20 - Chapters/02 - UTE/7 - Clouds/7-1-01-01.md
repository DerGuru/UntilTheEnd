We sat by the mountain trail, where the vegetation thinned and each breath brought cold air against my teeth. You opened your notebook across your knees. We had stopped here to rest, but you laid your pen across the page instead of putting it away.

"Start," you said.

"With what?" I said.

You kept your hands on the notebook. You had given me time to settle. Now you waited with the patience of a physician whose patient had postponed the examination long enough.

"Tell me who you are."

I let the question stand. Your fingers rested by the pen. I could have answered with a name, but you already knew the one I was using.

I looked at the sky. Thin clouds lay scattered above the ridge, their edges faint against the blue. They drifted slowly while I sat with my mouth closed, looking for somewhere to begin.

"I'm tired," I said.

It was the first answer I could give without arranging it for you. I was tired. I had been tired long before I understood what kept me alive, long before I could put words to the weight between my lungs and my spine. Even here, sitting still, each breath took work.

"I know," you said. "Tell me why."

You spoke quietly, with the insistence I knew by now. You could wait through a silence. You could also hear when I was using one to avoid you, which made you an inconvenient traveling companion.

I breathed.

The metallic taste was there, faint and persistent. It had lasted so many days that only its strength still registered, never its presence. My teeth vibrated with it, a low hum I could neither place nor quiet.

"I am mortal," I said.

The words crossed the pace of mountain air between us. They were easy to pronounce. I could even explain why they ought to be true. Your pen waited above the page while I searched my ribs for a change.

I found none.

I am mortal. My mouth produced the sentence and I understood every word, yet I might as well have been speaking about someone else. I let it settle, waited through a breath, and tried to feel it as a fact. It was a sentence I had said.

The other certainty went much deeper. I am immortal. My breathing continued without a catch. My qi moved through me in its usual course. I could question the belief with every thought I had, and the rest of me would carry on as it had for ages. My life rested on that certainty. Words reached only its surface.

You were watching. The pen hadn't moved. You had been waiting to record a change, and I had given you nothing.

"What did you feel?" you asked.

"Nothing."

"Nothing as in emptiness?"

"Nothing as in nothing happened. The words went in and came out the other side. My body doesn't believe them."

You considered. Your brows drew together. Your gaze dropped to my hands, then came back to my face. You wrote a short line, probably an uninspiring account of our first attempt.

"Again," you said.

"It won't—"

"Again."

I looked at the clouds. They had moved since I last looked, barely farther along the ridge. A thin edge dissolved. A change that small was visible from the ground; mine had defeated both of us at a distance of one pace. I lowered my eyes to my hands, still on my knees.

"I am mortal," I said again.

Nothing.

Xiǎohú lay nearby, amber eyes open. They found me, then closed in a yawn that showed all her teeth, and she settled her chin on her paws. At least one of us had made good use of the stop.

You wrote.

I sat.

The clouds moved.
