I sat. Eyes closed. The intentional closure that distinguished meditation from something else — the mind's decision to negotiate with itself without the distraction of the external.

The grass beneath me was real. The stone beneath the grass was real. The air entering my lungs was real. My lungs were real. The ribs around my lungs were real. The body containing all of it was real. Present, physical, taking up space.

I reached inward. The direction was different now. Outward was easy. This was inward. The interior navigation that had no physical correlate, the turning of attention from the observable to the experiential, from the world-as-object to the self-as-subject.

*I am mortal.*

The sentence was present in the mind the way all sentences were — as language, as structure, as the arrangement of sounds and meanings that the brain processed into comprehension. I understood it. The three words were clear and the clarity was complete.

*I am mortal.*

I believed it and pushed the belief inward — not as thought but as conviction, not as statement but as state, the pressure of a mind attempting to make a sentence true by force of wanting it to be true. And maybe that was the problem. Maybe the wanting was the problem. The desire to be mortal, the wish to be mortal — maybe that was the thing that made it impossible to be mortal.

Nothing.

The wall was refusal. The solid certainty of a barrier that existed precisely where it needed to exist to block precisely what I was trying to do. The wall was internal, self-generated, the configuration of self that had been in place for so long that the self and the wall were indistinguishable.

*I am mortal.*

/ɡɵ̆ŋ/. The old tongue surfaced — the sound of stone on stone, the oldest word for wall, the one that carried the weight of permanence and the solidity of a thing built to last. The wall was not resistance. Resistance implied an active process, a force opposing a force, the dynamic tension of conflict. This was not conflict. This was architecture. This was the structural reality of a self that had been built to survive and was now being asked to stop.

I opened my eyes.

"Nothing," I said.

You were beside me. Cross-legged. Your pen was out. Your eyes were on my face with the analytical attention that you applied to observable phenomena — the clinical gaze that treated symptoms as data and data as path.

"What did you feel?" you asked.

"Resistance."

"Where?"

The question was simple. The answer was not. Where? The resistance wasn't located — wasn't in the chest or the head or any anatomical district that could be identified and addressed. It was everywhere. It was the entire self. The wall wasn't in me — the wall *was* me.

"From me," I said.

The resistance came from me. From the core, from the marrow, from the place where conviction lived as structure. From *me*. From the part that had known — not believed, *known* — for millennia that death was inapplicable. That mortality was theoretical. That the sentence "I am mortal" was as meaningful as "I am a stone" — grammatically correct and ontologically false.

"From you?" you repeated. 

"From me."

You wrote. The pen moved with the precision that indicated importance — the deliberate strokes, the denser characters, the compression of meaning into ink that characterized your most critical recordings.

"Would you try again?" you asked.

"Maybe later or tomorrow. I need to let this sink in for a bit."

I stood. The clearing was unremarkable. The stream ran. The birds sang. Xiǎohú slept or pretended to sleep. The world was entirely, aggressively normal and the normalcy was the indifference of a reality that had no opinion about a woman's failure to change herself.

I walked to the stream and washed my face. The water was cold. The cold was external and manageable — the opposite of everything inside.

Behind me, you continued writing. The diagnosis was complete. The patient and the disease were the same entity. The cure required the destruction of the thing being cured.

I dried my face with my sleeve.

Tomorrow.
