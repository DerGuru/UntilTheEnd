We were still walking among trees hidden by the fog. A glimpse of branches brought back a nest I had once found in a plum tree. I could no longer place it among the years, but I remembered the three speckled eggs. I had touched one, and warmth had come through the thin shell.

That small warmth went with me as I walked. Life had gone on inside something I could have broken between two fingers. I thought of how fragile things survived, then of Wei.

The trees disappeared above their stumps.

Knee-high trunks stood in a perfect circle around me, their cross-sections smooth as polished stone. Above the cut, the branches and canopy of a hundred trees had become dust. I had seen no falling. White air filled the space where the crowns had been.

For an instant, nothing. Then wind rushed in from every direction, filling the space the trees had occupied. Dust swept past the exposed stumps toward me.

I stood at the center.

The fog cleared with it, and its edge matched the edge of the circle. The fog had been mine too.

In the cleared air a hundred steps lay open in every direction. Stumps filled the circle, each cut at the same height, dust settling across them. The extent took longer to grasp than the burst itself.

You were on the ground.

You had dropped flat just before the burst, your face in the dirt, arms over your head. The qi pressure had given you that much warning. The cut had passed above your back, shearing the trunks and leaving the ground below intact. Dust covered your sleeves. You were close enough that I saw it lift as you moved.

"A warning," you said, standing, brushing dust from your sleeves, "would have been nice."

I couldn't answer. My limbs trembled, and my next breath came shallow in the hollow of my chest. I had released enough power to do this without feeling myself choose it.

Then I saw another nest.

It lay near the boundary of the destruction, under what remained of a tree. Much of the woven bowl had survived the fall. Twigs curved around a lining of moss and down, the work of days still holding its shape on the bare ground.

Three eggs had broken inside it. Yellow yolk pooled on the brown earth. Half-formed birds showed through the cracked shells, their bodies small enough to lie in the curve of a fingertip.

The mother lay dead next to the nest. Her body was whole enough to have survived the fall. Something else had reached her when the tree disappeared. A residue hung in the air around us, and I had no name for it. That was what remained of a small life after the change I had made.

I knelt.

Three eggs lay in the lining. My eyes went from one broken shell to the next. Some warmth lingered where the nest had held them together.

The mother's feathers were plain brown. A sparrow, one of the common birds whose calls I had heard across so many forests. There would be others beyond this circle. More eggs would hatch elsewhere on the mountain.

I kept looking at these three.

My hands shook above the broken shells. Past them lay the dead bird, then the circle of stumps I had made without intending to. I lowered my hands to my knees. Even pressed against cloth, they trembled.

You had your pen out. While I knelt, you wrote in the notebook, glancing from the nest to the circle around us. You left me with the bird and told me nothing about it.

The smell of burned feathers drifted up, faint, and I knew it. I looked again at the small body on the ground.

I stood slowly. My legs shook under my weight. Even standing took attention.

Around us the mountain was quiet. No birds called nearby, no insects stirred in the fallen leaves. Past the circle, living trees still stood. I tried to look toward them.

Instead I looked at my hands again. Lines crossed the palms. The joints bent as they always had. I had lived with these hands for millennia; they looked as ordinary here as they had by the cooking fire.

"Let's move on," I said.

"Now?"

"Now."

We left the circle behind us. The dust was settling.

The smell of burned feathers followed us down the trail.
