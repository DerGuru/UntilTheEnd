The burst came as the cruelty of a power that operated below conscious access, the involuntary reflex of reality-shaping firing before the mind could intervene. Just the moment before and the moment after and between them — annihilation.

I was thinking about a nest I'd seen years ago. Decades. Centuries. The blur of time that made it impossible to locate the memory precisely. A bird's nest in a plum tree. Three eggs, speckled, warm. I'd touched one. The shell had been thin enough to feel the life inside, the fragility that separated existence from absence, the membrane between being and not-being that was thinner than paper and stronger than intent.

I was thinking about how fragile things survived. I thought about how Wei didn't. And the world broke.

The trees were sheared. The trunks remained below the cut, knee-high stumps in a perfect circle, their cross-sections smooth as polished stone. Everything above — canopy, branches, the mass of a hundred trees from shoulder height upward — was dust. White air. The transition occupied no time.

The sound was a hush — the collective exhalation of a hundred trees ceasing to exist at once, the vacuum that formed where mass had been, the momentary silence of air rushing to fill the space that matter had evacuated. Then: the rush. Wind from all directions, converging on the circle of nothing I'd created.

I stood at the center.

The fog cleared. Of course it did. The fog had been mine too.

In the cleared air, the damage was worse than the experience of causing it. A circle of devastation. A hundred steps in every direction. Dust settling. The silence that followed destruction, the world holding its breath in the aftermath of violence.

You were on the ground.

You'd felt it build — the qi accumulating in the air around me, the pressure that preceded the release, the atmospheric tension that your body had learned to read. You'd dropped flat a half-second before the burst, face in the dirt, arms over your head, the instinctive compression of a body making itself small. The wave had passed above you — chest height, radiating outward like a horizontal blade, shearing everything at trunk-level and leaving what was below untouched.

"A warning," you said, standing, brushing dust from your sleeves, "would have been nice."

I didn't answer. I couldn't. My limbs were trembling, my chest hollow, the body paying the tax for something the mind had signed without reading.

Then I saw another nest. 

It had been in one of the trees. The trees that were now dust, the trees that had been the boundary of the destruction. The nest was on the ground. It was intact — almost. The structural weave of the bowl was preserved, the architecture of twigs, moss and down that the mother had spent days constructing, the home she had built with the only tools available to her.

Three eggs were broken. Cracked. The yolks pooled yellow on the brown earth. The embryos inside — visible, half-formed, the tiny translucent bodies of birds that would never —

The mother was beside the nest. Dead. She had been in the tree when the tree ceased to exist. She had survived the fall but not the... I didn't know what to call it. The residual. The leftover. The toxicity of a space where reality had been forcibly rearranged, the environmental damage that my emotions inflicted on things too small to notice and too alive to withstand.

I knelt.

Three eggs. Three lives. Three futures that had been as real as breathing and were now as real as the trees, which was to say — not at all.

The mother's feathers were brown and unremarkable. She'd been a sparrow, maybe. Something common. Something that existed in numbers large enough to absorb losses like this without species-level consequence. The math of survival. Populations could sustain casualties, percentages were resilient, the aggregate could absorb the individual's destruction without breaking.

But the math didn't help.

I stared at the broken shells, the dead bird, the circle of nothing that I'd made without choosing to. My hands were shaking with something older than grief, rawer than anger, a feeling that predated language. It was the tremor of a being confronting the brute consequences of my own existence.

You had your pen out and wrote. You didn't say anything about the nest. You didn't say anything about the bird. You wrote. The writing was your response and it was as permanent as words wouldn't have been.

The smell of burned feathers reached me — faint, organic, the chemical signature of keratin exposed to forces it was never designed to encounter. The smell was wrong. 

I stood. My legs were uncertain. The exhaustion was deep, the kind that lived in the bones rather than the muscles.

Around us, the mountain was quiet. The birds that remained had fled. The insects had gone silent. The landscape hushed after violence. The collective breath-holding of creatures who had survived by virtue of distance.

I looked at my hands. They were ordinary hands. They had lines, joints and the wear of a body that had existed for millennia without changing. They looked ordinary.

They were.

"Let's move on," I said.

"Now?"

"Now."

We left the circle behind us. The dust was settling. 

The smell of burned feathers followed us down the trail.
