The cave came back. Fragments that were too clear to be fragments, pieces that fitted together without instruction, the memory assembling itself from the sediment.
Stone. Always stone. The walls were rough. I could feel them, feel the texture of unworked granite under small fingertips, the cold that was permanent, geological — the cold that lived in the rock itself and radiated outward in an endless, uninterested exhalation. A gap in the mountain that happened to be large enough for a very small person.

Darkness. A greyness seeped in from somewhere, the reluctant light that penetrated the cave mouth and diffused through the first turn of the passage. Enough to see shapes. Details dissolved into grey. The world at the beginning had been grey, cold and close.

Water.

The sound — the cave's heartbeat. The gurgling was regular, rhythmic, the water falling from a crack in the ceiling at intervals that could have been timed with a metronome. Each drip was a second, or a year, or an eon. At the beginning, time had no markers. The drip was the only clock. The drip was the only proof that the world was still operating.

"There was hunger," I said.

You looked up from your notebook. We were sitting where we'd been sitting. The mountain camp, the afternoon light slanting through thinning clouds, the ordinary present containing the extraordinary past. The fire had burned down to embers hours ago but neither of us had fed it. Your tea had gone cold in its cup, a skin of tannin forming on the surface. You'd balanced the notebook on one knee, the pen in your right hand, your left holding the page flat against a wind that came and went without pattern.

You sent me a questioning look, prompting me to elaborate.

"I was eating what the cave offered. Moss. Insects. Water from the drip. Whatever could sustain a small body."

"Do you think, you were immortal yet?" you asked.

"Not yet. The conviction, the immortality — it was subterranean. Instinctive. It was — forming. Like the stone herself. Layer by layer. Each day of surviving in the cave was another layer. Each day of not dying was another confirmation that I would not die. The cave was the crucible. The birthplace not of the body but of the conviction."

You wrote for a while. I watched Xiǎohú nose through the scrub at the camp's edge, investigating something between two rocks with the single-minded focus of a predator who'd found something that might be edible or might just be interesting. After a moment, she lost interest. 

The memory spoke of cold nights. An infinite sequence of cold nights, the same night repeated with variations so minor they didn't register as change. The small body curled on stone, the fetal arrangement of a child conserving warmth against a surface that had none to offer. The sound of breathing, her breathing, only her breathing, the singular respiratory track of the only person in the cave, in the world, in the grey beginnings of time.

"Was there anyone else?" you asked.

"No."

"Ever?"

I searched. The memory was clear but finite. It showed what it showed and withheld the rest — candle-reach memory, illuminating only the nearest walls and nothing beyond.

"Not that I can find. The memory, it's like looking into water. Clear at the surface. Darker as it goes down. I was alone. Whether there was someone before the cave, before the memory begins, I don't know."

The wind picked up. A gust that carried the smell of rain from the north side of the ridge and pushed the cold tea's surface into tiny waves. I pulled my collar tighter — a reflex, not necessity. The body still pretending it felt temperature the way bodies were supposed to.

"Before the memory."

"There might be nothing before the memory. The cave might be the first thing. No parents — not that there weren't any. There must have been. A child doesn't appear in a cave from nothing. But the memory doesn't hold them. No faces, no voices, no — " I stopped. The thought was larger than I expected, larger from inside than it appeared from outside. "The memory starts in the cave. Alone. As if everything before it was erased, or never recorded. Maybe they died before?"

Your pen had stopped again. Your lips parted slightly, the eyes widened. 

"Nothing before the cave," you said.

"Nothing I can reach. It starts there and everything before it is blank."

"How old?" you asked. The scholar again. Data points.

I consulted the memory. The hands — small, but not infant-small. The body that could curl on stone and survive the cold, that could find moss and catch insects. Capable enough to persist, young enough to fit in a gap in the mountain.

"Ten," I said. "Maybe twelve." The estimate surprised me — the specificity of it. The memory had dimensions after all, proportions that could be measured against the body I wore now.

Ten. Maybe twelve. About as old as Wei had been when I found him at the edge of a forest with a broken tooth and too much stubbornness.

The parallel landed in my chest like a stone dropped into still water.

I reached for your cold tea and drank it. You didn't look up. The tannin was bitter but the act of swallowing was a physical thing, a present-tense thing, and my body wanted to be reminded that it was here and not in a cave at the beginning of remembering.

The memory settled again. The sediment returning to the bottom. But the image remained, accessible. The cave. The cold. The loneliness. The drip of water measuring time that had no other measurement and a small body curled on stone, surviving. Each survival a layer, each layer a brick in a conviction being built without a builder.

I had made myself immortal without knowing I was doing it.

The cave had been the kiln. The solitude had been the fuel. And the conviction — I will not die — had formed the way ash forms in a fire. As the inevitable byproduct of a process that was happening beneath consciousness.

"I didn't choose it," I said. "The immortality wasn't a choice. It was a — sedimentation. Layer upon layer of surviving until surviving became the only possibility."

You looked at the sky. The clouds, thicker now, accumulating, the afternoon shifting toward the grey weight of potential rain.

"Then how do you un-choose something you never chose?"

The question sat between us like a stone in a field. Neither of us moved it. Neither of us could.

Xiǎohú yawned. From her safe distance, she watched us with her amber eyes and the patience of an animal that had no stakes in philosophical paradoxes but was willing to wait while the humans sorted themselves out.
