Afternoon light slanted across your notebook while I sat with my hands against the ground. The stone's roughness brought the cave back in pieces. I followed the turn of a remembered wall, then the narrow floor under it, finding how they fit together.

Stone enclosed everything I could recall. My small fingertips had traced unworked granite, cold even after I had held them there. The space was a natural gap in the mountain, big enough to fit in if I curled up to lie down.

Gray light reached through the cave mouth and faded at the first turn of the passage. It showed the walls but not their detail. The world at the beginning had been gray, cold and close.

Water.

I remembered its regular gurgle. Water fell from a crack in the ceiling, each drop clear in the confined space. I had lain listening through the dark, waiting for the next drip. How many had passed between sleeping and waking, I couldn't say.

"There was hunger," I said.

You looked up from your notebook. We were still sitting in the mountain camp. The fire had burned down to embers more than a watch ago, but neither of us had fed it. Your tea had gone cold in its cup, a skin of tannin forming on the surface. You balanced the notebook on one knee, your left hand holding the page flat against the fitful wind.

You raised your brows, inviting me to go on.

"I was eating what I could find in the cave. Moss. Insects. Water from the drip. Whatever could sustain a small body."

"Do you think you were immortal yet?" you asked.

"Not yet. The conviction was forming. I didn't know what I was doing. Each day I survived was another day when dying had failed to happen. I woke on the same stone. After enough mornings, I must have come to expect it. The certainty began there."

You wrote for a while. Xiǎohú nosed through the scrub at the camp's edge. She pressed her muzzle between two rocks, sniffed, and lifted her head. Whatever it was, it was still between the stones.

More cold nights came to me. I couldn't tell them apart. I remembered curling on the stone with my knees pulled up, shifting whenever the surface under me grew too cold. My breath sounded close to my face. When I held it, water filled the silence.

"Was there anyone else?" you asked.

"No."

"Ever?"

I searched as far as I could. The walls were clear, but past them lay a blankness that gave me no purchase.

"Not that I can find. The memory, it's like looking into water. Clear at the surface. Darker as it goes down. I was alone. Whether there was someone before the cave, before the memory begins, I don't know."

The wind picked up. A gust carried the smell of rain from the north side of the ridge, ruffling the cold tea. I pulled my collar tighter out of habit. The gesture was older than my understanding of how little the cold could do to me.

"Before the memory."

"There might be nothing before the memory. The cave might be the first thing I can reach. I must have had parents. A child doesn't appear in a cave from nothing. But I can't find their faces, their voices, or—" I stopped. Until I tried to name what was missing, I hadn't felt its extent. "The memory starts in the cave. Alone. As if everything before it was erased, or never recorded. Maybe they died before?"

Your pen had stopped again. Your lips parted, and your eyes widened.

"Nothing before the cave," you said.

"Nothing I can reach. It starts there and everything before it is blank."

"How old?" you asked, bringing your pen back to the page.

I considered the hands I remembered. They were small, though old enough to catch insects. That child had gathered moss, found water, and curled into shelter against the cold. I compared her with the body I sat in.

"Ten," I said. "Maybe twelve." I had expected to find only cold stone. Instead I had an age I could measure, proportions I could set against my own hands.

Ten. Maybe twelve. About as old as Wei had been when I found him at the edge of a forest with a broken tooth and too much stubbornness.

My breath caught. I reached for your cold tea and drank it. You didn't look up. The tannin was bitter. I swallowed slowly, the cup against my mouth, while the image of that boy stood next to the child in the cave.

The cave grew less immediate, though I could still reach it. I held the cup and recalled the water drip, the cold floor, and the small body curled against it. Morning after morning, I had woken there. That life left no room for anything but surviving the next night.

I had made myself immortal without knowing I was doing it.

I looked at the embers in our fire. In the cave, every night had repeated the same need: I will not die. I had lived by it long before I could have told anyone what it meant.

"I didn't choose it," I said. "The immortality wasn't a choice. It accumulated. Layer upon layer of surviving until surviving became the only possibility."

You glanced at the sky. The clouds were thicker now, and the afternoon had shifted toward the gray weight of rain.

"Then how do you un-choose something you never chose?"

I turned the cold cup between my hands. Your pen rested above the page again.

From past the disturbed stones, Xiǎohú yawned and settled on the ground. We sat over a question neither of us could answer.
