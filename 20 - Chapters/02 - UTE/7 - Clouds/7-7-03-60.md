Over the next days, small changes accumulate on her walks between the house and the market. Her steps land differently on the packed earth. Warmth moves through her even when she gives it no direction.

Her qi keeps its strength, but its movement has changed. The distinction is worth making, though she can think of no one who needs to hear it. For millennia it held her to a single imperative: preserve, continue. Now its warmth flows freely, like water through a riverbed long blocked. The channels were already there. The movement is easy. She does not expect easy.

She stops in the path with morning sun on her shoulders. The village wakes around her. The qi runs warm through places where she has grown used to holding it steady.

For the first time since a girl on a mountain refused to die, she feels it move this freely.

And she feels time.

She has known for millennia that heartbeats pass and years accumulate. Here duration is a current against someone standing in a river. Each heartbeat is present, then gone. She moves within that one-way flow, passing with it instead of standing apart.

This is what she feared. Now she stands still, fascinated by each small departure. People around her live in it every day, taking for granted a sensation they have never lacked. She has known its absence. She has watched years leave everyone around her while she stood outside their passage. To her the current is new. She half-closes her eyes on the village path, giving a passing moment far more attention than her neighbors would find sensible.

Her skin feels thinner. She presses a fingertip to her forearm as if testing a bruise. The skin gives, ordinary flesh that will wrinkle, spot, and eventually lose its strength. She lifts the finger, and the shallow impression fades. She runs her thumb across the inside of her wrist and finds the pulse. She has known that steadiness for millennia. Time inside it is new.

She walks to the market.

Noise fills the crowded market. Colored awnings sag over stalls pressed close together, leaving little room for the people between them. Fresh produce mingles with the tang of sweat. Under both lies the sweetness of overripe fruit that has yet to find a buyer.

A voice. Loud. Louder than necessary. Louder than anything has ever needed to be.

"SQUEEZE IT."

A man stands behind a vegetable stall, his broad face above arms thickened by a lifetime of lifting crates. He holds out a round red tomato.

"If it gives," he says, still at full volume although Yun stands a pace away, "it's ripe."

As far as she can tell, her hearing is perfectly adequate.

She squeezes gently. The fruit gives under her fingers, enough to show the ripeness he means.

"See?" he says. "Easy."

She buys the tomato with several other things he insists she needs. She probably does. Qi chose or supplied so much for her that she has to relearn how to judge ordinary produce. Most people learn it at their mother's elbow. She is coming to it late.

She carries the vegetables home. They include a yielding tomato recommended by a man who treats volume as a sales strategy.

She begins cooking. The vegetables are a small puzzle to someone who has eaten for millennia without needing to. She has watched thousands of meals prepared in hundreds of cultures and knows what ought to happen between a raw ingredient and a finished dish. The knowledge is vast enough to make her hesitation over a few vegetables ridiculous. Her hands lack the practice to use it. Watching someone swim has never been the same as entering the water.

The result is technically food. She can name every ingredient, and none of them has gained anything from being brought together.

She eats standing in the kitchen. The food is mediocre, but she can account for its faults herself. She chose these vegetables. She prepared them badly. There is something tender about owning so small a failure. She finishes the meal without blaming fate or something acting through her. Tomorrow she can try again with what she has learned.

Each heartbeat is present, then gone.

Fascinating.
