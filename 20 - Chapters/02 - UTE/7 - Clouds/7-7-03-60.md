The change arrives over days, the way weather changes — in increments that the body registers before the mind can name.

Her qi has changed. Not diminished, but changed. The distinction matters, though she is not sure to whom. For millennia, her qi has held. Every particle devoted to a single imperative. Preserve! Continue! Now, that the imperative is gone, the qi flows. It flows through her, not away. Spreading it's warmth. Much like water flows through a riverbed that was blocked for a very long time, finding paths that were always there, moving along channels designed for movement, and the moving is — easy. She did not expect easy.

She stops on the path between her house and the market. She stands in the middle of the packed earth with the morning sun on her shoulders and the sound of the village waking around her. And she feels the qi moving inside her body with the fluid freedom of something that has come home.

For the first time since a girl on a mountain refused to die, she feels it move.

And she feels time.

Not as a concept. Not as the abstract awareness that has accompanied her for millennia — the intellectual knowledge that seconds pass and years accumulate like sediment. It feels like a current when you stand in a river. Something flowing through her that is not energy but duration. Every second is present and then gone. The one-directional flow that mortality provides, the arrow that points one way and does not care who is watching.

That was, what she was so afraid of. But now, she is fascinated. Each second a small departure. Each moment a small goodbye. The sensation that billions of people experience every moment of their lives and take for granted because they have never known its absence. She has known its absence. She has lived without time's current for so long that the current is new, which makes her stand very still on a village path with her eyes half-closed and a look on her face that would concern the neighbors if any of them were watching.

Her skin feels thinner. She touches her forearm — a small test, curious, the way you press a bruise to see if it still hurts. The skin gives under her fingertip. The elastic response of skin doing what skin does without supernatural assistance. Ordinary skin. Human skin. The kind that will wrinkle and spot and eventually forget how to hold itself together. She runs her thumb across the inside of her wrist and feels the pulse there, steady, and the steadiness is both old and new.

She walks to the market.

The market is noise and color and the organized chaos of commerce in a space too small for the commerce it contains. Stalls crowd each other like teeth in a child's mouth. Awnings sag. The smell of fresh produce mingles with the human contribution of bodies working in proximity, the particular tang of sweat and effort and the faint sweetness of overripe fruit that nobody has bought yet.

A voice. Loud. Louder than necessary. Louder than anything has ever needed to be.

"SQUEEZE IT."

A man behind a vegetable stall. Wide face, thick arms, the physical configuration of someone who has been lifting crates since his whole life. He holds a tomato toward her — red and round.

"If it gives," he says, still at full volume despite the fact that Yun is standing a meter away and is not, as far as she can tell, hard of hearing, "it's ripe."

She squeezes. The tomato gives — slightly, the elastic resistance of fruit at the correct ripeness, the brief deformation that indicates readiness.

"See?" he says. "Easy."

She buys the tomato. And several other things he insists she needs, which she probably does, because she has yet to relearn how to identify good produce. The qi provided. The qi no longer provides. She is relearning the skill that everyone else had learned at their mother's elbow, millennia later.

She walks home with vegetables she chose on the advice of a man who treats volume as a sales strategy and a tomato that gives when squeezed.

She cooks. The vegetables are a puzzle — the knowledge gap of a woman who has eaten for millennia without needing to, now navigating the distance between raw ingredient and prepared meal without the intuition that practice provides. She knows, in theory, how food works. She has watched thousands of meals prepared across hundreds of cultures over thousands of years. The knowledge is vast and entirely useless, because knowing how something works and knowing how to do it are separated by the same gulf that separates watching someone swim and swimming.

The result is food. Technically. The culinary equivalent of a sentence that is grammatically correct and says nothing.

She eats. Standing. In the kitchen. The food is mediocre. But the mediocrity is hers — not inherited, not imposed, not the product of circumstance or fate. Hers. She made this. She made it badly. There is something almost tender about the ownership.

Every second present and then gone.

Fascinating.
