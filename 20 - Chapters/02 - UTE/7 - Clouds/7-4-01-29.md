The stream beside the clearing ran clear over flat stones, the sound thin in the early light.

"Are you ready?" you asked.

I didn't feel ready, which was a weird feeling for me. I was ready for almost everything for a long time now. Nothing had been able to surprise me, to challenge me, to push me to the point where I couldn't handle it anymore. I was ready for anything because I'd been through everything. 

But I demonstrated anyway. Not to impress. I'd passed that point where impressing anyone mattered, the threshold beyond which competence became private. But to test and to verify. Because a witness made the results real in a way that solitary practice didn't.

I thought about the stream beside our campsite — the thin mountain stream that ran over rocks, through moss and produced the continuous sound that mountains used as ambient noise. The water lifted. Not just a drop this time. A ribbon. A continuous, flowing, self-sustaining arc of water that rose from the stream, hung in the air and moved where I believed it belonged. No effort. No strain. It was frightening, how fast I was coming to grips with it. How easy it felt after only a few weeks of trying. How fascinating to learn something new in many millennia.

I returned the water to the stream. Seamlessly. As though it had never left.

A closed bud on the bush nearest my right hand. I believed it open and the bud opened. Believed it closed and it closed. Believed in the shade of pink that existed between rose and dawn and the petals shifted, the color changing like a blush, the botanical compliance of a living thing responding to a woman's aesthetic preferences.

The sunlight entering the clearing. I believed it bent and it bent. The shadow of the tree beside you moved to the left without the tree moving, the light source shifting in the impossible geometry of a sky that was cooperating with a feeling rather than a position.

The air warmed. Slightly — I wasn't going to boil us. The controlled escalation that distinguished precision from power. Then it cooled and warmed again. The atmospheric modulation of a woman adjusting reality's thermostat with the casual accuracy of someone who had done it enough times that the doing was no longer an event.

Xiǎohú lifted off the ground. Her amber eyes widened — the startled expression of an animal that had been resting and was now hovering and didn't understand why. I set her down. Gently. She shook herself and relocated to a position further away from me. As if that would help. As if distance could protect her from the woman who was bending the world.

You watched and nodded. Approval, assessment and conclusion in one gesture. 

"You're ready," you said.

"I'm not."

The answer rising from somewhere below conscious processing, the response that the self generated before the mind could intervene.

"Why do you think so?"

Three breaths. Four. The count of silence that separated the question from the answer I didn't want to give. The air in the clearing was still — genuinely still, not the artificial stillness of a woman's power suppressing the wind but the natural quiet of a mountain morning before the thermal currents began.

"Because I'm afraid."

There it was. Something I had not put into words for millennia. Had not felt like in millennia. I was afraid. 

The words hung in the air. Literally. The air around us responded to the admission the way air responded to all my feelings. The temperature dropped. The breeze stopped. The clearing held its breath.

You said nothing and waited. The patient silence of a man who understood that certain declarations required space, that the air around a confession needed to remain undisturbed long enough for the confession to find its shape.

When the response finally came, it was short. "Good." You said, with a nod.

"How is that good?"

"Anyone who has no fear has nothing to lose." You set down your pen. You looked at me with the direct gaze that you used for moments that mattered, the full-spectrum attention that you rationed carefully because the full weight of your observation was more than most moments could bear. "You're afraid because you need this to work — and because it might. That's a beginning."

I looked down. I could bend water, grow flowers, shift light, warm air and lift objects. I could reshape the external world with a precision that made physics optional and reality negotiable.

Yet, I still couldn't reshape me.

The inward turn, the reversal of power from outward to inward, from world to self, from everything-except to the-only-thing, remained. The next step. The only step. The step I was afraid of.

Because I was afraid.

And being afraid was, according to You, a beginning.

I didn't believe you. But I believed that you believed it and the distinction between those two things was narrower than I'd expected.
