The trail climbed through thinning forest. Trees grew shorter here; the air carried the mineral sharpness of altitude. We had taken another path away from the circle, following the slope where it offered easier ground.

"Try the opposite," you said. 

"The opposite of what?"

"You know what sadness does. Find out what something else does."

I shortened my stride. After watching the trees disappear, I was reluctant to test what else I might do. Yet you were right that we needed to know. I couldn't keep walking among living things while refusing to learn how I affected them.

"Think of the drop," you said.

I remembered the water suspended above its leaf. The clearing had shone upside down inside it, and you had held your pen still while you watched. I recalled that small curve of light with unusual clarity, even after everything that had followed.

I thought about it.

Low thorny bushes lined the trail, hugging the ground in the exposed air. In the one nearest my left hand, a bud swelled.

You saw it before I did. "There."

The bud opened as we watched. Petals parted one after another, spreading far enough to show their pale inner surfaces. The whole movement took a breath, each change distinct, though far faster than ordinary growth.

A small white flower stood fully open on the bush. It should have been a bud for another fourteen days or so. Its petals spread wider than any flower of that kind I remembered.

Around it, the grass was greener.

The change was subtle, like the difference between well-watered ground and a drier patch next to it. Richer green spread unevenly from my feet to the edge of the trail, leaving the farther grass in its ordinary shade.

"So," you said, stopping beside me. You looked from the grass to the flower. "Grief destroys. Hope—"

"Grows things."

"Grows things."

We stood in the greener grass, looking at the open flower. I had held on to the possibility that the destruction was an isolated failure. Here was another feeling, followed by another visible change. I would have to allow for it whenever I walked this close to anything living.

"You're practically a whole climate on foot," you said.

I looked at you.

"That was a compliment," you added.

"It wasn't."

You let the correction stand. It was hard to tell whether that was decency or the good sense you usually showed when I looked at you like that.

We walked on. Rich green marked every place I stepped, and the grass past it was duller. Looking back, I could trace our route by the difference alone. Flowers opened one by one along it, pale spots among the bushes we had passed.

The burned-feather smell returned.

I knew it was memory. We were far from the nest, but the new flowers had brought back the three broken eggs and the plain brown bird beside them. I looked back again and again, trying to hold the blooms apart from that small body.

The grass around my feet browned briefly, a change at the edge of my vision as you turned toward it.

"I saw that," you said.

"Saw what?"

"The grass. You thought about something and the grass responded."

"I thought about the nest."

You took out your pen without answering. Soon you were writing as you walked, giving the page more attention than the uneven trail. I watched where you put your feet.

"Every emotion," I said. "Not just the big ones."

"No."

"Every flicker. Every passing thought."

"Apparently."

The trail climbed on between rocks. Above us lay the deep blue of high altitude, crossed by a few clouds. I studied their edges for a change that coincided with what I felt.

I couldn't tell. They might have been moving in the wind; they might have been changing because of me. Even close study left me uncertain.

Xiǎohú walked through the greener grass at her usual pace. Pale flowers glinted in her gold-flecked eyes as she turned her head. She stepped between the blooming bushes without hesitating, following the altered trail.

We camped early. The sun was still above the ridge when you stopped, set the pack down, and began preparing the fire.

"Why are we stopping?" I asked.

"Because you're tired."

"I'm not tired."

"Your grass is getting patchy."

I looked down. The green around my feet had gone patchy, rich in some places and ordinary in others. You had noticed before I had. I could deny being tired, but you had an inconvenient second opinion growing around my boots.

I sat.

Rice cooked over the small fire. Evening light turned gold along the ridge, then orange. By the time purple settled into the hollows, I had been sitting for a while without trying to change anything.

I thought of the nest again. The faint smell came back with the image of the shells. Around my feet a small circle of grass browned, the nearest blades wilting against the ground.
