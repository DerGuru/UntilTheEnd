A signature reaches her from the ridge, two hundred paces east. Yun turns toward it while the bird calls on by the rock. She knows that presence.

It is old in a way few things she encounters are old.

Zhìwēng.

She stands. The Wei memory is still warm. The bird is still calling. The fear is still sitting where it sits.

She walks east. Xiǎohú waits on the rock, her amber eyes following Yun across the slope.

He stands on the ridge as easily as he might sit by a fire. His posture takes no effort. He was on this mountain before she arrived; she is sure of it, though she found no sign of him until now.

She knows his face from long ago. Zhìwēng, the Wise Old Man: the last name he offered her, with a shrug at the business of names. They have sat together a few times across the long span of their lives. Little needed explaining at those meetings. They could leave important things alone for a while.

Here, in the gray overcast of altitude, his face looks the same. Habituated.

Two fingers. Raised. Index and middle. The old greeting, unused between them for centuries: equals meeting on a road.

She stops. Ten paces.

She raises two fingers in return. The gesture is slower than his, smaller, but clear.

"Hello, old friend."

His face shifts toward a smile and stops just short. Younger faces would give it more movement.

"Hello, Liè," he says.

The old name tightens her chest. Liè. People in the southern plains had called her that when she was still burning, when justice and violence were the same gesture to her. She could try to count the years since she last heard it. The feeling it brings needs no count.

"Or should I use the new one?" A pause. "Yun?"

"Yun," she says quietly, leaving the old name where he placed it.

He accepts it with a nod. Once he had endured her burning, continuing to speak until she could hear him. He had asked, without judgment, whether she was finished. She had been. She had needed someone to ask before she could recognize it.

"How do you like my cave?" he says.

"Damp," she says.

"Yes." A pause. "It has always been damp."

He lowers his hand.

"You held," he says. His voice is warm and low, carrying easily across the ten paces. "Seven heartbeats. What pulled you back?"

Her voice meets the mountain air. "The wall."

"What wall? Whose wall?"

She studies him in the gray light. The question is simpler than Xu Ran's, simpler than the ones she asks herself. She has named the wall's source often enough. Hearing him ask leaves her with the answer she has been circling.

She does not respond. He does not need her to.

Cold wind passes between them, along the side of her face.

"I watched you protect the settlement," he says. "Every wall. Every window. Every path."

A pause. The duration of one breath.

"Everything except."

Except. She remembers the dead grass, then the night she placed barriers around the settlement. Every surface she could protect had received her attention. The one place she could not change was inside her.

She meets his eyes. He has lived long enough for her to leave much unsaid, including what it means to outlast the people who once understood her.

He looks back without hurry. She lets the silence last.

Yun exhales.

"You knew," she says. "What I did to myself."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

His eyes rest on her face. His expression approaches regret, though she cannot be certain. They have both lived with the consequences of interventions that seemed useful at the time.

"Would you have believed someone else's answer about something like that?"

She exhales again. She would have disputed the answer, found a reason it could not apply to her, and continued searching elsewhere. The understanding she has came through reaching it herself. She can hardly ask him to have spared her that part.

He nods once. She knows the conversation is ending.

"Don't be afraid. I hope you'll succeed. This time."

He winks toward Xiǎohú on the rock, then turns. She lets the last nod serve as their farewell.

"What happens if I don't?" Yun calls after him. His signature is already diminishing. It fades before she can follow it farther.

She stands alone on the ridge. Xiǎohú is a rust-colored shape on the rock below. The bird has stopped calling, leaving the sound of wind over stone.

She stares at the place where Zhìwēng stood a moment ago.

Don't be afraid. Easier said than done.

The words settle under her sternum, where the fear still tightens her breathing. Wei's face is close enough to remember. She lets both stay.

She walks back to the rock. Xiǎohú lifts her head. The bird begins calling again as Yun reaches the place where she sat.

The sentence stays.
