A signature on the ridge. East. Two hundred paces. She knows it the way the body knows weather — not through analysis but through the recognition that precedes analysis.

Old. Her kind of old. And known.

Zhìwēng.

She stands. The Wei memory is still warm. The bird is still calling. The fear is still sitting where it sits.

She walks east. Xiǎohú stays on the rock, watching with amber eyes, the animal assessment of a situation that does not require an animal's participation.

He is standing on the ridge. Not sitting — standing, the posture of someone for whom standing and sitting are equivalent states. He has been here longer than she. The knowledge arrives without evidence. He was on this mountain before her.

His face. She knows it from a long time ago. Zhìwēng. The Wise Old Man. His last known name, the one he'd offered with a shrug that contained centuries of not caring what anyone called him. They had sat together a few times across the long span of their lives. As old acquaintances do when importance has been exhausted and what remains is the comfort of being understood without explanation.

Here, in the grey overcast of altitude, his face looks the same. Habituated. 

Two fingers. Raised. Index and middle. The old greeting — the one that hasn't been used in centuries, the greeting of equals meeting on a road.

She stops. Ten paces.

Two fingers. Raised. The gesture mirrored — slower, smaller, but returned.

"Hello, old friend."

His face shifts. Not a smile — the territory adjacent to a smile, the way beings of their age approximate expressions that younger faces wear openly.

"Hello Liè," he says.

The name lands in her chest like a hot coal. Liè. The name the people of the southern plains had given her when she was still burning. When justice and violence were the same gesture and she was both. She hasn't heard it in — the number doesn't matter. The feeling does.

"Or should I use the new one?" A pause. "Yun?"

"Yun," she says. Quietly. The way you close a door you don't intend to open again.

He nods. Accepts. The way he accepted her burning, once — watched it, said nothing, waited until she was done. He was the one who had asked, in a voice without judgement, whether she was finished. And she had been. She just hadn't known it until someone asked.

"How do you like my cave?" he says.

"Damp," she says.

"Yes." A pause. "It has always been damp."

He lowers his hand.

"You held," he says. His voice is warm and low. The acoustic register that old beings use when volume has lost its purpose. "Seven seconds. What pulled you back?"

Her voice meets the mountain air. "The wall."

"What wall? Whose wall?"

The question sits in the grey light. Simple. The simplest question she has been asked — simpler than any of Xu Ran's, simpler than any she has asked herself. The kind of question that does not require an answer because the question is the answer.

She does not respond. He does not need her to.

Wind passes between them. Cold. The punctuation of altitude.

"I watched you protect the settlement," he says. "Every wall. Every window. Every path."

A pause. The duration of one breath.

"Everything except."

The word arrives in her chest where the fear sits. Except. The word that has followed her from the dead grass, from the body, from the night she spent placing barriers on every surface except the one that mattered.

She looks at him. The looking of one old thing at another old thing — level, neutral, the looking that exists between beings who share the loneliness of having outlived any context for loneliness.

He looks back. Equal. Ancient. Unhurried.

Yun exhales.

"You knew," she says. "What I did to myself."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

He looks at her. The look contains something adjacent to regret — adjacent, not the thing itself, the territory near regret that beings of their magnitude occupy because regret requires the belief that intervention would have helped. And they know better.

"Would you have believed someone else's answer about something like that?"

She exhales again. The answer is no. The answer has always been no. Every piece of understanding she possesses was earned by arriving at it herself. None of it could have been given. All of it had to be found.

He nods. Once. The nod of conclusion — the gesture that replaces paragraphs.

"Don't be afraid. I hope you'll succeed. This time."

He winks at Xiǎohú and turns. No salute this time. No two-finger farewell. The nod was the greeting and the nod was the farewell. 

"What happens if I don't?" Yun calls after him. But his signature already diminishes. Fades. And is gone.

She stands on the ridge. Alone again. Xiǎohú is a rust-colored shape on the rock below. The bird has stopped calling. The wind continues because wind always continues.

She stares at the place, where Zhìwēng stood just a moment ago. 

*Don't be afraid. Easier said than done.*

The sentence hits where the fear sits, where the Wei-memory sits, where all the important things sit, in the chest, below the sternum, above the diaphragm.

She walks back to the rock. Xiǎohú lifts her head. The bird resumes. The mountain does not comment.

The sentence stays.
