I sat beside the trail with my hands turned upward on my knees. Deep lines crossed the palms, though the skin around them showed little age. I followed one crease with a finger. There was nothing in those hands by which someone could read the centuries of work they had done.

The nails were ordinary. So were the joints. I had carried the rabbit in these hands, feeling its warmth while I chose to kill it. In the forest, they had hung at my sides.

That was the part I could not get past. I had destroyed the trees without raising a hand. What caused it lay in the feeling itself, past any movement I could watch or learn to withhold. I could keep my fingers open here on my knees while something far deeper changed the ground around me.

How many times?

I took up the ridgeline question methodically. I could begin with the deaths that had mattered most, then examine what had happened around me. Even a few instances might help.

Wei came first. Then the names from the evening fire, the faces I had barely recovered before losing them again. I had grieved for each of them. But I had rarely attended to the weather or the birds while it happened.

The silence around Wei was clear enough to recall. Smaller losses might have left smaller changes. A wind had risen; an animal had fled. I remembered such things without knowing whether I had caused them. At the time, I had accepted them as part of the place where I stood.

You had given me a way to reconsider those memories. It changed what I understood of them while leaving the events themselves untouched. I could neither go back to examine the ground nor restore anything that had died there.

My gaze dropped to my hands again. I remembered the three eggs in their nest. I pictured the small bodies in the shells. This much I had seen closely enough to know: a feeling had passed through me, then they were dead.

"Stop," I said, recognizing the spiral I was following down.

I had spoken to myself. My hands lay open, as harmless-looking as before.

The training had begun with water, flame, and plants. I could change each deliberately now. What I needed to reach was the feeling that changed them without permission. Understanding it meant examining it, yet each attempt at examination brought me back to the nest.

"I need to stop," I said.

You stood three steps behind me on the trail. You had left that distance again while I sat with my hands on my knees. I did not have to meet your eyes to hear you.

"Stop what?" you said.

"Feeling."

"That's not healthy."

"Then I need to stop leaking."

"Yes."

"How?"

"I don't know yet."

I had wanted an answer from you, even one we would have to test. A suggestion would have given me something to do after standing up. You had always found another question when a solution failed. This time you stood behind me with the same uncertainty I had.

The smell of burned feathers returned. It came and went now, clear for a stretch before the smell of the trail took over again. I knew there was no bird beside me.

Three eggs.

The number was small enough to keep before me. With the names, I lost count almost at once. Here each shell came separately, each in its place in the lining.

Three eggs that I had broken with a feeling.

A crow landed on a rock by the trail. Iridescence showed across its black feathers as it tilted its head toward me. It settled, then looked along the path.

"Control isn't enough," I said. "I need to stop."

"You said that."

"I mean it differently now."

You waited.

"I've been treating this as a skill problem. Learn to control it. Get better. Master the technique. But it's not a skill. It's me. The control is me. If I control it, I'm still the thing that needs controlling. If I master it, I'm still the thing that needs mastering."

"What's the alternative?"

I didn't know. The question reached closer to the difficulty, though it gave me little to work with yet. I watched the crow, a living thing sitting within reach of me.

Xiǎohú watched the grass around my feet. Green blades rose there, then browned against the ground. The changes followed my breathing again.

A wind came from the east, carrying pine resin and the sharpness of cold stone. I drew it into my lungs. The mountain lay under me, solid through each change in the grass.

I went on breathing. Somewhere in that continuation was a choice I had yet to reach.
