By midmorning I had moved water, brought plants into bloom, and bent light across the clearing. I could warm the air or cool it by knowing its temperature had changed. Objects rose from the ground as easily as the drop had risen from its leaf. I sat among these alterations and prepared to turn inward again.

"I am mortal." I waited after saying it. My breathing went on, steady as ever.

"My finger could bleed." Once, that had produced a hesitation lasting scarcely a heartbeat. I held the finger before me and reached for the same possibility. My qi had steadied so quickly that the change survived only as a memory. I could not reproduce it.

"Water, plants, light, temperature, and falling," I said. "All controllable. Everything external responds. Everything internal doesn't."

You sat against your boulder with the notebook open. Your pen lay idle across the page. You had stopped writing some time ago, though you still watched each attempt closely.

"The eye can't see itself," you repeated.

"You already said that yesterday."

"That doesn't make it any less true today."

I looked at the sky. The clouds hung lower today, heavy over the ridge, and had thickened while we practiced. Their dark undersides made me wonder how long we had before rain.

"Maybe you're too close," you said again. "To yourself. The tool works on everything except the toolmaker. Because, in your case, the toolmaker is the tool. The conviction that shapes reality is the same conviction that constitutes your reality. You can't use yourself to change yourself because that needs changes to both the tool and the user."

"That's what you said last time as well. The paradox."

"I know, I'm repeating myself, but have you solved it?"

"No."

"Then it bears repeating."

You were right. I lowered the finger to my knee. Another explanation had brought us to the same place, with my hand unchanged before me. I could describe the difficulty more precisely each day without getting past it.

"What if," I said, "the answer is what we talked about? Stopping the holding."

"You tried that."

"I tried willing myself to stop holding. Which means I assume or believe that I am holding."

"You tried not-willing yourself to stop holding."

"Also holding."

Your mouth twitched toward a smile, then tightened. The exchange sounded absurd even to me. We had gone around to the beginning again, and I was still sitting in the same body.

"There has to be a way," you said. "You exist. You weren't always immortal. According to your cave memory, at some point in your past, the conviction had formed. And if it had formed, it can be reversed. It must."

"The formation was unconscious."

"Then maybe the reversal must also be unconscious."

I sat with the suggestion. Cool stone pressed against my palm, bringing back the cave floor. I had built the conviction through survival, one night following another, long before I understood what I was doing. There had been no decision I could now reverse.

Changing it would have to happen gradually, too. I could live differently and let one experience follow another until something deeper shifted with them. But I could scarcely picture experiences that would do it.

The first certainty had taken millennia. I looked at the unchanged skin of my hand again.

"Patience," you said, following my gaze. "The one thing you have no shortage of is time."

I looked at you. Thirty years left, maybe forty. You sat against the boulder, giving this morning to me as though there would always be another. I could lose that whole span among the years I was trying to undo.

I kept the thought to myself.

"Patience," I agreed. "I have time."

Rain began to fall from the low clouds. The first drops struck the rocks one by one, each impact clear in the pauses between our words. In the cave I had counted time by that sound. Then came the scent of wet earth, sweet as it had been the day I met Wei.
