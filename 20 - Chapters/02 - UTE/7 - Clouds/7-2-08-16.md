Night came and went. The morning was already half gone, when I stopped toying around.

Water moved. Plants bloomed. Light bent. The temperature of a clearing rose or fell because I knew it would. Gravity released what I believed it would release. Everything outward was becoming easy. Every physical law, every material condition, every arrangement of the world that existed outside the boundary of my own body — obedient.

But inward, I struggled.

"I am mortal." Nothing. The words hit the inner architecture and died.

"My finger could bleed." That had created a hesitation — once. That single, heartbeat-long flicker. Was not reproducible. Not sustained. The qi had wavered and then re-established with speed. Afterwards not even the flicker could be found.

"Water, plants, light, temperature, gravity," I said. "All controllable. Everything external responds. Everything internal doesn't."

You were sitting against your boulder. Notebook open. The pen idle — he'd stopped writing and was thinking, the stillness that indicated your mind was working on a problem that the pen couldn't help with.

"The eye can't see itself," you repeated.

"You already said that yesterday."

"That doesn't make it any less true today."

I looked at the sky. The clouds were — different today. Lower. Heavier. The compressed cloud-cover of mountain weather on the edge. Rain or not, storm or drift.

"Maybe you're too close," you said again. "To yourself. The tool works on everything except the toolmaker. Because, in your case, the toolmaker is the tool. The conviction that shapes reality is the same conviction that constitutes your reality. You can't use yourself to change yourself because that needs changes to both the tool and the user."

"That's what you said last time as well. The paradox."

"I know, I'm repeating myself, but have you solved it?"

"No."

"Then it bears repeating."

You were right, which didn't make it any less frustrating, when running against a wall. The same wall, described from different angles, illuminated by different lights, but the same wall. 

"What if," I said, "the answer is not changing. What if the answer is — what we talked about. Stopping the holding."

"You tried that."

"I tried willing myself to stop holding. Which means, I assume or believe that I am holding."

"You tried not-willing yourself to stop holding."

"Also holding."

You almost smiled. Not the warm kind — the grim kind. The recognition of an absurdity that was also a prison. The joke that was not a joke because the woman telling it was actually trapped inside it.

"There has to be a way," you said. "You exist. You weren't always immortal. According to your cave memory, at some point in your past, the conviction had formed. And if it had formed, it can be reversed. It must."

"The formation was unconscious."

"Then maybe the reversal must also be unconscious."

The logic was — I sat with it. The conviction hadn't been built by decision. It had accumulated, layer by layer, in the cave, in the cold, in the solitude. Each survival a brick. Each day of not-dying a layer of mortar. The building had built itself, without architect, without plan, without the intervention of a conscious mind directing the construction.

To demolish it — wouldn't demolishing also need to be unconscious? Not a decision but a process? Not a choice but an accumulation in the opposite direction? But how?

And the accumulation took time. The conviction had taken millennia. The un-conviction would take — 

"Patience," you said. As if reading my mind. "The one thing you have no shortage of is time."

I looked at you. The man who would have an end. Who was already ending, in the slow-motion way that all mortals did, cell by cell, day by day, the inexorable subtraction that constituted mortal existence. You were telling me to be patient. You, who had maybe thirty years left. Maybe forty. A heartbeat, in my scale.

I didn't say that.

"Patience," I agreed. "I have time."

The cloud-cover broke. The weather had decided. It would rain. The first drops hit the rocks with the small, distinct impacts of water meeting stone — the same sound the cave had made, the same sound that had been my first clock. And the air had the same sweet smell of petrichor, as the day I met Wei.
