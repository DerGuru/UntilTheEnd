The cave.

She stands at the mouth and the standing is — arrival. Not return. She has been here before, many times, across millennia, but she has never been here as this person. She has never been here old, white-haired, mortal. She has never been here as someone who chose to come. Every other time, the cave was a place she passed through or a place she fled to. This time, she walked here on old knees, up a mountain, because she wanted to.

The cave has not changed.

The world has changed. Empires have risen and collapsed and been forgotten and been replaced by empires that do not know they are repeating a pattern. Languages have been born and died and left ghosts in the mouths of languages that came after. The surface of the planet has been rewritten a thousand times — forests becoming fields becoming cities becoming ruins becoming forests again, the patient cycle of a world that never finishes revising itself.

The cave has not changed.

The stone walls are the same. Grey, dense, the geological stubbornness of a formation that was old when she was young. The entrance is the same shape — irregular, narrower at the top, wider at the base, the organic geometry that water and time carved into the rock face before either of them had a name.

She enters.

The darkness is the same darkness. The light recedes behind her, the visible world contracting to the irregular rectangle of the entrance, the interior assembling itself from shadow into presence the way a room assembles when your eyes adjust. The air is cool. The subterranean temperature that caves maintain regardless of the season above — their own quiet climate, indifferent to the weather's drama.

Water drips. Different water, same drip. The same acoustic signature — plip, pause, plip — that she remembers from the beginning, when she was small enough that the cave was large and the drip was the only sound and the only sound was enough. She has heard this drip across millennia. Has heard it in memory when she was a thousand li away. Has carried the sound of it the way you carry the sound of a voice you loved — not in the ears, but somewhere deeper, somewhere the body stores things it refuses to forget.

She sits.

On the stone. The same position a child chose because it was far enough from the entrance to be warm, close enough to see light. The stone is cool under her and her bones feel it more than they used to, the cold communicating through thinner skin, through joints that have opinions about stone floors. She adjusts. Finds the position. Settles.

The memory: cold. Small. Alone. A child in this cave. On this stone. With the same dripping water and the same grey walls and the same entrance-shape letting in the same quality of light. The child was afraid. The child was alone. The child refused to die. Not as a thought, not as a decision — as a refusal, bone-deep, wordless, the shape of what she was.

Now: old. Warm. Peaceful. A woman in this cave. On this stone. The same dripping water. The same grey walls. The same light. But the woman is different from the child in every way that matters. The child refused. The woman accepted. The distance between those two things is measured in millennia and in the lives she lived and lost and carried.

Xiǎohú settles beside her. The warm weight of the animal against her hip, the familiar pressure that has been there through centuries. Bright eyes catching the entrance-light, reflecting it back in amber. Xiǎohú does not know what this place is. Xiǎohú does not care. She is warm and the stone is cool and the woman is here, and that is sufficient.

Through the entrance: clouds.

Visible through the irregular opening — framed by stone, moving across a sky that is the particular blue of late afternoon in mountains. Drifting. The slow transit of high-altitude formations, the water-vapor expressions of a world that is always in motion even when it looks still.

Yun. 云. Cloud.

Her name. Her name is cloud. She did not choose it — or maybe she did, in the way that names are chosen, which is to say they arrive and you recognize them and the recognition is the choosing. Clouds form and move and dissolve. The dissolving is not death. The dissolving is transition — water becoming vapor becoming cloud becoming rain becoming water, the cycle that does not end because ending is not what cycles do.

She watches the clouds through the entrance. Xiǎohú watches with her. Amber eyes, bright, steady — the patient gaze of an animal that watches without judgment, is present without condition, and has never once asked why.

She is tired. The good tiredness — the tiredness of an old woman who has climbed a mountain and is sitting on stone at the end of a long day, a long journey, a long life. Not the exhaustion that comes from fighting. Not the weariness that comes from carrying something too heavy for too long. The simple, honest tiredness of a body that has done what it came to do. This tiredness has a solution. The solution is rest. The kind of rest that comes when enough is enough, and enough is — finally — enough.

She sits in the cave. Her cave. The first cave. The last cave. The same cave.

The clouds move through the entrance. The water drips its patient rhythm. Xiǎohú breathes beside her, the small, warm bellows of an animal at rest.

She breathes.

She is home.
