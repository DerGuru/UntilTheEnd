The memory came at night, while I sat by the fire close to sleep. We had finished the exercises. I had stopped searching deliberately through the past, leaving my attention to drift with the embers. In that undefended interval, something I had struggled to reach during the day became clear.

"I want to tell you something," I said.

You looked up with the pen already in your hand. It was always there when we sat together, ready for whatever I might find words for. I had come to depend on that. I could begin without asking you to prepare.

"About the beginning."

You waited. The fire was low, mostly embers, giving more heat than light. Its warmth reached my knees while your face grew less distinct on the other side.

I spoke slowly. During the first telling, I had struggled to keep pace with the memories. Now I chose each word before going on, giving the account a path you could follow. I was describing something I had never set out properly for anyone. Getting it right felt like a debt. I had lived inside a mistaken account for so long that I owed this one its proper telling, including the places where I was uncertain.

"I don't know exactly when," I said. "That's the first thing. I don't know the year or decade. Probably not even the millennium. But it was early. Very early. When I was still new."

New. I looked at my hands in the dim light, remembering when they had held so little history. There had been a time before the weight accumulated, when another year was something I could meet without carrying so many others into it.

"Everyone died."

Two words gave you the outcome. I let them stand, hearing how little they said about the time it had taken.

"Not at once. It wasn't a catastrophe. It was time. People around me aged, sickened, and died. One by one. The women of the tribe, the children I'd grown up with, and the people in the settlements I passed through later. Their bodies followed the same course. They all died."

Orange faded to gray on an ember. Heat still rose from under the ash. I waited for the next words.

"But I didn't."

The sentence sounded plain. Millennia fit into those few words without giving you any sense of their duration. Across the fire, your pen moved to another line.

"At first I thought I was lucky. Cuts healed fast. Falls didn't break anything. The things that should have killed me didn't. I thought I was just harder to hurt than most people. Some people were."

You wrote slowly, matching my pace. When I stopped, your pen stopped too.

"Then I thought I was different. Something other. Neither sick nor blessed nor cursed. Different. The word covered everything I couldn't explain, which made it useful for a long time."

My hands might have belonged to a woman of forty. I turned them toward the embers. They had rested harmlessly at my sides while things around me changed; looking at them still gave me little sense of what I had done.

"And then, still in my early years, when I was alone... I can't identify a moment. It was a kind of crystallization."

Crystallization came close. Something uncertain had become fixed without my choosing a point at which it should happen. I could remember waking again, finding myself unchanged, and expecting another morning to follow. Eventually I had stopped considering any other possibility.

"I stopped thinking 'I don't die' and started knowing 'I can't die.' The knowing became me."

The fire was nearly gone. Stars gave little light inside our small circle. Your face was barely visible, but your breathing kept you close as I spoke into the dark.

"The belief became real. The knowing became real. I believed with the whole of me, as a child believes. There was room for one conviction. After enough mornings, I could no longer separate it from myself. What began as a desperate hope became the truth I lived in. I couldn't bear to die, so I came to believe that I couldn't die. Eventually that belief made it true. I had built an answer to the fear before I understood what I was doing."

The fire died. The last ember went gray. The darkness was complete.

"I was afraid," I said. "That's the part I didn't— That's the part."

The mountain night was quiet: no insects at this altitude, no nocturnal birds. The wind had dropped. In that still air each breath between us carried as I spoke of something buried longer than whole civilizations had lasted.

"I was a child. In a cave. Alone. And I was afraid. Afraid of dying. Afraid of being alone. Afraid of the vulnerability of a small body in a large world. And the fear became the belief."

I remembered the cave's cold floor. The child curled there had needed to survive. I had built the protection from inside it, using fear until it became conviction. That protection had held through all the lives I lived afterward. Now I needed to take apart the very thing that had kept the child alive. It was as permanent as my belief in it.

Permanent until the belief changed.

I could no longer see you. I listened for the pen. It had stopped.

"Ran?"

"I'm here."

Your voice was different, quieter than it had been while you asked questions. I heard you draw breath before speaking. In the dark, that small change told me more than I could have read in your face.

We were silent for a long time, the silence that follows a flood of words.

"So you're telling me," you said, "that your immortality, whatever this power is, and your isolation all began with—"

"Fear. Yes."

"A child in a cave."

"Yes."

Your words brought me back to the first memory: cold stone around a small body, the cave beyond it empty. I had reached the same child from the other end of all those years. She had believed herself into survival. I was trying to relinquish what she began.

We sat in the dark after the fire died. You were nearby, and I no longer needed to see your face to go on. The truth had become simple enough to say.

I did not sleep that night. Cold reached the place where the fire had warmed my knees. I had looked under all the explanations I had given myself and found the thing I least wanted to find.

Fear.
