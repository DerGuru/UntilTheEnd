The beauty of the blooming plant and the rising drop had opened something. A window. A glimpse into a memory that was warm instead of sharp.

Wei.

A fire. Night. His face lit from below, the orange light catching the angles of a twelve-year-old who should have been asleep. The canteen was empty. The stream was too far. He had dirt on his face and the expression of a person assembling a new category in real time.

"How old?" he had asked.

"Very."

"Older than the forest?"

"Yes."

"Older than the mountain?"

"Yes."

He'd swallowed. Dry. The swallow of a boy who had just been told by a woman he trusted that she was older than the rock beneath him. And then — because he was Wei, because the essential, irreducible thing about Wei was this — he'd said:

"No wonder you're in such a bad mood all the time."

Wei's face by firelight. The way he looked at me when he'd decided something, his eyes direct, too direct, the gaze of a person who had never learned to look away because looking away implied a hierarchy and Wei did not do hierarchy.

And the warmth. The non-romantic, undefined warmth that existed between us in the spaces between words. He knew a little of what I was — not the full story, but he felt it in the sense of something too large to prove and too present to deny.

I had said yes. And his response had been — laughter. His. Mine, almost.

I opened my eyes. The clearing. The bowl, the plant and the extinguished candle. The mountain morning. You sitting across from me, pen poised, alert — a navigator reading weather in the shifts of my face. You looked expectantly at me.

I had let Wei in and I needed to protect the memory. The memory was too tender to share, too private, too fragile. Like a water drop that would fall if observed too closely.

I shook my head. Slowly. Almost undetectable, but you noticed anyway.

You set the pen down. Your hands rested open your knees, the posture of a man who understood that this moment did not belong to the notebook. Some data was not data. 

The warmth of the memory remained — a low heat, like embers, like the residual temperature of a fire that has been out long enough to stop being fire but not long enough to stop being warm. Wei was there. In the substrate of my memory, in the deepest layers, in the place where the oldest convictions lived alongside the latest losses.

The grief had changed.

I would never claim less. The grief had the same weight, the same fundamental quantity of loss. But the shape had changed. Now the grief was a warmth. The radiation of something that had existed intensely. Their existence continued to emit heat even after the source was gone.

I missed him.

"He would have liked this," I said.

You waited.

"The drop. The plant. He would have liked seeing it. Would have said something — stupid. Obvious. Would have tried to make the water move himself. Failed. Said something about it. Grinned."

My voice was steady. The steadiness of someone speaking about a loss that has been carried long enough to become part of the carrier rather than the load.

You nodded. You didn't pick up the pen.
