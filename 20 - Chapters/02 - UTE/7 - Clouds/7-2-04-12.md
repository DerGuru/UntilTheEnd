I sat looking at the plant after the drop had settled on its leaf. You were beside me with the notebook, waiting for what I would say next. I could almost hear the question Wei would have asked. It brought back another evening when he had stayed awake by our fire.

I remembered orange light on his dirt-streaked face. The canteen had been empty, the stream too far away. He should have been asleep, but he was looking at me with a question he had spent some time considering.

"How old?" he had asked.

"Very."

"Older than the forest?"

"Yes."

"Older than the mountain?"

"Yes."

He had swallowed with a dry mouth, his eyes on me while he took in the answer. Then he had said:

"No wonder you're in such a bad mood all the time."

I could still see his face by the fire. Once he had decided something, he looked straight at me, prepared to tell me what it meant. Being older than a mountain explained my temper. It had done little for his manners.

He had known enough of what I was to ask. He trusted my answers, though their size must have been hard for him to grasp. He had laughed at his own conclusion, waiting to see whether I would join him.

I had almost managed it.

I opened my eyes to the clearing. The bowl stood next to the plant and the unlit candle. You sat across from me, your pen poised, waiting for me to explain what had changed in my face.

I wanted to keep that evening to myself for a little longer. Describing it would mean choosing words while his face was still so clear.

I shook my head, a slow motion, barely visible, but you noticed anyway.

You set the pen down. Your hands rested open on your knees. You let me have the silence.

The morning air touched my face while I held on to his laughter. I remembered how close we had sat to the fire, how easily he had expected an answer from me. For once I could stay with that evening without immediately reaching the end of his life.

His absence still hurt. I looked at the plant, picturing him crouched before it with another impossible question. There was room now to remember what it had been like to have him near me, as well as what losing him had cost.

I missed him.

"He would have liked this," I said.

You waited.

"The drop. The plant. He would have liked seeing it. He would have said something stupid. Obvious. Then he would have tried to make the water move himself. He'd have failed, found something to say about it, and grinned."

My voice was steady.

You nodded. You didn't pick up the pen.
