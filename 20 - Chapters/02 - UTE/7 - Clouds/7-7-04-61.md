It is the second day of the week. She does not know why the day matters. It should not matter — it is market day, the day she goes to the stalls and lets Loudcumber tell her which vegetables she needs. Beyond that, the day has no particular significance. But the precision of it anchors the discovery in the calendar of ordinary life, and ordinary life is what she has now, so market day it is.

A basin of water stands on the wooden table, serving as mirror because mirrors are a luxury the house does not provide. She bends over it to wash her face. The water is cool. Her reflection ripples, steadies and looks back at her — the same face she has worn for millennia, the same arrangement of features that has been fixed since before anyone alive was born.

Except. There is a wrinkle. One. Left side, beside the eye. Small and fine, the crease that time produces when time is allowed to. The kind of line that a friend would call a laugh line, if she had friends and if she laughed.

She touches it and traces it with her fingertip. The skin is slightly different there — not rough, but textured, like a fold in a sheet of paper that has been smoothed flat but remembers the folding. A line on a map of a country she has never visited.

She straightens and looks at the face in the basin again. The wrinkle is — she has no word for it. Fascinating, maybe. The way a first snowfall is fascinating to someone who has only read about snow.

"So that's what it feels like," she says. To the face. To the basin. To the house that contains her, the wrinkle, and the morning.

The scratch comes later. That afternoon, in the garden, under the plum tree that is doing what plum trees do in this season — blossoming, the pink-and-white canopy that smells like something between sweet and almost. She is pulling weeds — the basic labor that gardening requires of everyone, immortal or otherwise — and a thorn catches the back of her hand. A thin line of red. Small. The kind of scratch that bodies collect from gardens and forget about by dinner.

Except she watches it. The thin red line stays. Beads, slightly. Does not close. For millennia, the qi would have sealed this before she noticed it — the automatic repair of a body that treated damage as a clerical error to be corrected immediately. Now the scratch stays open. It will scab. It will heal at the speed that scratches heal, which is the speed of days, not seconds. She watches the small red line the way you watch a clock whose hands have started moving after years of being stopped.

Everything is new. That is the extraordinary thing. After millennia of sameness — the same face, the same skin, the same body performing the same functions with the same tireless precision — the smallest change is revelation. A wrinkle. A scratch that stays. The modest evidence of mortality performing its work on a body that has been exempt for longer than most civilizations have existed.

A voice. From the fence. The neighbor, holding a jar of something pickled, the kind of jar that arrives without explanation because explanation would imply that generosity requires justification. She holds it over the fence the way one holds a verdict — arm extended, no negotiation.

"For you. You're too thin."

Yun takes the jar. The neighbor's eyes do what the neighbor's eyes always do. Assess. Quickly. Thoroughly. Without a trace of apology.

"You have wrinkles," the neighbor says.

"One."

"Wrinkles come from laughing."

"I don't laugh," she says.

The neighbor looks at her.

"You should start," the neighbor says. She turns to leave, then stops. "Come eat with me tomorrow. You look like you need a proper meal."

She leaves. No follow-up. No elaboration. The neighbor says what she means and then removes herself before the meaning can be diluted by discussion.

The scratch on her hand has begun to scab — a small dark line, the body's first draft of repair.

She goes back to the basin. Looks at the wrinkle again. Traces it again.

Something shifts around her mouth. Not a smile. The territory adjacent — the place where a smile would be if a smile decided to visit.

Tomorrow, perhaps.
