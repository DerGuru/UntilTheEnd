Morning. Early. Dew on the grass. Xu Ran is standing. The pack on his back — shouldered, strapped, settled. The weight of a pack put on for the last time in a direction.

The notebook is under his arm. The leather dark with age and the accumulated moisture of mornings like this one. Dew-mornings, early-mornings, the mornings that exist in the temperature range between night and day where water hangs on everything, the world wet with becoming.

Yun stands.

They face each other. The clearing between them is small. Three steps, two, the distance that has been their constant and is now their final.

"It's complete," he says.

"Nothing is complete."

"Close enough."

The phrase is his. His philosophy compressed into two words: the rejection of perfection in favor of adequacy, the pragmatic acknowledgment that done is more valuable than perfect and that the gap between them is habitable.

"I've written everything down," he says. "As well as I could. The story of a woman who has seen everything and still stands."

"That sounds depressing."

"That sounds human."

Reframing. His way of taking the shape she has given something and turning it until it is different and — truer. The distinction he insists on and that she is still learning to value.

He holds the notebook. Paper, leather, ink and thread. The material form that contains years of walking, talking and the excavation of a life too long for one volume — recorded in one anyway. Dog-eared. Stained. Some pages water-damaged. The creases at the corners speak of usage that exceeded design specifications, the structural fatigue of paper asked to hold more than paper was built to hold.

He is taking it with him. She doesn't ask to keep it.

The not-asking is — everything. The release. The act of letting go. The thing she is trying to learn.

"Where will you go?" she asks.

"Down."

"And then?"

"Somewhere that needs a story."

His profession. His identity now. The itinerant chronicler. The man with the pen.

"Thank you," she says.

The words are inadequate. The insufficiency of gratitude expressed in two syllables for something that requires — more. More syllables. More time. More of the language that can carry weight. But two syllables are what she has. What she offers. Close enough.

His phrase. Given back.

She steps forward. Closes the distance. Places her hand on his chest — over the core, the fractured meridian center that has been filling with crystallized qi. She feels it. Denser than before, the sediment layered and solidifying, the cracks narrower. Not complete yet, but almost.

"It's not finished yet," she says.

"I know."

"The rest you can do yourself. Cultivate normally. The channels are clear. It'll take longer that way, but it'll get there. Eventually. Give it a few years without my presence."

He looks down at her hand. Looks up. The expression is — she doesn't name it.

"I— ," he stops.

She removes her hand. Steps back. The three steps. The distance.

He nods. 

He turns.

He walks.

Steadily. The pace of a man who is leaving because leaving is correct and who is unhurried because hurrying would diminish the departure. The pack sits on his back. The notebook is under his arm. His feet find the trail. The downward trail, the direction away, the path that leads from this clearing to somewhere else.

She watches.

The clearing is still. The dew is present. The sun is arriving — the first edge of light touching the ridge, the golden line that separates night from day and past from future and together from alone.

He doesn't look back.

That is — right. Looking back would have been different. Would have been the gesture that converted departure into drama, that elevated leaving into loss, that turned a man walking away into a symbol of something being taken.

He is a man walking away. Walking toward something else. Walking because walking is what he does between the recording and the next recording, the ambulatory process that connects stories. Maybe later he'll even return to this mountain. Start cultivation and sect of his own?

The trail takes him behind the ridge. The pack. The notebook. The pen. The flat voice, the surgical humor, the three-step distance, the terrible cooking and the one night of good not-rice.

Behind the ridge. Gone.

The clearing is — empty. No. Not empty. Cleared. The difference between absence and presence-removed, between nothing and something that was and no longer is. 

Xiǎohú is beside her. Watching the ridge. Watching her. Watching the ridge again.

The wind moves the grass. Real wind. Mountain wind. The atmospheric movement that is weather and nothing more. The certainty of it, the knowing that the wind is wind, is new and fragile and hers.

*He took every word I gave him and made it ink. That was close enough.*

She stands in the clearing. Morning arriving, dew evaporating. Somewhere on the other side of the ridge, a man walks with a notebook that contains her life. She let him take it.

She lets him go. She lets everything go. And for the first time, that is not the same as losing.
