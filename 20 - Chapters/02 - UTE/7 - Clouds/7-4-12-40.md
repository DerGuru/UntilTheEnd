Dew covered the grass when you shouldered your pack. You adjusted the straps until the weight settled properly, then took the notebook under your arm. We had begun so many mornings with those same movements.

Damp darkened the worn leather. Water clung to everything before the sun reached us: the grass, the pack, and the cover you held against your side.

I stood to face you.

Three steps lay between us, then two as I found level ground. I had grown used to measuring the distance without needing to.

"It's complete," you said.

"Nothing is complete."

"Close enough."

I knew the phrase. You had used it over meals, over difficult pages, and whenever I demanded a certainty you could not provide. Close enough had brought us farther than perfection would have.

"I've written everything down," you said. "As well as I could. The story of a woman who has seen everything and still stands."

"That sounds depressing."

"That sounds human."

You could turn an account a few degrees until something truer showed in it.

The notebook rested against your side. Paper, leather, ink, and thread held years of walking together. I had told you a life too long for one volume; somehow you had recorded it anyway. Pages were dog-eared or water-stained, the creased corners marking where you had returned, trying to understand.

You were taking it with you. I had given you the words. I left the record under your arm instead of asking for it back.

"Where will you go?"

"Down."

"And then?"

"Somewhere that needs a story."

I looked at the hand holding the notebook close. You had found work you meant to continue past this mountain.

"Thank you," I said.

There was more I could have put into those words: more time, more of the language I had spent filling your pages. After telling you so much, two syllables were all I had left.

Close enough. I could give your phrase back.

I stepped forward to place my hand on your chest, over the broken core. Qi had crystallized more densely along the fracture. I followed the narrowing cracks to where the layers had settled since I first found them. The repair was almost complete.

"It's not finished yet," I said.

"I know."

"The rest you can do yourself. Cultivate normally. The channels are clear. It'll take longer that way, but it'll get there. Eventually. Give it a few years without my presence."

You looked down at my hand, then up at me.

"I—"

I took my hand away and stepped back, restoring the three paces between us.

You nodded.

Then you turned toward the trail.

You walked at your usual steady pace. The pack sat firmly on your shoulders, the notebook under your arm, and your feet found the downward path. You had chosen to leave; I could let you do it without asking you to hurry or to stay.

The first light touched the ridge. Dew shone on the grass between us, bright wherever the sun reached.

You continued without looking back.

I let that be right. I could watch a man leave for another part of his life without turning the departure into something taken from mine.

Perhaps you would return to this mountain one day. With your core repaired, you might cultivate here or even found a sect. You carried those possibilities down the trail.

At the bend the pack still showed. Then the ridge hid it.

I stayed where I was, remembering the dry voice across the fire, the questions I resisted, and the three steps you used to leave between us. The terrible cooking came back too. Once you had made good not-rice. I still had my suspicions about the other meals.

Your usual place by the fire was bare. The packed things had left a gap near the stones.

Xiǎohú stood next to me. She faced the ridge, glanced at me, and faced the trail again.

Wind moved the grass and touched my face. It was weather. That certainty was new enough to handle carefully. I had left the wind alone.

You had taken every word I gave you and made it ink. That was close enough.

Morning warmed the clearing, and the dew dried on the grass. Somewhere past the ridge, you were walking with the notebook that held my life. I had let you take it.

I stood next to the fox, my hands empty. I had let you go. For once I felt what was left instead of counting what I had lost.
