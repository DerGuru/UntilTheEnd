How do you unlearn swimming? It was an analogy to my predicament, the question of how to unlearn immortality when it had been the only thing I'd ever known. The conviction of immortality was like the muscle memory of swimming — a pattern that had been reinforced for so long that it was the default state of being, the way a body moves through water without conscious thought.

So in short. You can't. Not actively. Not by deciding to forget the coordination of arms and legs and breath that the body has encoded into its motor pathways. You can't sit on a shore and will yourself into forgetting buoyancy, can't concentrate your way out of the muscle patterns that keep you afloat. Swimming, once learned, lives in the body like language lives in the throat — too deep for conscious access, too integrated for surgical removal.

But you can stop practicing.

Stop getting in the water. Stop moving your arms in the remembered patterns. Stop reinforcing the pathways with repetition. And eventually the body begins to forget. Not like you forget a fact or a face. The swimming remains as potential, as dormant capability, as the ghost of a pattern that no longer runs because the conditions that triggered it no longer exist.

Millennia of practice.

That was the scale. Not years of swimming lessons. Not a childhood of summers at a lake. Millennia — eons — of reinforced conviction, every day a practice session, every survival a repetition, every moment of continued immortality another stroke in the water. My body had been swimming for so long that the swimming had become the body, the pattern was the structure, the muscle memory was the muscle itself.

How do you forget something your body has been doing since before your recallable memory began?

*A brick at a time.*

Morning. Fire already lit. Rice from the dwindling supply. Enough for perhaps a week if rationed, supplemented with the bitter greens that mountain terrain offered to those willing to eat them. Soon we might have to hunt. Above the clearing, clouds stacked in layers — high cirrus over low stratus, the sky layered into strata while we organized ourselves into routine and went back to yesterday's conversation.

"The hesitation, you described yesterday," you continued. "After you decided that your finger could bleed. That was a brick. Or some losened mortar."

"A lose brick."

"That's a start."

You were doing your thing — the pragmatic dissection, the scholar's approach to immortal despair. Breaking the unbearable into its components. Reducing the existential to the operational. Making the impossible feel like a task list.

"Not 'I am mortal,'" you said. "That's the building. You already start with: 'This finger could bleed.' When you manage that, monve on to: 'This hand could hurt.' followed by 'This body could tire.' Each one a brick. Each brick a claim against the certainty. You don't demolish — you erode."

"That could take—"

"How long did building the conviction take?"

I didn't answer. The answer was: almost my entire duration of existence. The building had taken everything. Every moment. The conviction wasn't a thing I had finished building at some point — it was the accumulation of every moment of being alive. Layers upon layers. Stone upon stone. To erode that — even brick by brick — was the work of a lifetime that had no defined length.

"I know," you said. Reading the calculation on my face. "It's impossible. On any reasonable timeline."

"Then—"

"Reasonable timelines are for reasonable people." The micro-expression. The almost-smile. The flash of something that might have been warmth in a face that was calibrated for measurement. "You're not reasonable. At least your timeline isn't. So maybe the impossible is possible for you. Maybe the impossible is just a longer timeline than anyone else has to work with."

I looked down. I could make leaves float and water rise and flowers bloom. I could reshape every physical law the universe had established. I could change everything except myself.

"Maybe you're too close," you said.

"To what?"

"To yourself. You're trying to use your own tool on your own tool. The eye can't see itself."

The eye can't see itself. One needs a mirror. But these last months had been mirrors — the season of mirrors, the time when every reflective surface had shown me something true. I had looked in the mirror. I had seen myself. I knew the face.

But seeing the face was not changing the face.

"What would be a mirror?" I asked.

You shrugged. The shrug of a man who had pushed his logic to its boundary and reached the place where logic surrendered to intuition, where the scholar's framework gave way to the unmapped territory that only the subject could navigate.

"Something you can see yourself in. Something that shows you what you look like from outside. Something that isn't you — but reflects you."

You picked up your pen. Wrote something. Your notebook was your mirror — the place where you saw yourself reflected in the act of documenting another. Whatever you wrote was between you and the paper.

I sat with the question. What mirror could show an immortal what mortality looked like? What surface could reflect a face that had been unchanged for eons and show it — changed?

Xiǎohú approached. Sat near my knee. The amber eyes looked up at me with the unreadable attention of an animal that had no stakes in human problems but was willing to sit near them.

I looked into her eyes.

Amber. Clear. The reflection of my face — small, inverted, contained in two copper discs. My face, as seen by a fox, as seen in a fox's eyes.

No answer came. The mirror reflected faithfully. I was who I was. The face was unchanged. The conviction was unbroken.

But the question was better now.
