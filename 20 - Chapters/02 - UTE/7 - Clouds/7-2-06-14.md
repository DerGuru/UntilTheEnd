By morning the fire was lit again. You measured rice from our dwindling supply while I watched the pot. With care, the bag would last another seven days, with bitter mountain greens to stretch it. Soon we would have to hunt.

The water brought swimming to mind. Once you had learned to keep yourself afloat, how would you unlearn it? You could sit on a shore for a day, concentrating on forgetting, yet your arms would find their old movement as soon as you entered deep water.

A bubble broke the surface of the pot. Swimming belonged to the body as much as to memory. A person could fail to explain the stroke and still make it perfectly. I could no more reach into that habit by thought than into the certainty keeping me alive.

But a swimmer could always stay ashore. Days would pass without a single stroke, and the skill would grow harder to find when it was needed.

I sat by the fire and followed that possibility as far as it went. Every day I survived was another repetition. Millennia had reinforced the habit I wanted to loosen. Even as I considered giving it up, my breath came easily. The body I was trying to change went on doing the thing that had formed it.

Your suggestion from yesterday gave me a place to begin: a brick at a time. I looked at the finger where the qi had hesitated. Its skin looked as ordinary this morning as it had on the trail.

Above the clearing, thin high clouds crossed a low gray layer. You finished measuring the rice. We returned to yesterday's conversation while it cooked.

"The hesitation you described yesterday," you continued. "After you decided that your finger could bleed. That was a brick. Or some loosened mortar."

"A loose brick."

"That's a start."

You looked at the same finger I had been examining. Faced with a problem I could scarcely bear to consider whole, you always found a smaller part to work on. It made another morning's practice possible.

"Not 'I am mortal,'" you said. "That's the building. You already started with 'This finger could bleed.' When you manage that, move on to 'This hand could hurt.' Then 'This body could tire.' Each one a brick. Each brick loosening the certainty. You erode it."

"That could take—"

"How long did building the conviction take?"

I looked at my hand. Nearly my entire existence lay behind that certainty. I could find no point where I had finished building it; each day of survival had added to it. Even now, by a cooking fire, I was adding another morning. Working backward through that pile would take longer than I could imagine.

"I know," you said, watching my face. "It's impossible. In any reasonable span of time."

"Then—"

"Reasonable spans of time are for reasonable people." The corner of your mouth lifted. "You're not reasonable. At least your lifespan isn't. So maybe the impossible is possible for you. Maybe it simply takes longer than anyone else has."

I looked down. I could make leaves float, water rise, and flowers bloom. I had changed things I once accepted as laws of the world. My own hand rested on my knee, as unchanged as ever.

"Maybe you're too close," you said.

"To what?"

"To yourself. You're trying to use your own tool on your own tool. The eye can't see itself."

The eye needed a mirror. These last months had handed me so many that I scarcely knew which revelation to consider first. I saw myself more clearly than before. The water in the pot gave back a pale reflection of my face as I leaned forward. The knowledge had changed little.

"What would be a mirror?" I asked.

You shrugged, your hands opening briefly above your knees. You had followed the argument as far as you could. I would have to recognize whatever came next for myself.

"Something you can see yourself in. Something that shows you what you look like from outside. Something that isn't you, but reflects you."

You picked up your pen to write. I wondered whether the notebook served that purpose for you, whether setting down what you saw in me let you see something of yourself. You angled the page toward your knee. Whatever you wrote was yours.

I sat with the question while the rice cooked. I needed a reflection in which I could recognize mortality as my own possibility. I had looked at this unchanged face for so long that imagining another version of it felt dishonest.

Xiǎohú came to sit near my knee and turned her amber eyes up to me. I looked down into them.

My face lay small in the clear amber. I saw myself there from outside, held in the eyes of a fox. She tilted her head, and the reflection shifted.

I didn't move. The sight gave me no answer, yet I looked longer than I had expected to. At least I knew more clearly what I was searching for.
