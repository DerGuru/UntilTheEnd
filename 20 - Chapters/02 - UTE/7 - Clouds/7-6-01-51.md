The village is twenty houses, a well, a market that operates on twice a week, a rhythm that has been running longer than any of its inhabitants can remember.

Yun finds a house. Small. One room. A stove and a bed. The garden behind the house is overgrown, the weeds thick in every direction, vegetation that has not been cut in years.

The landlord has as many houses as teeth. Three of each. He stands in the doorway, names a price and she pays it — coins, the residue of Xu Ran's haggle with the herbs she had shown him. He counts the money with his fingers and his gums. Then leaves.

She sweeps.

The broom is stiff and bristles with a wooden handle. She sweeps the floor. Dust rises and she coughs. The cough is — real. The physical response that the qi would normally suppress and is — letting through. Partially. Enough dust gets through to make her cough.

She sweeps anyway.

The dust settles on new surfaces. She sweeps those surfaces, the dust rises, settles again, and the cycle is the Sisyphean domesticity of floor-cleaning, the perpetual negotiation between clean and not-clean that every person who has ever held a broom understands.

Wei's voice.

It arrives during the sweeping — uninvited, the memory that surfaces when the mind is occupied with something too mechanical to require full attention. His voice, clear, flat and unimpressed with everything including the concept it is expressing:

"Then stop being afraid."

She holds the broom still.

The Wei compression that reduced complex problems to simple instructions, the rhetorical strategy of a boy who had no patience for nuance and no respect for complexity. *Stop being afraid.* As though fear were a coat that could be removed. As though being afraid were a choice that could be unchosen. 

Simple. Direct. Unimpressed.

So Wei. But Wei had never said that. 

She stands in the middle of the dusty room with the broom motionless in her hands and Wei's voice fading back to wherever Wei's voice lives when it is not being useful. The dust is settling. The light through the window is the late afternoon's light, gold and angular, cutting across the floor in the geometric pattern that windows produce when the sun is low enough to enter almost horizontally.

She sweeps. Because what else should she do?

Outside, the village is doing what villages do. Roosters. Children. The auditory wallpaper of communal human life — voices at varying intensities, footsteps on packed earth, the creak of a cart on the main path. The sound of twenty households existing simultaneously, each producing its own acoustic contribution to the collective composition.

She sweeps the dust out the door. Into the afternoon. Into the light. Into the village that is living around her with the indifferent vitality of a community that does not know what she is and would not care if it did.

Xiǎohú is on the bed. Already. The territorial claim of an animal that identifies comfortable surfaces and occupies them with the absolute certainty that the occupation is justified. Watching the sweeping. Unimpressed.

Everyone is unimpressed with her.

She continues sweeping. The floor emerges — wooden, worn, the patina of boards that have been walked on for decades, each plank carrying the traffic patterns of previous inhabitants in the grain-deep tracks of footsteps, furniture legs and the general evidence of lived life.

This is either the last place or the next place and the distinction is a question she does not need to answer yet.

She sweeps.
