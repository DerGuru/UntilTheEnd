Twenty houses stand around a well. The market meets twice in every seven days, an old rhythm evident in the worn ground between the buildings. Yun follows the path past them, looking for somewhere to stay.

Yun finds a house. Small. One room. A stove and a bed. The garden behind it is overgrown, the weeds thick in every direction, uncut for years.

The landlord has as many houses as teeth. Three of each. He stands in the doorway to name a price. She pays it with coins left from Xu Ran's bargaining over the herbs she had shown him. He counts the money with his fingers and his gums, then leaves.

She sweeps.

The broom has stiff bristles on a wooden handle. Dust rises. She coughs, her throat scratching where qi would usually keep the dust away. The protection lets enough through for her body to respond. She waits until she can draw a clear breath.

She sweeps anyway.

Dust settles on new surfaces. She sweeps those next, raising another cloud that settles farther along. Each clean patch requires making another patch dirty first.

Wei's voice.

The sweeping brings it to mind, flat and unimpressed, while her hands go on with the movement. The advice arrives with the same impatience he brought to most complicated things.

"Then stop being afraid."

She holds the broom still.

He had always reduced complicated problems to instructions she could scarcely object to without sounding foolish. Stop being afraid. He might have told her to take off a coat she no longer needed.

Simple. Direct. Unimpressed.

So Wei. But Wei had never said that. 

The imagined voice fades. The broom rests motionless in her hands. Late-afternoon light enters almost horizontally through the window, laying a gold shape across the floor. Dust settles through it while she weighs what he said against what she still hears him saying.

She sweeps. Because what else should she do?

Outside, children call over the noise of roosters. Footsteps pass on packed earth; a cart creaks along the main path. Twenty households go about their day, the sounds overlapping, none of them the whole of it.

She sweeps the dust out through the doorway into afternoon light. The people outside continue their conversations. None shows much interest in the woman taking possession of the small house.

Xiǎohú is already on the bed. She has settled into its softer surface while Yun works with the broom. Her eyes follow the bristles across the floor, then half-close.

Everyone is unimpressed with her.

The wooden floor emerges under the dust. Decades of use have worn paths across the boards: tracks left by feet, marks from furniture legs, darker places that saw less traffic. Other people lived in this room before her.

This is either the last place or the next, and the distinction is a question she does not need to answer yet.

She sweeps.
