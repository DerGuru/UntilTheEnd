Morning light reached the notebook next to you. The last character was unfinished. Your pen lay on the ground with ink dried on its tip, where you had left it during the night. I looked from the interrupted stroke to your hands.

You were looking at me.

I had spent enough time under your observation to recognize the difference. Usually that attention preceded another question. This morning it came from someone who had spent the night listening.

Sometime during the story you had set the work aside. The page showed where it stopped. You had not returned to it.

"Everything started with fear," you said.

"Yes."

Your nod was smaller than usual. Your gaze dropped to the pen. I waited for you to pick it up, to find the next useful question as you so often did.

You picked up the pen. You looked at it. You set it down again.

"I need—" you started.

"What?"

"Time. To understand what you said."

This was new. You usually had a question ready before I finished answering the last one. Now you needed time. I let my hands rest in my lap, giving you the wait you had given me.

The stream ran next to us. Birds had begun calling closer again, one falling quiet before another answered. I listened to the spaces between their calls while you thought.

"A child," you said after a while. "You as a child built this."

"Not built. Believed."

"Is there a difference?"

"For me? No."

The morning passed with the notebook open on the ground. The unfinished stroke was visible whenever I looked toward your knee. Usually you would have completed it before attending to anything else. This time you left it there.

You built a fire to cook over. Your hands knew the sequence even while your attention was elsewhere. Water went into the pot. You measured the grain and waited for the heat, a task that needed no answer to anything we had discussed.

The not-rice was tolerable. We ate with the notebook next to you. Today even you ate without tasting, pausing with the next mouthful still on your spoon.

"You were afraid to die," you said between bites. "So you believed yourself immortal. To protect yourself from the fear."

"But that belief imprisoned me."

"Both can be true."

"True."

I held the bowl, weighing both truths. The belief had protected me more effectively than anything else could have. I was still living inside that protection, unable to leave. The child had done what she could with what she had. Her answer had been sufficient to keep her alive, catastrophic in how long it lasted.

"And now you want to stop believing."

"Yes. I need to unbelieve what I believed. Dismantle the conviction. Take apart the—"

"No." The word was quiet. Final. "You need to stop being afraid."

"Why is that?"

"You told me the fear of death produced the belief. Not the other way around. The belief is the structure, but fear is the foundation. Remove the foundation, and the structure has no support. Then it can be dismantled. Then it can change. It can dissolve."

"But I don't want to be immortal anymore," I said.

"Wanting to be mortal isn't the same as not fearing death." You looked at the fire. "You can want something and still be terrified of its consequences. The wanting is here." You touched your forehead. "The fear is deeper."

The distinction was correct. I wanted mortality as a prisoner wanted freedom, with the urgency of long confinement. The wanting sat in my chest while I held the bowl. So did the memory of how quickly I tightened against any opening. The place outside was still frightening.

"So I need to stop holding the fear."

"Stop reinforcing it. Stop feeding it. Let it dissolve."

Dissolve. Like clouds.

I looked up at the clouds. An edge thinned into blue while another patch gathered farther along the ridge. A shape lost its outline without anything tearing it apart.

Could a belief dissolve?

Perhaps a conviction held for millennia could lose its shape as the conditions maintaining it eased. I had spent every morning trying to take it apart. The edge of the cloud faded, and it looked nothing like what I had been doing.

Xiǎohú pressed against my leg. Her weight was warm through my clothes. I left my leg where it was; she settled closer. We sat like that while the light shifted toward afternoon and the stream ran on.

You reached for the notebook to turn to a fresh page. You readied the pen, then looked at me. I recognized that posture.

"Tell me," you said.

I looked at you.

"The valley," you said. "Where I was attempting my breakthrough. Before you arrived. What happened there?"

I could give you the events in the valley. Explaining what I had brought there would take much longer. I looked at the fresh page, thinking of the millennia I had never told anyone about.

"And after," you continued. "Until now. I want to know everything. Especially your inner thoughts."

You waited with the pen above the empty page. I looked at the space where the first line would go. After so much preparation, I still had to choose where to begin.

I opened my mouth. I closed it. I opened it again.

I started. 

"The forest smelled like rain that hadn't fallen yet."
