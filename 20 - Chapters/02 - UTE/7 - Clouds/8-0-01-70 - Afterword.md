## How did this story come to be?
Third time is the charm. Or as we say in German: Aller guten Dinge sind drei.

If you read one or both of my previous books, I already told you about my process. Nevertheless, I am still learning. Every book project teaches me something I did not know before.

This time I wanted to try something ambitious. A series. Seven books. Seven legs of a journey. Each book with its own structure, its own themes, its own tone. But all connected. All part of the same story held together by one single character — an OP MC. 

I need a structure I can follow, so I used the Seven Point Structure by Dan Wells — hence seven books. I call the whole thing the journey. Each book is a step on that journey - the legs. Each book is itself structured in seven parts — the segments. Each part has one or more chapters, depending on how many scenes it takes to carry its purpose.

Now I had a structure and a trope I wanted to try. How to make a story out of it?

I began by asking myself questions. 

### How does one become absolutely OP? 
The closest answer: With time. So the MC was old - OLD - very old. Stoneage old. Which meant, the MC needed to be immortal.

### What does immortality do to a person? 
I found inspiration in one of my favorite movies "The man from Earth" — really good movie, by the way. I highly recommend it. The MC there is also immortal and roughly 14000 years old. But it wasn't the first time I thought about eternity. My grandfather was a priest, so I grew up with the idea of eternal life after death. And I never liked it. I follow the idea of living in the now. And even though I am far from suicidal, I want my life to end at some point. Maybe that is the reason I don't cry at funerals. They had their lives. They don't hurt anymore. They were loved and are missed now. I never felt sad about their death, even though I still miss and love my grandparents. I always thought, that feeling sad would mean, that they suffer now, but they don't. They are just gone. Nowhere I could follow. And that is fine.

I had thought about eternity a lot during my teens and early twenties. 

Sure it will be fun at the beginning. Nothing to worry about. Nothing can harm you. You have all the time in the world to explore, to learn, to experience. But then what? What happens when you have done everything? Seen everything? Experienced everything? And when everything repeats itself over and over, again and again? Those who ignore history are doomed to repeat it. And those who live forever are doomed to go through it repeatedly — forever. Every attachment you form will be ripped away from you. Either by time or violence. Every person you care about will die. And if you are the only one moving on, you are effectively alone.

I think, that this will break almost everyone at some point. If you're not a buddhist monk, your views on life and death will change. Maybe even then. Nothing will matter anymore. Not things and not people. Nothing. You will go numb and become indifferent. You will lose your empathy, your compassion, your purpose. You will lose your humanity. You will become a monster. And either you get stuck there or you find a way out of it. 

And after all of this, Yun starts her journey on her way out, when she has almost done it. 

There is the start. 

### What would be the goal of someone who had lived through all of that? 
For me there is only one answer: To end it. Finally. Becoming mortal. And there is our ending. Everything in between is the story. Yun's final journey. 

### What kind of story would that be?
At first I wanted an action comedy as LitRPG but over the course of my thinking I realized that the story I was about to tell was not really a LitRPG story. And far away from comedy. It was more of an introspective cultivation story. So I went with that. 

There were more questions which I won't bore you with. But the point is: I had a concept and a structure and a trope. And I had a lot of questions. And I had to answer those questions to get to the story.

### The Process
My process looked roughly like this: First a concept. Then a general outline for the journey. Then a finer outline for each leg or book. Then the segment outlines. And finally  scene outlines for the chapters.

Since I am a developer I am used to iterative processes. So each step was iterated until I was satisfied with the current result. I even went back and forth between the different levels of structure, when I had new ideas for the story. Letting them enter on top and going through the motions again. Like with the LitRPG idea and a system.

As a result, the outline was done.

I cannot stress this enough. THIS IS THE STORY. And if you do it the same way, this would be your story. The AI is just a tool. It is not a real co-author. It does not create the story - I did. It does not have real ideas, it presents just options, that are feasible but not always good for the story. It does not have real creativity. It does not have imagination, even if it simulates it quite well. It is a machine that processes text based on patterns it has learned from the data it was trained on. 

And without close guidance it is not even good at writing any story.

So in order to have a good result your story must be complete and ready. You must know everything and more. And **you need to tell it to the AI**. All of it.
- your characters. 
- your plot. 
- your world. 
- your themes. 
- your tone. 
- your style. 
- your rules. 
- your structure. 
- your pacing. 
- your scenes. 
- your dialogue. 
- your descriptions. 
- your emotions. 
- your conflicts. 
- your resolutions.

EVERYTHING! 

Telling the AI is the important part, because if you don't tell it, it will make up something based on the patterns it has learned. And even if that something might be good, it might not be what you want. And usually it is empty and meaningless. So you need to tell the AI what exactly you want. 

There was a rumor once, that AI will make us developers obsolete. After my experiences in writing, I can answer that with a definite **No**. AI will not make us obsolete. It will make us more efficient. It will let us focus on the creative part. But it will not replace us. Sometimes it felt like I was programming the AI to write these books. And in a way I was. But in doing that, I was also writing the book. The AI was just a tool that helped me to write faster — and most likely better than I would have on my own.

## What I learned or did better this time

**Read your outlines**. Correct them. They are your map. They are your compass. They hold the structure together. If they are wrong, everything built on them will be wrong. Question them until you are satisfied. The better the foundation, the better the house.

**Do not tell the AI a single tone**. I made that mistake. Telling it to write with dread gives you text that drowns in dread. Telling it to write hollow and disconnected gives you the first chapters of Echoes — which were hard to read and harder to fix. You only know the edited versions — I know their original state. So keep the tone mixed. The mixture is what makes prose readable. 

**Have your writing rules ready**. Have your StyleDNA prepared before you start and update it after each book. The more specific you are, the tighter the leash, the better the result. Without that leash the AI drifts. It writes things that sound impressive and mean nothing. Or not even impressive.

**Do not set a word count target**. I learned this the hard way. A fixed number hollows out the prose. It fills pages with repetition and empty phrases. The scene is the unit. The scene moves the story, develops the characters, transports the reader. Word count is a byproduct. Focus on the scene. The rest follows. Then the books are shorter — so what? The story is what matters. Not the number of words.

I said don't set a word count target. This afterword is already longer than most of my chapters. Point proven, I suppose.
