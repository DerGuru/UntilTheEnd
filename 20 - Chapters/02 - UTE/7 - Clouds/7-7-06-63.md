Leaves fall from the plum tree one at a time, then several together, until the ground under it is covered. Yun watches green turn yellow, then brown. Fallen leaves soften into the soil. She has seen thousands of autumns in forests that no longer exist, on mountains worn down to hills. This time she watches knowing that the season reaches her too.

Rain.

Yun is in the garden when the rain starts. The first few drops give little warning before water falls heavily across the beds.

She gets wet.

Her clothing darkens. Hair flattens against her skull and each drop strikes the skin beneath it. Qi had sheltered her for millennia, turning the rain aside as an awning does at a doorway. She had nearly forgotten how thoroughly weather could reach a body. Now she stands inside it, getting wet with the garden.

Water runs along her nose, then drips from her chin. Each drop is cold enough to distinguish from the next. She stays in the garden, enjoying it. Wet fabric rests heavily against her arms. A movement of her head sends water from her hair onto her neck. The rain lands one way on skin, another in cloth, and runs off her in a third.

A voice reaches her from next door. The neighbor stands dry in her doorway with her arms crossed. She frowns at Yun as though coming in from the rain were a skill she should already possess.

"Get in. You'll get sick!"

She could get sick. She has outlasted plagues, fevers, and passing colds for longer than people have kept records of them. Now a neighbor can warn her about standing in wet clothes. The possibility is absurd enough to make her smile in the downpour.

She goes inside, leaving drops across the floor. Dry clothes feel good against her skin. She changes slowly, tending a body that has stood outside too long.

She bends over the basin. More wrinkles mark the face looking back, though she has stopped counting them. This time she notices gray strands at her temples, a few more along the part. She touches one. Its texture is unchanged, its weight the same. Color is leaving so gradually that she has to look twice to see how much has gone. She has watched people leave a gathering one by one, barely noticing until the room was half-empty. This departure happened while she was busy with meals and the garden. She turns her head to catch the other temple in the light.

She returns to the door to watch the rain ease. Once it stops, she walks into the garden.

"Hi," comes a voice. Small.

The Counter stands at the fence, brown-haired and serious. She counts stones, steps, and anything else she can assign a number to. Afternoon light catches her from behind, making a halo she would count if she could see it. Her attention is on Yun's face. She examines it with the same care she gives the stones along the lane.

"Seven."

"Seven what?" Yun says.

"Your wrinkles," the girl says. She turns away to count the fence posts as she goes.

Yun smiles. How many times has she smiled since arriving? She has not kept count. The Counter might have an answer; the Counter has an answer for nearly everything.

But some things are better left uncounted.

That evening the village celebrates the harvest. The festival fills three streets. Handmade lanterns hang from fence posts and the tree where the paths meet. Some are lopsided. Their orange light moves unevenly over the people under them, warm enough to make most faces look kinder than usual.

The neighbor brings dense, sweet mooncakes. Yun eats one by the well, lotus paste sticking to her teeth. She has eaten them in a thousand autumns, in a thousand villages. The texture is the same as ever, but hunger makes it comforting in a way she barely remembers. Her body has room for this food. She takes her time over the mouthful, letting the sweetness linger under the moving lanterns. Around the well, people go on with the small conversations that keep a festival going after its first lights.

The Counter announces that there are fourteen lanterns. She has checked them all. Yun looks along the three streets, accepting the number without needing to verify it.

Orange light falls across Yun's seven wrinkles. They catch it as laugh lines do. She has yet to laugh often enough to earn that explanation, but the evening gives her somewhere to begin.

Winter.

Cold enters through the gaps around the shutters. It creeps under the door, requiring more blankets and a daily fire. She kneels with kindling in her hands each morning. When the flame catches on the first try, the satisfaction is particular: warmth she made herself. The room changes as the fire takes. She stays near it until the chill leaves her fingers, then reaches for the tea.

She drinks hot tea while Xiǎohú curls by the fire. The fox's eyes are closed. Her flank rises steadily in the warmth, paws tucked under her.

Wrinkles. Gray hair. She has learned hunger, rain-soaked clothing, and the cold of winter. Now she drinks tea beside a fire she built herself.

A life.
