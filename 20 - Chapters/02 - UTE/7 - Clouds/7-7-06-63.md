The plum tree drops its leaves. One at a time at first, then in small delegations, then in the resigned mass departure that autumn demands of deciduous things. The green becomes yellow becomes brown becomes mulch, and she watches the transformation with an attention she has never given autumn before. She has seen thousands of autumns. Watched the leaves turn in forests that no longer exist, on mountains that have worn down to hills. But she never watched the leaves fall with the understanding that seasons now apply to her too.

Rain.

Yun is in the garden when it starts — one of those mountain rains that doesn't build, but arrives with the full commitment of a sky that has been holding its water for too long. The clouds open and everything underneath gets wet.

She gets wet.

Her clothing darkens, her hair flattens against her skull, and her skin receives the individual impacts of a thousand drops arriving with the careless force of water that does not know it is falling on someone who has never been rained on before. Not really. Not like this. For millennia, the qi deflected it — kept her dry the way an awning keeps a doorway dry, so automatically that she forgot rain was something that happened *to* people rather than *around* them.

She stands in the garden in the rain. The water runs down her face. Along her nose. Drips from her chin. Each drop is a separate event, a small cold announcement. She stands there enjoying the sensation.

A voice. From next door. The neighbor, standing in her doorway, dry, arms crossed, the position of a person who has had the basic sense to come in from the rain and cannot believe that this requires explanation.

"Get in. You'll get sick!"

The irony. She — getting sick. She, who has been immune to every pathogen, plague, and passing cold for longer than disease has had a name. Getting sick. From rain. The possibility is so absurd and so wonderful that she almost smiles, standing there in the downpour like a woman who has forgotten how umbrellas work.

She goes inside. Dripping. The floor receives her water without complaint. She changes into dry clothes and the sensation of dry fabric against wet skin is — good. Simple and good. The comfort of a body being taken care of by the person living in it.

She bends over the basin and the face that looks back has changed again. The wrinkles have multiplied — she has stopped counting — but it is the hair she notices. Grey. Not all of it, not yet. Strands at the temples, a few along the part, the particular in-between shade of pigment giving way. The color of transition. She touches a strand and it feels the same — the same texture, the same weight. Only the color has left, the way guests leave a party one at a time until you look up and the room is half-empty.

She returns to the door and watches, as the rain stops. She walks into the garden. 

"Hi," comes a voice. Small.

She turns. The Counter stands at the garden fence — the girls who counts stones, counts steps, counts everything that can be enumerated and treats the enumeration as sacred duty. Small, brown-haired, face serious with the gravity of important work. The afternoon light catches her from behind and gives her a halo she would probably count if she could see it. The child is studying her face with the analytical intensity she applies to all counted things — the same focus she gives to stones and steps and lanterns.

"Seven."

"Seven what?" Yun says.

"Your wrinkles." The girl says and turns away, counting the the fence posts as she goes. 

Yun smiles. Small and real. The kind that arrives without permission and stays without asking. How many smiles, since she arrived at the village? She does not know, the Counter would. The Counter counts everything. 

But some things are better left uncounted.

This evening, the village celebrates the harvest with a festival. Is is small. Three streets. Paper lanterns hang from the fence posts and the tree at the intersection where paths meet — handmade, lopsided, the uneven craft of village hands that produce beauty through effort rather than precision. The light they give is orange and unsteady but warm. The kind of light that makes faces look kinder than they are.

The neighbor brings mooncakes. Dense, sweet, the compressed pastry that the season demands. She eats one standing by the well, the lotus paste sticking to her teeth, the texture unfamiliar and comforting in the way that unfamiliar things become comforting when they arrive at the right time. She has eaten mooncakes before. In a thousand autumns, in a thousand villages. But she has never tasted one with a body that was hungry for it.

The Counter counts the lanterns. Fourteen. She announces this to no one in particular. Fourteen lanterns. The village's entire luminous inventory, cataloged and confirmed.

Yun stands in the orange light. It falls on her face, the face with seven wrinkles. They catch the light as laugh lines do, even though she did not earn them through laughing. Not yet.

Winter.

It gets cold. Real cold. The kind that gets into the house through the gaps in the shutters and the crack under the door. The kind that requires blankets and fire. She builds fires. Daily. The domestic ritual of keeping warm, the ancient partnership between humans and flame that she now participates in, kneeling on the floor with kindling in her hands and the particular satisfaction of a fire that catches on the first try.

She drinks hot tea. Xiǎohú curls by the fire. Amber eyes closed. The total relaxation of an animal in a warm place — body surrendered to the heat with the complete trust of a creature that has decided warmth is trustworthy and does not need to be monitored.

Wrinkles. Grey hair. A woman who sweats and hungers and gets wet in rain and gets cold in winter and drinks hot tea by a fire she built herself.

A life.
