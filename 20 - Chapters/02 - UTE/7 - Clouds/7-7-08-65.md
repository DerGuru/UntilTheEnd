Her white hair shows clearly in the basin. It has passed through the gray of transition into the color of paper, of clouds. She studies the bones under the changed surface of her face. The proportions are still recognizable. Lines have spread everywhere else, accumulating over years without asking for her attention.

Her hands tremble when she lifts her teacup. They once caught arrows in flight. Now the tea shivers with her grip. She drinks it anyway. Making peace with that tremor is one of her more impressive accomplishments.

A voice from the fence. Older than she remembers it.

"You got old."

A smile forms on Yun's lips. The Counter has grown since the child who counted stones first came to her window. Her face is longer, her voice lower. Twelve, perhaps fourteen now, with the half-finished proportions of a girl still growing. She has kept the counting through all those changes.

"Yes," Yun says. "Finally."

"Your wrinkles," the Counter says. She studies Yun's face with the same serious attention she gives everything she counts, though she can look at it from a greater height now.

"How many are there?" Yun asks.

"Too many to count."

From the Counter, of all people. The girl who counts everything has reached a number she is prepared to leave unfinished. Yun watches her keep looking at the lines. Something close to wonder crosses that serious face, as if the uncounted had finally offered her something she likes.

Yun smiles. The smile adds another wrinkle, probably. She is past caring.

"Come in," she says. "I'm cooking."

The Counter sits at the table. Yun follows her glance from two chairs to the window, then to three candles, two of them unlit. Her lips form silent numbers. The counting is as natural to her as breathing. She has brought it into the house along with her longer limbs and lower voice. Years have changed so much about the girl without taking away that small, constant occupation.

Yun cooks good rice. Years of burned pots have taught her how much water to use, how high to keep the heat, and when to move the pot. She has eaten the results of enough failures to remember them. Now the grains fall apart light and separate under the spoon.

She serves equal portions into two bowls. Xu Ran did this beside the mountain fire, dividing what he cooked between them each evening. She has made the same arrangement without planning to. She sets the bowls where both can be reached.

A knock at the door.

The neighbor fills the doorway, short and broad. She lifts her face toward the smell from the stove before looking at Yun. Food has always given her an excellent reason to appear.

"You're cooking?"

"Yes. Please come in and share some with us."

The neighbor takes the remaining chair. Yun brings a chest to the table for herself, then fills a third bowl. The rice is still good. On her first night here, the neighbor carried a pot through the door. Now Yun returns the food as readily as she received it.

"When did you learn to cook?" the neighbor asks.

"When I stopped trying to force it," she says.

The words work as cooking advice. The neighbor has a bowl before her; the Counter has another sentence to attend to. For Yun, the answer reaches farther back, through the years she spent fighting her own conviction. Stopping that struggle made room for changes she could not force. She leaves the larger meaning unspoken. The smaller one is enough to share over rice.

This one.

A word from the old language surfaces as she watches steam rise from the bowls. She has barely used that tongue in a century. It gives her a name for the longing that has begun to form, for a place she has been away from a long time.

Home.
