Her hair is white now. Not the grey of transition. It had gone past that now, all the way through to the other side. It came white like paper, the way clouds are white. She looks at herself in the basin and the face that looks back is still recognizable — same bone structure, same proportions, the architecture underneath unchanged — but the surface has been rewritten. Lines everywhere. The wrinkles have multiplied over the years with the quiet efficiency of a process that, once started, does not need encouragement.

Her hands tremble slightly. The fine tremor of age, a small instability in the connection between intention and execution. The hands that could catch a arrow mid flight now shake when they hold a teacup. She holds the teacup anyway. The tea trembles in the cup. She has made peace with it. It is one of her more impressive accomplishments.

A voice from the fence. Familiar. Older than she remembers it.

"You got old."

A smile forms on Yun's lips. The Counter. Not six anymore. The child has grown — the face longer, the voice lower, the limbs stretched into the half-finished proportions of someone who is twelve, perhaps fourteen, and still growing with the unselfconscious determination of a body that has places to be. The counting continues. The counting will always continue.

"Yes," Yun says. "Finally."

"Your wrinkles," the Counter says, studying her face with the same analytical focus she applies to everything — the same intent assessment, the same numerical seriousness, only now delivered from a greater height. 

"How many are there?" Yun asks.

"Too many to count."

From the Counter. The girl who counts everything. Too many to count. The admission hangs in the air for a moment and the Counter looks at her with something close to awe — the awe of a mathematician encountering infinity for the first time, finding it not frightening but beautiful.

Yun smiles. The smile adds another wrinkle, probably. She is past caring.

"Come in," she says. "I'm cooking."

The Counter comes in and sits at the table. She silently counts two chairs, one window, three candles, two of them unlit. The counting is automatic, the low-level numerical narration that accompanies the Counter's existence the way breathing accompanies everyone else's — involuntary, constant, the quiet engine of a mind that cannot stop measuring the world and does not want to. Yun sees her eyes darting around and her lips forming silent words.

She cooks. The rice is good. Years of practice. The water ratio is correct. The heat is calibrated. The timing — the timing is right. Yun had learned the timing through burnt pots and mediocre meals, through evenings of eating things she made badly and mornings of trying again. The rice is fluffy. Separate grains. She had finally understood what rice wants.

Yun serves two bowls. The equality of shared meals — the same gesture Xu Ran performed every evening on a mountain, the giving and receiving that constitutes the smallest unit of community. She did not plan it. It just felt right.

A knock at the door.

The neighbor stands outside. Short, wide, appearing in the doorway with the inevitability of a weather pattern. Her nose follows the scent with the instinctive interest of a body that knows quality before the mind confirms it.

"You're cooking?"

"Yes. Please come in and share some with us."

The neighbor sits down on the last chair. Yun gets a chest as an improvised chair. Three at the table now. Three bowls. The rice is still good. The woman who received rice on her first night is now providing rice, returning the gesture. The reversal happened without announcement, without ceremony, the way the most significant things happen — gradually, and then all at once.

"When did you learn to cook?" the neighbor asks.

"When I stopped trying to force it," she says.

The sentence sits in the air between the three of them. The neighbor hears it as cooking advice. The Counter hears it as one sentence. Yun hears the larger sentence underneath — the one about walls and conviction and the long journey from a woman who fought everything to a woman who learned that stopping produced results the fighting never could. But the larger sentence is hers alone. The smaller one is enough to share.

This one.

A word surfaces. Not from the language of the village, not from any language she has spoken in the last century, but from the older one, the one that arrives when the others are insufficient. A single word, quiet as the steam rising from the rice. Together with a longing to a place she has not been to in a long time. A place that is not here.

*Home.*
