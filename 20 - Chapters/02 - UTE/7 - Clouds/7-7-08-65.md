Her hair is white.

Not the grey of transition — past that now, all the way through to the other side. White the way paper is white, the way clouds are white, the way things become when they have stopped holding onto color. She looks at herself in the basin and the face that looks back is still recognizable — same bone structure, same proportions, the architecture underneath unchanged — but the surface has been rewritten. Lines everywhere. Not seven anymore. The wrinkles have multiplied with the quiet efficiency of a process that, once started, does not need encouragement.

Her hands tremble. Slightly. The fine tremor of age, a small instability in the connection between intention and execution. The hands that could reshape reality now shake when they hold a teacup. She holds the teacup anyway. The tea, still bad — always bad, reliably bad, her one consistent culinary failure — trembles in the cup. She has made peace with bad tea. It is one of her more impressive accomplishments.

A voice from the fence. Familiar. Older than she remembers it.

"You got OLD."

The Counter. Not six anymore. Not seven. The child has grown — the face longer, the voice lower, the limbs stretched into the half-finished proportions of someone who is ten, perhaps eleven, and still growing with the unselfconscious determination of a body that has places to be. The counting continues. The counting will always continue.

"Yes," she says. "Finally."

"Your wrinkles," the Counter says, studying her face with the same analytical focus she applied at six — the same intent assessment, the same numerical seriousness, only now delivered from a greater height. "How many?"

"Too many to count."

From the Counter. The girl who counts everything. Too many to count. The admission hangs in the air for a moment, and the Counter looks at her with something close to awe — the awe of a mathematician encountering infinity for the first time, finding it not frightening but beautiful.

She smiles. The smile adds another wrinkle, probably. She is past caring.

"Come in," she says. "I'm cooking."

The Counter comes in. Sits at the table. Counts the chairs (two), the windows (one), the candles (three, two unlit). The counting is automatic, the low-level numerical narration that accompanies the Counter's existence the way breathing accompanies everyone else's — involuntary, constant, the quiet engine of a mind that cannot stop measuring the world and does not want to.

She cooks.

The rice is good.

The realization arrives with the steam. The water ratio is correct. The heat is calibrated. The timing — the timing is right. She learned the timing the way you learn anything worth learning: through burnt pots and mediocre meals and the long educational curriculum of failure, through evenings of eating things she made badly and mornings of trying again. The rice is fluffy. Separate grains. The particular quality of rice cooked by someone who finally understands what rice wants.

She serves. Two bowls. The equality of shared meals, the democratic distribution of identical portions — the same gesture Xu Ran performed every evening on a mountain, the giving and receiving that constitutes the smallest unit of community. She did not plan the echo. The echo came on its own.

A knock. The door.

The neighbor. Short, wide, appearing in the doorway with the inevitability of a weather pattern. She stands there and her nose does what noses do when they encounter the smell of good rice — it follows the scent with the instinctive interest of a body that knows quality before the mind confirms it.

"You're cooking?"

"Yes."

"For me?"

"If you sit down."

The neighbor sits. Three at the table now. Three bowls. The rice is good. The woman who received rice on her first night is now providing rice, the recipient become the source, the fed become the feeder. The reversal happened without announcement, without ceremony, the way the most significant things happen — gradually, and then all at once.

The neighbor eats. Pauses. Looks at the rice. Looks at the woman who cooked it. The evaluative gaze that the neighbor applies to everything, from rice to weather to the moral character of people she has known for thirty years.

"When did you learn to cook?" the neighbor asks.

"When I stopped trying to force it," she says.

The sentence sits in the air between the three of them. The neighbor hears it as cooking advice. The Counter hears it as data. She hears the larger sentence underneath — the one about walls and conviction and the long journey from a woman who fought everything to a woman who learned that stopping produced results the fighting never could. But the larger sentence is hers. The smaller one is enough.

She sits with white hair and trembling hands and too-many-to-count wrinkles, serving good rice to two people who eat it, and the moment is — she collects it. This one.

This one.

A word surfaces. Not from the language of the village, not from any language she has spoken in the last century, but from the older one, the one that arrives when the others are insufficient. A single word, quiet as the steam rising from the rice.

*Home.*
