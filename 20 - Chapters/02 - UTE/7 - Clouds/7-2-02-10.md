The plant bloomed.

Not immediately. Not the instant response to a command. The seedling sat in the cooking pot — our only pot, the cracked ceramic that you'd patched with a river clay, now filled with earth instead of rice. You had dug it up that morning — a pine seedling, barely a hand-span tall, pulled from the forest floor with its roots intact and a clump of mountain soil still clinging to them. 

The morning was overcast. Low clouds sat on the ridgeline above us, grey and still, weather that could hold for hours without committing to rain. The clearing smelled of wet stone and the residual smoke of last night's fire. You had set the pot on a flat rock, the hairline fracture still visible — the old crack from my disturbance, running rim to base, sealed but not healed. The water bowl beside it reflected the cloud-cover in its small, dark circle.

"When you're ready," you said.

You were sitting cross-legged, three meters back. Notebook open, pen horizontal across the page, the prepared stillness of a man who understood that documentation required patience and patience required posture. Xiǎohú had found a boulder at the clearing's edge and was watching with the amber-eyed neutrality of a creature that assessed all phenomena — plants, water, breakfast — with equal detachment.

I looked at the sapling.

Not willed. Knew. A bloom was — there. Already present, already contained in the tight spiral of petals, already real, waiting only for the arrangement of conditions that would allow the contained thing to express itself. I didn't make the plant bloom. I knew the plant was blooming. The distinction was the lesson.

The petals were open.

Not opened — were open. Had always been open. The spiral that had been tight was now not-tight and the transition between states was not a process but an absence of process — the bloom was there. A fact that is there after you remember it. Pink at the edges — the pink of wild mountain flowers, deeper than cherry blossom, lighter than plum. White toward the center. Yellow at the heart.

The roots pushed outward. The ceramic groaned — a sound like a knuckle cracking, small, definitive — and the pot split. Lines, radiating from the base where the roots had found the limits of their container and exceeded them. Soil spilled through the gaps, dark and damp, and a small tree sat in the wreckage of our only cooking vessel with its impossible bloom open and its root system exposed giving the morning a smell of turned earth and something faintly sweet.

You looked at the pot. At the bloom. At the pot.

"Conifers don't bloom," you said.

The tone was flat. The statement was botanical fact — the kind of fact that should have been unassailable, the kind that the natural world had confirmed for every generation of every species that had ever produced a pine cone instead of a flower.

"That was also our rice pot," you added.

I looked at the pieces. The ceramic shards, of our only pot that was, currently, in five pieces on a rock with a flowering pine tree growing out of it.

I knew the pot was whole.

Not willed. Not forced. The same state — the knowing, the certainty, the bone-deep conviction that the pot was intact because the pot was intact. The ceramic was whole. The cracks were — not there. The fractures, old and new, were absent because the pot's wholeness was the true state and the breaking had been the aberration.

The pot was whole. Not became whole — was whole. The way the bloom had always been open. The shards were not shards. The ceramic was continuous, unbroken, the surface smooth. The cracks had not been.

Soil was still spilling from the drainage hole at the bottom.

You picked up the pot. Turned it. Ran your thumb along where the old crack had been. Your expression did something complicated.

"The crack is gone," you said. "Not sealed. Gone."

You breathed in.

The expression on your face was — I filed it. Stored it in the memory-archive alongside the moments worth keeping. Your face, holding a pot that shouldn't have been whole, did something I hadn't seen before. The clinical mask didn't crack — but it dissolved. For a duration measured in heartbeats, the man behind the scholar was visible. Wonder, unprocessed, un-categorized, the raw human response to the impossible.

Then the mask reformed. You were a scholar again. You set the pot down, picked up your pen. And began to write.

"The bloom took you eight breaths," you said. "The pot — how long?"

"I don't know. I wasn't counting."

"Much shorter. Three breaths. Maybe two." You wrote the number. Drew a small box around it. "The repair was faster than the growth."

"The pot was whole before it broke. The bloom hadn't happened yet. One is restoring. The other is creating."

You looked at me. The pen stopped.

"You're saying restoration is easier than creation."

"I'm saying that the knowing is easier when there's a before to remember."

You wrote that down. Underlined it twice. The notebook was developing its own visual language — a taxonomy of margins and marks that only you could read and that would, one day, be the only record of what happened on this mountain.

The pine seedling sat on the rock beside the restored pot, its bloom already wilting in the mountain air — the natural direction reasserting itself, petals already crumpling, the brief impossibility returning to the jurisdiction of ordinary botany.

The cloud-cover held. The morning remained grey, diffused, the light even and shadowless. A bird called from somewhere beyond the clearing's edge — tentative, a single note, the first sound of wildlife at the boundary of the quiet radius.
