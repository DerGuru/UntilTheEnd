The pine seedling stood in our only cooking pot, its roots packed in earth where we usually cooked rice. You had dug it up that morning with a clump of mountain soil around the roots. Barely a handspan tall, it rose above the rim as I settled before it.

The morning was overcast. Low clouds lay still along the ridgeline, thick enough to last for watches without rain. The clearing smelled of wet stone and smoke from last night's fire. You had set the pot on a flat rock. River clay filled the old fracture from rim to base, leaving a visible seam where my qi had cracked it. Next to it, the water bowl held a small, dark reflection of the cloud cover.

"When you're ready," you said.

You sat cross-legged three paces back, your notebook open. The pen rested across the page while you waited. Xiǎohú watched from a boulder at the clearing's edge, the plant, the bowl, and our breakfast all in her view.

I looked at the sapling.

I let myself know that it bore a bloom. The flower was as clear in my mind as the seedling before me. Its petals lay in a tight spiral, already present in that certainty. I held it until looking at the pine felt like looking at a plant in bloom.

The petals were open.

I had seen no unfurling. A moment earlier I had been looking at a tight spiral; now petals spread from its center. Their edges were pink, deeper than cherry blossom but lighter than plum, like wild flowers higher on the mountain. The color faded to white toward a yellow heart. The whole flower stood out against the pine.

The roots pushed outward. Ceramic cracked sharply under them. Lines spread from the base, separating the pot into pieces. Dark, damp soil spilled through the gaps. A small tree stood in the wreckage of our cooking vessel, its impossible bloom open above exposed roots. The smell of turned earth reached me, with something sweet under it.

You looked at the pot. At the bloom. At the pot.

"Conifers don't bloom," you said.

You said it flatly, as you might have corrected someone too young to know a pine cone from a flower. I looked at the pink petals again. Every pine either of us had seen should have supported your objection.

"That was also our rice pot," you added.

The pot lay in five pieces on the rock. We would need it for dinner. At present, a flowering pine stood among the pieces.

I knew the pot was whole.

I knew its shape from our meals: the continuous curve of ceramic under my hands, the weight of it full of rice. I held that certainty while looking at the pieces. I knew the intact pot well enough to settle into the certainty without the earlier strain.

The pot stood whole next to the seedling. I had seen no edges draw together. Smooth ceramic curved around the bowl, and both the new fractures and the old clay-filled seam were gone. I remembered the breaking clearly, yet found no trace of it on the surface.

Soil was still spilling from the drainage hole at the bottom.

You picked up the pot, turned it, and ran your thumb along the place where the old crack had been. Your lips parted as you felt the smooth surface.

"The crack is gone," you said. "Not sealed. Gone."

You breathed in.

I remember your face above that pot. For a few heartbeats, you simply held it, your thumb resting where the repair had been. Usually you would already have a question. This time you looked at the ceramic as though it required all the attention you had.

Then you set the pot down and picked up your pen, with one more glance at the smooth side.

"The bloom took you eight breaths," you said. "How long for the pot?"

"I don't know. I wasn't counting."

"Much shorter. Three breaths. Maybe two." You wrote the number, then drew a small box around it. "The repair was faster than the growth."

"The pot was whole before it broke. The bloom hadn't happened yet. One is restoring. The other is creating."

You looked at me. The pen stopped.

"You're saying restoration is easier than creation."

"I'm saying that the knowing is easier when there's a before to remember."

You wrote that down, then underlined it twice. Marks crowded the margins of your notebook. I could not follow all of them anymore, but you found your place easily.

The pine seedling sat on the rock next to the restored pot. Its bloom was already wilting in the mountain air, the edges of the petals crumpling. The pink curled inward.

The cloud cover held. The morning was gray, its light even and shadowless. A single birdcall came from past the clearing's edge. I turned toward it. It was the first so near the boundary of the quiet around us.
