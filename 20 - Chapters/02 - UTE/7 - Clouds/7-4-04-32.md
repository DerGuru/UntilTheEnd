On the third morning I sat in the clearing with my hands pressed against my knees. I repeated the sentence more firmly than before, holding each word until the effort tightened my jaw. On the fourth day I pressed harder. By the fifth, I was bringing force to every attempt.

I am mortal.

I hammered at it like a man striking stone, trusting another blow to deepen the mark. I had solved enough difficulties that way to return to it when the quieter attempts failed.

The echo disappeared.

The resistance grew more solid as I pressed. The faint impression beyond it was gone. Each attempt made the limit clearer and left less room around the certainty that I would continue.

On the fifth day I opened my eyes to the same clearing. Water ran over the stones; birds called in the trees. Inside me, the resistance was stronger than it had been on the first morning.

You were watching. My jaw was tight, my hands clenched against my knees. Your gaze dropped to them before returning to my face.

"You're fighting again," you said.

You said it quietly. Birds called past your shoulder as I tried to ease my hands open.

"What else am I supposed to do?"

"This isn't a fight."

"What is it then?"

You set down your pen. I waited while you considered the answer, your hands still near the notebook.

"A surrender."

My hands tightened again. You could offer that word after the few years you had lived. I had spent millennia doing whatever continued existence required of me.

"I don't surrender," I said.

"Exactly." You waited for me to answer. When I said nothing, you said, "That's the problem." You stood, walked to the fire, and began cooking.

I did not move while you worked with your back to me. Soon the not-rice was in the pot. You tended the food and left the sentence where I would have to deal with it myself. I had another objection ready. You gave me no argument to answer.

The ground pressed hard under me. I thought of the child on the cave floor, building this certainty through nights she had to survive. It was easy to call her a girl I remembered. Harder to acknowledge that I had been that frightened child, that I had made the certainty permanent.

Now I was pressing against it with the same force I had used to hold on.

Surrender.

I turned the word over and watched your shoulders move as you stirred. I knew how to fight, how to endure, and how to hold on. Those actions had sustained me longer than I could remember. I tried to imagine setting them down without reaching for another action to replace them.

Letting go felt like surrender. Past surrender lay failure, then death. The old sequence ran as easily as my breath.

I wanted to become able to die. Yet every approach to the possibility met the same fear that had kept me alive. I could understand my aim while reacting to it as a threat. My hands were still on my knees, and I could not picture what surrender would look like in practice.

You went on stirring the not-rice. I could have called you back into the conversation. You would offer another sentence, and I would find a way to oppose it. I watched the spoon instead.

You cooked. I sat.

The wall remained.

Even now I wanted to win this. Winning meant surviving, as it always had. I had brought that certainty to a task whose purpose was to end its hold on me.

I needed to unlearn survival.

The sentence was easy enough to form. I looked down at the hands that had carried me through so many years. Survival lay in their habitual movements, in my breath, and in the qi beneath my skin. I could scarcely find a part of myself that had escaped its practice.

The smell of cooking grain reached me. You watched the pot.

I closed my eyes again. This time I let the resistance be. It had been part of me for so long. I sat with its thickness and the certainty, without forming the next sentence against it.

The wall was still there when I opened my eyes.

But I was still there too.

My hands rested open on my knees. I had spent that time next to the resistance without attacking it. The difference was there, though I had no name for it yet.
