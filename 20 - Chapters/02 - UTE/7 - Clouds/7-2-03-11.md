A drop of water rested on one of the blooming plant's leaves. The leaf was broad, green, and waxy. I leaned close enough to see the drop's oblong shape, clear at its edges where light passed through.

I looked at it.

The drop rose slowly from the leaf. Once free of the surface, it rounded into a sphere. I held it in the air between the plant and the sky, light passing through it. The clearing appeared inside that small curve, inverted. A whole patch of trees lay upside down in a drop of water.

You stared.

Your mouth hung open. The pen was motionless in your hand. Above us, a thin cloud crossed the sun, and light dimmed inside the drop, then brightened. You followed the change without looking down at your notebook. After all your careful records, that still pen struck me most. The drop returned to the leaf while you watched.

"Again," you said.

"Why?"

I glanced at the unused page. You had seen it happen. What could another attempt show you?

"Because it's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen."

You were still looking at the leaf when you said it. Usually an observation came with a qualification, something you needed to establish before trusting your own account. This time you let the words stand.

Silence.

I raised the drop again. It hung above the leaf, reflecting the clearing in its curved surface. I held it there while you watched. Your pen lay still. For a while I attended to what you did: a small sphere of water in the morning light.

Then the drop fell.

My certainty had loosened, and the drop struck the leaf with a small, clear sound. Only then did the tiredness arrive. I let my shoulders settle while the water spread back into an oblong shape.

Beneath what you had found beautiful lay my unease. I had made water rise where it should have fallen. I could not tell how far that change might reach if I believed something larger. I thought of sunrise over our camps, of rain striking the trees, and of all the ordinary things I had trusted to happen by themselves. I had been living among them with this power for ages. How much had I already changed?

You were writing now, finally setting down the observations you had left unrecorded while the drop hung before us. Your pen moved steadily.

Xiǎohú sat on her boulder. Her amber eyes followed the drop back to the leaf. Then she turned toward the trees.

"The world is clay," I said.

You looked up.

"I form the world like clay. But it was always clay. I just didn't know I was the one with hands."

Your pen paused. You held your breath, looking from me to the plant, before you spoke.

"If you have hands," you said. "And the world is clay. What have your hands been doing for the last several thousand years?"

I looked down at my hands. The clearing lay in the same morning light, the drop resting on its leaf. I could picture too many things I might have touched beyond this place. I had no answer for you yet.
