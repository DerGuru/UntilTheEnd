A single drop, sitting on a leaf — one of the blooming plant's leaves, broad, green, the surface slightly waxy. The drop was ordinary. Transparent, oblong, shaped by surface tension into the geometry that water adopted when resting on a surface to which it didn't fully adhere. A single drop.

I looked at it.

The drop rose. Off the leaf, upward, against the direction that gravity had established as the only acceptable direction for water drops on leaves. It rose slowly in a gentle, unhurried climb of a thing finding that up was the only option. It hung in the air between the leaf and the sky, a sphere of water suspended in nothing, catching the light, refracting the world into a small, inverted image contained within its surface.

You stared.

Your mouth — slightly open. The pen in your hand, stationary. The notebook forgotten. Above us, a thin cloud crossed the sun and the light shifted, the drop's suspended surface dimming and brightening as the shadow passed and the world it contained — the small, inverted world inside the water — flickered like a memory. For a man who processed the world through documentation, the abandonment of the pen was the most honest response you could produce. The drop returned to the leaf. The moment returned to ordinary time. The world returned to the world.

"Again," you said.

"Why?"

Not coyness. A genuine question. Why again? What would repetition prove that the first occurrence hadn't?

"Because it's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen."

The words arrived without the qualifications that typically accompanied your statements. No clinical framing. No observational distance. *The most beautiful thing I've ever seen.* Raw. Immediate. A man looking at a water drop that was defying physics and seeing beauty instead of data.

Silence.

I picked up the drop again and held it in the air. The drop held. The air held. The moment held — the, suspended quality of a moment that all participants recognize as significant. The rare synchronization of event and awareness that turns time into a room you can stand inside.

Then the drop fell.

Naturally. The conviction that had sustained it — exhausting to hold, but I hadn't noticed the exhaustion until the release — dissolved and the drop returned to gravity's jurisdiction, hitting the leaf with the small sound of water meeting surface, the note that was also an ending.

There was danger in the beauty. If I could reverse gravity for a water drop, what else could I reverse? What laws applied to me and which were suggestions? If the world was clay — formable, reshapable, subject to the pressure of my conviction — then everything I'd ever experienced had been experienced in a world that I had been unconsciously forming without permission. Every sunrise. Every rainfall. Every ordinary physical event might have been filtered through the lens of a conviction so deep it shaped reality before reality could shape itself.

I looked at You. You were writing now — the pen recaptured, the scholar's autopilot engaged, the notation of observations that would fill your notebook with the empirical record of impossibility. 

Xiǎohú sat on her boulder. The amber eyes had watched the drop rise and fall — and filed it under irrelevant.

"The world is clay," I said.

You looked up.

"I form the world like clay. But it was always clay. I just didn't know I was the one with hands."

You processed. The pen paused and you held your breath for a moment. The moment where the scholar's mind tested every word before releasing it.

"If you have hands," you said. "And the world is clay. What have your hands been doing for the last several thousand years?"

The question sat in the clearing. I did not answer it. The answer was too large and too true and too frightening. The clearing was too small, the morning was too ordinary and the drop had already fallen.
