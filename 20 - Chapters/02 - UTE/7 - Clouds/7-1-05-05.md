Rain came overnight. A patient, persistent rain like the cave's drip that made sleep difficult for reasons that had nothing to do with the sound. I lay awake keeping the rain from reaching us and listened to the mountain drink.

By morning, the world was washed. The trail steamed where the early sun hit wet stone. You packed the camp in the efficient, wordless routine we'd developed — bedrolls, cook pot, notebook wrapped in oilcloth. I scattered the fire's ashes. We walked. Higher. The trail thinned, rock more exposed, the vegetation now more suggestion than fact. The sky was scrubbed clean, the emptiness that follows weather.

You hadn't spoken since we'd started. A half-hour of nothing but footsteps and breathing and the creak of pack straps. I was beginning to think the silence was its own statement when you adjusted your pack — the familiar, habitual recalibration of a man carrying weight over uneven terrain — and spoke without looking at me.

"Learn it as a tool," you said. "Then put it down."

I stopped.

"What?"

"You're trying to force a door that opens inward," you said. "Every time you push, it closes harder. So stop pushing."

"And do what?"

"Learn the mechanism first. You can warp reality — you've proven that. But it's uncontrolled. It fires defensively. What if you controlled it?"

"I've controlled it. At the river. The sect —"

"That was reactive. I need them gone, they're gone. What I mean is systematic control. Deliberate, practiced, like a craftsman — not a miracle-worker. Small things. Then bigger things. Then — " you paused. The pause was loaded, heavy with the conclusion you wanted me to reach on my own.

"Then myself."
 
"Then yourself."

I considered. The trail climbed. Xiǎohú was ahead, as always, the amber shape navigating terrain with an efficiency that made our bipedal efforts look grotesque.

"Since when are you that clever?" I said.

"I wasn't considered one of the best cultivators of my sect for no reason. Body tempering only gets you so far."

I almost smiled.

The logic was — logical. The frustration of the last two days had been because trying to demolish a building by running into it headfirst didn't work. You were suggesting I learn architecture first. Understand the building. Map its structure. Identify the load-bearing walls. And then, with the knowledge of someone who understands what they're dismantling, take it apart piece by piece.

Craftsmanship.

"How?" I asked.

"Start small. You bent a tree without meaning to. Bend one on purpose. Understand how it works from the inside — not what happens, but what you do when it happens. Where the conviction sits. What it feels like. How the qi responds. Map yourself."

You were right. The recognition was annoying in the way that someone else's rightness always is — the moment where your own intelligence feels inadequate and another person's feels presumptuous and the fact of their correctness makes both feelings irrelevant.

"You've been thinking about this," I said.

"I've been thinking about you," you said. And then, before the sentence could acquire weight, "In a clinical sense. Observational."

"Of course."

"I'm a scholar."

"Of course."

We walked. The clouds — high, thin, reformed from the dissolved remnants of yesterday's cover — drifted with the purposelessness that I was beginning to envy. Form without grip. Shape without will. The clouds didn't decide to be clouds. They were clouds because the conditions produced clouds and when the conditions changed, they were something else.

The plan. Your plan. It was — a plan.

The steps were clear but the path between them was not. I was standing at the beginning of it with a fox and a scholar. 

"We need to find a place," I said. "Somewhere stable. Where I can practice without —" I gestured at the barren rock around us. "Without this. I need things to work with. Trees. Water. Something that responds."

You looked at the ridge ahead, then back down the trail we'd come up. The treeline was visible below — a dark band of green where the mountain still allowed it.

"So we back down," you said. "There was a plateau. An hour below the treeline, maybe two. Flat ground. A stream."

"You noticed that while walking?"

"I notice everything while walking. It's a curse."

You almost smiled. "A plateau with trees and a stream. And enough distance from anything you could accidentally flatten."

"That's the idea."

We turned around. The simple act of reversing direction on a mountain trail. 

One step back. 

Two steps forth.
