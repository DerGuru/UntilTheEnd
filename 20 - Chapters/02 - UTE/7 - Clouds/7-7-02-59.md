She stands, which takes longer than it should.

Not much longer — a breath, maybe two. Her legs do what they have always done, but there is a negotiation now, a small conversation between muscle and gravity that used to be settled before it began. She feels heavier. Only slightly, the difference between weight deflected by qi and weight carried by bone. Her feet press into the floorboards and the floorboards creak. And the creak is — honest. The sound a house makes when it is holding someone real.

She looks at her reflection in the basin by the door. The same face. The same proportions, the same arrangement of features she has worn for millennia. But she sees it differently now — the way you see your own face after an illness, when the familiar becomes strange and every line reports in like a detail you overlooked for years. A human face. A face that will age.

She goes to the garden.

The seeds she planted weeks ago are growing. She kneels beside them — the ground cool and damp under her knees, the particular dampness of morning soil that hasn't seen the sun yet — and looks at the shoots. Small, green, fragile in the way that new things are fragile. They are growing without her help. Without the unconscious shaping that her qi has been performing on everything around her for millennia.

These shoots are growing because of dirt and water and sun and nothing else. The modest miracle that the world performs every day without needing permission.

She touches a leaf. It bends under her finger, then springs back when she lets go. The resilience of something small that doesn't know it should be impressive.

And then — something else. Something she notices because it is no longer there. The pressure. A pressure that had emanated from her for so long that it was normal — the way you stop hearing a hum until the hum stops and the resulting silence is louder than the sound ever was. The unconscious shaping of temperature and weather and the behavior of small creatures — gone.

The world around her settles. The garden, the house, the village beyond the fence — all of it adjusting to the absence of her influence, the way a room adjusts after someone opens the windows. The adjustment is small. Her pressure had always been subtle: keeping rain slightly to the side, temperature slightly comfortable, small animals slightly distant. Background noise. So quiet she had forgotten it was there.

A bug crosses her hand. Small, brown. It does not detour. Does not sense the qi-perimeter that has been diverting small creatures for millennia. It walks across her hand, across her wrist, and continues into the grass with the unhurried confidence of an animal that has places to be.

She watches it go. There is something about the bug's indifference that she likes.

And she remembers. Everything. At once. Thousands of years compressed into a single breath — every face, every name, every place she has stood, every season she has watched turn. The weight of it should crush her. Had crushed her, before. Had broken through walls that fought to contain it. Had stopped a river mid-current while she knelt in the water and the world bent around the force of it.

Nothing bends now. The garden stays exactly as it is.

The sensation lasts one breath. Maybe two.

Then the memories settle. As if they were snow that settles after it falls — still present, covering everything, but resting now. The memories are hers and she is theirs and the mutual belonging is not a weight but a warmth, the kind of warmth that a house holds after a long day of sun.

She sits in the garden, watching the bugs cross her hands. The shoots grow at the speed of growing. The sun is warm on the back of her neck, the simple warmth of a star that has been doing its job for billions of years and sees no reason to stop.

She is at peace. She does not examine the feeling. She does not test it for authenticity. She sits in it the way you sit in a chair that fits — without thinking about the sitting.
