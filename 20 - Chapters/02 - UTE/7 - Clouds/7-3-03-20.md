Smoke drifted low from our fire before the morning wind carried it off. We had camped along the edge of the destruction, where living forest met the stumps. Trees still stood next to us, but the air tasted of dust. Few birds had returned. I had sat awake all night, afraid of what might happen if I let my attention loosen.

You had slept. You had learned to rest where you could, even next to damage you would have to examine in the morning. Watching you fold your bedroll, I saw what that discipline gave you. Your hands were steadier than mine.

You made tea over a small fire. The same pot had traveled with us for so long that I no longer remembered when you first brought it. Steam rose from the rim while you waited for it to brew.

"When you got sad," you said, "the trees died."

You spoke evenly, each word clear over the small sounds of the fire. You watched me, waiting to see whether I would answer.

"Correlation isn't causality," I said.

I had used that argument before. Two events occurring together did not mean one had caused the other. It was a sound objection, easily offered from beside a fire. I had reached for it quickly, with the stumps in plain view behind you.

"That was causality," you said. "I went through it. Right beside you."

You poured the tea. Steam rose between us and thinned in the cold morning air.

"You were thinking about something. Your face changed. The fog thickened. Then—" You gestured at the circle of stumps. "That's not correlation. That's not even causality in the philosophical sense. That's you making what you feel."

I drank the tea. It was adequate, as reliably drinkable as everything you brewed. I took another mouthful before answering.

The fog had thickened as I thought of Wei. The burst had followed the memory of the nest. You had been close enough to watch my face through both changes. I went over the sequence and found no break in it.

I held the warm cup between my hands. If a feeling could change the things around me, every feeling carried that risk. Grief had already left a forest in stumps. I imagined what anger might do, then wished I had stopped at what was visible.

An earthquake. A flood. Things far past the tree line where we sat, struck because I had felt something near them.

"Don't do that," you said.

"Do what?"

"Spiral." You set your cup down. "I can see it. Your eyes go somewhere else when you spiral. You stop looking at what's in front of you and start looking at everything that could go wrong."

"Everything is going wrong."

"No. One thing went wrong. You destroyed some trees. Things happen."

"I killed three birds before they even hatched, and their mother."

You paused. Your lips parted, then closed. I waited while you reconsidered whatever answer had come first.

"Yes," you said. "You did."

You left it at that. The cup was warm against my fingers.

"So what do I do?"

"Learn," you said. "That's the whole structure. That's why we're here. You learn what you are and then you learn to be it differently."

I looked at the circle. Dust had settled overnight across the bare brown earth. Between the stumps lay ground the canopy had hidden. The smell of burned feathers had faded in the morning wind. I remembered it clearly.

"Every emotion," I said. "Every single one."

"Apparently."

"For how long?"

"What do you mean?"

"How long have I been doing this?" I gestured toward the circle, remembering the fog that had enclosed us. The bird was out of sight from here. "Millennia? Since the beginning?"

You didn't answer immediately. You picked up the pen. You wrote something and set the pen down.

"That's a good question," you said. "That's the first good question you've asked."

Xiǎohú sat at the edge of the destruction, facing the boundary between trees and stumps. I could not remember seeing her move during the burst. Perhaps she had fled and returned. Her calm told me nothing about how she had survived it.

I finished the tea. It was cold.

"Every emotion," I said again, testing the words while looking at the cut trunks.

You put out the fire. I watched you pack camp, one small task giving way to the next.

We walked between living trees. Behind us, the circle grew smaller until trunks hid most of it. Needles shifted above the path in the same wind that had passed over the stumps.

I tried to feel nothing.

That didn't work either.
