You washed the grain twice that evening. That was the first sign. Usually you cooked the not-rice competently, without ambition. Grain, water, and heat kept us fed. You rarely asked more of them.

Tonight you sliced a wild onion, the bitter kind that grew in shallow soil between rocks. You had found ginger too, improbable at this altitude but present. You built the fire larger than usual, checking each stick's position before placing the pot over it.

Xiǎohú watched next to me. I left you to the careful work, which got the attention you usually reserved for the notebook. The spoon moved steadily through the grain.

For once, the food did not catch on the bottom. You turned the pot where the heat was uneven. Actual technique made a considerable difference.

"You must become fearless," you said over the pot, "in order to accept the fear."

Steam rose between us while you stirred.

"Is it clear to you," I said, "how absurd that sounds?"

"Yes."

"And it's still true?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because what you're holding to is in the way."

You stirred. The fire crackled. We had said enough for one evening.

Then you spoke more quietly. "I think I've written most of it."

You pointed at the notebook with the spoon.

"The notebook?"

"Your story. As much as I could get." You gestured toward the trail beyond the fire. "With gaps. With ink stains."

The notebook lay next to you, as always. Its covers were worn; thread showed along a binding strained by being opened in rain. You had treated those pages as the most important thing you carried. I knew how high your standards for importance were.

I looked from the notebook to you.

You served the food into our bowls. I took a bite, then another.

You had made good not-rice.

We had shared mediocre grain, burned grain, and grain whose chief virtue was that it could be swallowed. Now three ingredients had received your actual attention. The difference was in every mouthful.

I ate everything, scraping the last grains from the bowl.

"It's good," I said.

"I know."

The mountain darkened while the fire burned lower. We sat with empty bowls, the worn notebook between us. Your pen lay still next to it.

My voice had filled those pages through your hand. You asked; I answered. You wrote down a life I had never told anyone. Now the story had reached the place where we sat.

I looked at the clean bottom of my bowl. You could have made good rice every time. Laziness or strategy?
