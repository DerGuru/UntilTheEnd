Xu Ran cooks carefully this time.

That is the first sign. He usually cooks the not-rice the way he does everything — competently, without ambition. Grain. Water. Heat. The triangle of sustenance traversed daily with the efficiency of a man who treats food as fuel and cooking as procedure.

Tonight is different. He washes the grain twice. Slices something green — a wild onion, the bitter one that grows in shallow soil between rocks. He had found ginger. Improbable at this altitude, but present. He builds the fire larger than usual, each stick placed with intention rather than efficiency.

Yun watches. Xiǎohú watches. Neither interrupts the ritual — the careful hands, the measured movements, the attention he usually reserves for the notebook now directed at a pot of not-rice.

He stirs. The grain does something it has never done under his hand: behaves. The compliance of food responding to actual technique rather than exposure to heat and hope.

"You must become fearless," he says, over the pot, "in order to accept the fear."

The sentence arrives while he is stirring. Steam rises between them.

"Is it clear to you," she says, "how absurd that sounds?"

"Yes."

"And it's still true?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because what you're holding to is in the way."

He stirs. The fire crackles. The silence that follows is comfortable — two people who have said enough for one evening and know it.

Then, quieter: "I think I've written most of it."

He points at the notebook with the spoon. His voice is careful — a man approaching a subject from the side because the front is guarded.

"The notebook?" she says.

"Your story. As much as I could get." He gestures — the mountain, the trail, the months. "With gaps. With ink stains."

The notebook was beside him. Always beside him. The covers worn, the binding exhausted — paper and thread that have been opened and closed and carried through rain. He had treated it as the most important object in the world by someone whose standards for importance are extreme.

Both of them know.

He serves. Bowl to bowl. She takes a bite.

He has made good not-rice.

Every meal they have shared — the mediocre grain, the burned grain, the adequate grain that defined his culinary range — and now this. Three ingredients and actual attention and the result is *good*.

Yun eats everything. Every grain, the bowl scraped clean.

"It's good," she says.

"I know."

The fire dies slowly. The mountain darkens around them. They sit with empty bowls, a dying fire and the notebook between them — worn, stained, full.

Her voice, his pen. The slow excavation of a life she had never told anyone. He asked, she answered. He wrote, she watched the pen move. Now the pen is still and story has caught up.

He could have made good rice every time. Laziness or strategy?
