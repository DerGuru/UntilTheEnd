I closed my eyes again, hands on my knees. Mountain air cooled my throat as I drew a slow breath, held it, and let it go. I had arranged myself as carefully as I could. You waited next to me with your notebook.

"I. Am. Mortal," I said. I gave each word its own breath, trying to make the sentence reach the certainty under it. The effort was plain. That, at least, had changed.

I pushed harder. I left my qi alone, reaching instead for the conviction through which I had been changing reality since before I understood what I was doing. It lay below thought, as certain as my own hands. I tried to place the statement there.

The self-protection responded.

Before, I had met headaches, gaps in memory, and thoughts cut short. This time I was aware of the mountainside throughout. A movement passed through the camp around me, sudden enough to interrupt my next breath.

Ten paces away, a young pine stood among the rocks. Its trunk bent halfway toward the ground. The air was still. For a heartbeat it held there, roots fast in the rubble, then straightened. Needles shook loose from its branches.

"You just bent a tree," you said.

I opened my eyes. The trunk was straight again, its branches still. Fresh needles lay scattered over the rocks below.

"I did not."

"You did."

"I was trying to—"

"I know what you were trying to do. You tried to believe yourself mortal but instead you bent a tree." You were writing, your pen moving quickly across the page. "The qi. I felt it. It erupted."

The residue lingered. My fingers tingled, and a vibration ran through my jaw. Power had discharged while I was occupied with the sentence. I had let go of no restraint that I could remember, yet the pine needles lay there.

"Again," I said.

"Is that—"

"Again."

I tried again. I pressed harder against the conviction, bringing everything I had to bear, repeating the sentence through clenched teeth. I am mortal. I am mortal. On the third repetition, a sound rose from the ground.

The loose stones around the camp were vibrating, a thin, close sound against the ground. Some of them cracked. Fine fractures crossed their surfaces like those left by a hard frost, though the temperature had held steady. Under the loose stones, the mountainside lay still.

Scrub grass curled in the rock crevices. Every blade turned toward me, its tip pulling free of the shelter of the stones. My qi pressed against them.

My qi was flaming.

It flared beyond the bounds I had set for it. I had meant to change myself, yet my power was moving outward, bending the grass as it had bent the tree. Each time I pressed at the conviction, the flare strengthened. The hold within me tightened.

"Stop," you said.

I stopped. My shoulders sagged at once. I sat with my hands on my knees, breathing through the exhaustion. The metallic taste had grown thick enough to coat my tongue, copper-bright with each swallow.

Your eyes went from the cracked stones to the curling grass, then narrowed. You turned back a page in the notebook, taking your time. I waited.

"The problem," you said, "is not that you can't believe it."

A pause.

"The problem," you said, "is that every part of you does believe that you're immortal. And when you try to contradict that, it doesn't argue. It doesn't resist. It—" You gestured at the surroundings. "It defends. Autonomously. Like you pull a hand from fire. As an instinctive reaction. A reflex. You have to unlearn the reflex."

"I know."

"Now." You wrote something, then underlined it. "How does one unlearn a reflex?"

I had no answer. Xiǎohú had moved twenty paces away, past the cracked stones. She watched us from there, clear ground between her paws and the nearest trembling blade.

How does one unlearn a reflex?

The clouds moved overhead. A ragged edge dissolved into the blue while fresh vapor gathered farther along the ridge. Wind carried it above us. Its shape changed as it went.

My hands had closed against my knees.

I eased the fingers apart. At the next thought of mortality, they tightened again.
