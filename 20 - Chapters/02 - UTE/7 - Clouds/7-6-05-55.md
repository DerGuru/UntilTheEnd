Yun rises from under the tree and brushes dust from her trousers. She goes inside to make tea. Through the window, petals are still falling into the garden.

The tea is bad. The water was too hot or too cold, or she found the precise temperature at which tea becomes hot water with herbs. She drinks it anyway, standing at the window.

Petals fall from the plum tree. One loosens from a branch, and the air is still until another follows.

What has happened under the tree?

The conviction has lived in her as a fact. Everything else rests on it, as a roof rests on the wall that carries its weight. She has been able to name the belief for some time. Under the tree, its hold changed.

It has shifted.

The thing that was "impossible to loosen" is now "difficult to loosen." The difference sounds minuscule. It is everything.

She sets the tea down and lets it cool.

A child counts somewhere down the lane. A cart creaks past, and wind moves through the trees. Clouds hang above the garden. She listens to those ordinary sounds and considers a change large enough to alter the rest of her life.

She washes the cup and puts it away.

Outside, the village continues. The child's voice is still counting. "Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen."

She looks through the window at a brown-haired child in the lane. Six, maybe seven. Her limbs are too long for her small torso, and every bend toward the ground is awkward.

The child is counting stones.

She presses an index finger to each stone as she gives it a number. Fourteen. Fifteen. Sixteen. Gray stones lie among brown ones, the ordinary contents of a village lane. Each gets equal care.

The child looks up. She studies the strange adult, her face giving Yun nothing to prepare for.

"How many stones in your garden?" the child asks.

Yun glances at the stones under her window. For someone doing the child's work, the question is perfectly reasonable.

"I don't know," she says.

The child takes this as a personal failing. Her eyes narrow under a frown. Yun has neglected something a householder ought to know.

The child returns to counting. Seventeen. Eighteen.

Yun watches her move down the lane from stone to stone, each with the same deliberate touch. Whatever the purpose of this self-appointed work, the girl takes it seriously.

She turns from the window to the small, clean, quiet house. The fear is still in her, quieter than yesterday. She can attend to the room without losing track of it. There is space for both now.

She sits at the table.

The conviction is whole, but it has shifted. A door ajar is still the same door, set in the same frame. Yet light passes through the opening, and air reaches the space behind it. She sits with that small difference.

She breathes. The breath goes all the way in. The breath comes all the way out. 

Shadows lengthen through the afternoon. The child's voice fades down the lane. The work of counting continues farther than Yun can hear.
