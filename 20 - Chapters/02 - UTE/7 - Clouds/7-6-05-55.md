Yun stands up. Brushes the dust from her trousers. Goes inside. Makes tea. 

The tea is bad. The water is too hot or too cold or somewhere in the middle that produces tea that is technically tea but fails to achieve the qualities that distinguish tea from hot-water-with-herbs. She drinks it anyway. Standing at the window. Looking at the garden.

The plum tree is still there. The petals are still falling — intermittently, the natural shedding that the season produces, each petal departing the branch on its own schedule.

What has happened under the tree?

Her conviction. The statement that has lived in her — not as thought, not as belief but as a fact. The foundational, structural, architectural fact around which everything else was built. The load-bearing wall.

It has shifted.

The thing that was "impossible to loosen" is now "difficult to loosen." The difference might sound minuscule, but the difference is everything.

She sets the tea down and lets it cool.

The world continues. The afternoon holds its own sounds — a child counting somewhere down the lane, the creak of a cart, the wind in the trees. The garden grows. The sky holds its clouds. The largest moment happens in a whisper.

She washes the cup and puts it away.

Outside, the village continues. The child's voice is still counting. "Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen."

She looks through the window. A child — six, maybe seven — stands in the lane between the houses. Small. Brown hair. Her limbs too long for the torso, coordination approximate, the body still negotiating its own proportions.

The child is counting stones.

Each stone receives a number. Each number receives a touch — the index finger pressing each stone with the deliberate contact that counting requires. Fourteen. Fifteen. Sixteen. The stones are not remarkable. They are the ordinary stones of a village lane — grey and brownish.

The child looks up and looks at Yun. The child's face is — unreadable. Neutral, assessing, the blank appraisal that children perform when encountering adults they have not yet categorized.

"How many stones in your garden?" the child asks.

The question is absurd and perfectly logical in the framework of a mind that counts everything.

"I don't know," she says.

The child appears to consider this a personal failing. A small frown and a slight narrowing of eyes — communicate the disapproval of someone who has encountered the uncounted and finds it unacceptable.

The child returns to counting. Seventeen. Eighteen.

She watches from the window. The counting continues down the lane, the child moving from stone to stone with the methodical progression of someone performing important work, the seriousness that children bring to self-assigned tasks.

She turns from the window. The house is small, clean and quiet. The fear is there, in the corner, quieter than yesterday, present but no longer pushing its presence. Like a guest who has been acknowledged and has stopped demanding attention.

She sits at the table.

The conviction has shifted. Still whole, but shifted. The difference between a locked door and a door slightly ajar — the same door, the same frame, the same hinges. But there's light coming through. There's air coming through.

She breathes. The breath goes all the way in. The breath comes all the way out. 

The afternoon passes. The shadows lengthen. The child's voice fades down the lane, counting things that do not need counting but are counted anyway because the counting is the point.
