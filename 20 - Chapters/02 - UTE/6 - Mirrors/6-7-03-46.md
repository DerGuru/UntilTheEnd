You unpacked the bag.

Everything came out: every piece of dried food, every spare cloth, every tool you'd accumulated across the thousand-li logistics of traveling with an immortal who didn't technically need supplies and a mortal who absolutely did. You spread it all on the flat rock, a taxonomy of necessities.

Rice. Two portions left, maybe three if you stretched. Dried meat. Herbs, three kinds, the medicinal ones you'd gathered along the way, the ones you pretended were for flavor but actually kept in a separate pouch labeled in your scholar's notation. Flint. String. The small folding knife. Two cloths. A sewing kit, the one I'd used for the robe, needle and thread and a strip of reinforcing fabric. Your notebook. The brush. The ink stone.

You touched each piece. Picked it up, weighed it in your hand, not with a scale but with palms that had packed and unpacked this bag long enough to know the weight of each item and detect variance. Set it down. Next piece. The procedure was systematic, the inventory of a person whose next leg of the journey would have no resupply points.

I sat across from you, close enough that the items between us were a shared field, the domestic spread of two people organizing their life on a flat rock on a mountainside. You handled each thing with deliberate contact, each item a decision. Keep. Discard. Repack.

This was pragmatism. How much rice, how much water, how many days. The mathematics of mortal survival, applied by a man who understood that immortals might not need rice but mortals did, and that a mortal who stopped eating stopped walking. And if the mortal stopped walking, the immortal would be alone.

You counted rice grains, not literally, but almost, giving the bag the attention of someone for whom each grain represented distance and time. Time was the only currency your mortal body traded in.

The thought connected. Your hands sorting provisions fit the image that had been forming since the river. The problem of letting go wasn't dramatic. It wasn't a single moment of release, a theatrical opening of fists or a climactic spiritual event. It was geological. The millennia were layers of stone, accumulated over time and removable only over time, the whole structure solidified into something that resembled permanence but was still just layers. Just time. Just accumulated decisions that had hardened into fact.

You don't demolish a mountain by hitting it once.

You demolish a mountain the same way it was built, particle by particle, grain by grain, tiny changes over timescales that make individual effort seem absurd. Your hands on the rice bag, weighing, counting, deciding: this was the method. Piece by piece. Step by step. Not the grand gesture. The daily work.

I reached for a stone near my knee and picked it up. An ordinary stone, gray, smooth, river-rounded, one of countless on every mountainside. I held it. Turned it. Its weight settled in my palm, registering through skin, nerve, and the mechanical sensitivity of fingers against mass.

I set it down.

You finished your inventory and began repacking. The order had changed, tightened and redistributed, the new arrangement reflecting revised judgments about weight, accessibility, and the number of days between here and wherever we were going.

You looked up.

"Tomorrow?"

The word was simple and practical, asking nothing more than whether we'd leave tomorrow.

"Tomorrow," I said.

Tomorrow. The next day. One day. The smallest unit of forward motion.

Xiǎohú was already asleep nearby, curled, tail over nose. Small, regular exhalations. She slept completely, without the half-waking vigilance that characterized human rest.

I helped you pack by handing items over: the rice bag, the herbs, the cloths. My hands doing hand-things. The needle and thread went into the side pocket. The ink stone, wrapped in its cloth, went near the bottom where the weight would settle.

We packed the bag together.
