I sat in the field.

The grass was growing. Not visibly, not the time-lapse acceleration of plant biology, but the grass around me was taller than the grass further away. The blades closest to where I sat were longer, greener, more vital. As if my presence nourished them, as if the excess energy of the mechanism, running continuously and involuntarily, leaked into the ground and fed whatever was nearest.

Where a hundred and thirty-nine people had stood. No. Where a hundred and thirty-nine people had *been*. There were no impressions. No trampled grass, no scuffed earth, no evidence. Just grass. Just wind. Just the empty field.

I let the memories come again. The thousands of years before Wei, the accumulated lifetime of a self that had believed itself into immortality and then walked the world it had shaped, night after night, millennium after millennium.

Battlefields.

A plain where two armies had met and I'd walked between them. I'd fought. I always fought, picked a side or didn't, and the result was the same. The arrows that should have killed me didn't. The spears slid past. The men on both sides died, I stood in a field of the dead, and the war was over because I'd ended it.

Every time.

A siege that lasted four months until I walked through the gate. I'd been inside the walls, or outside; the details had compressed. The smell was still there: burning grain, wet stone, the sweetness of decay in summer heat. A river crossing where the water ran red for three days until I crossed it and the dying stopped.

I'd ended wars the way storms end droughts. By arriving. By the sheer weight of something that couldn't be killed walking into a space where killing was the point.

And then I'd stopped needing to fight. Somewhere across the centuries, I'd crossed a threshold that had no name. Cultivation couldn't describe it. Technique couldn't contain it. I'd walk onto a battlefield and the fighting would stop, and the killing lost the argument.

Someone had figured out what I was. Not the whole truth, but enough to know that I could be aimed, that a word and a direction were sufficient. He'd had me for centuries, or I'd let him, the distinction blurred by time. His weapon. His answer to every problem that had a location.

I remembered the day I stopped being aimed. Only ash was left. After that came the walking, for a very long time.

Then faces poured into me again.

A woman who laughed too loudly in a market I couldn't place. A child who looked like Wei but wasn't. The chronology dissolving under the weight of quantity. An old man who told me his name but I'd forgotten it before he finished. A cultivation master I'd sought out, not because I needed the technique (I already knew more than he could teach) but because I wanted the vocabulary for what my body already did. He'd been proud to have such an apt student. He'd died believing he'd created something.

Gone. All of them. Dead. They had reached the ending I couldn't reach, the natural conclusion of every face I'd ever seen, and I had walked on. I'd walked past them as I walked past stones and trees and rivers.

The pattern you had named.

Proximity. Attachment. Loss. Continuation. The four-beat rhythm of my existence. Get close, feel something, lose it, keep going. Repeat. Across centuries. Across millennia. The pattern was not chosen. It was structural, the inevitable product of an immortal consciousness moving through a mortal world. Things near me lived. Things near me died. I continued.

And with every loss, every face that stopped, every name forgotten, the belief deepened. *Everything I love dies.* Each loss confirmed the conviction. Each confirmation made the next loss more likely, because my belief shaped reality and reality produced loss.

A spiral. Downward. Self-reinforcing. Millennia deep.

You sat next to me. You'd arrived without announcement, walked across the field, settled in the grass, and put a bowl of rice between us. Xiǎohú circled you twice and lay down against my thigh. The bowl sat there. The rice cooled. Neither of us touched it.

You didn't speak.

You sat. Your presence, mortal and temporary, the warmth of a human body aging in real time, was the anchor.

The faces continued. I let them come. Let them pass. Let the tide of many thousand years wash through me and recede.

Loneliness.

So complete that it no longer registered as loneliness, because I had no reference point for anything else. I hadn't known I was lonely because I'd always been lonely, and immortality was the same condition in a different vocabulary.

The rice cooled.

The shadows moved. Time passed, mortal time, measured by the angle of light, the temperature of rice, and the slow progression of a day through its phases. I sat in the empty field letting the memories flow. The grass grew and the world went on around a woman who was remembering how long she'd been alone.

Eventually, the tide receded.

The faces dimmed. The losses stepped back from the foreground. What was left was the residue, the sediment of millennia compressed into an afternoon, the exhaustion of a consciousness that had just reviewed its entire portfolio and found it heavy.

The rice was cold.

You hadn't touched it either. You'd sat through the entire tide, patient. Your face was worried. Mine was tired.

I picked up the bowl. Rice was rice.

I ate.

Xiǎohú's ear twitched in her sleep.

You stood. Brushed grass from your knees.

"Their camp is still out there," you said. "South. I saw the smoke yesterday."

You walked toward the southern ridge. Your pack was light. Your steps had direction.

I didn't ask. The field was enough company. Xiǎohú was warm against my leg.
