The mountain path climbed, patient and sustained, an incline that gained altitude without rushing. Switchbacks through pine, the trees thinning as we rose. Spring had arrived while I wasn't paying attention: blossoms on the lower branches, pink-white, snow if you squinted and blossoms if you didn't, the annual insistence of biology doing its work regardless of who watched.

The air was cold.

The cold had been sharp since the breach and would stay so. But this cold was different. This cold was pleasant, crisp and clean, the cold of altitude in spring, air that tastes like it had been washed and put out to dry. I breathed it in, and the breathing was an act of small pleasure rather than survival.

You walked three steps behind me.

I stopped and turned.

You were there, walking staff in one hand, the other hooked through your pack strap, wearing the neutral concentration of a man navigating terrain. You stopped because I stopped, your walking speed not your own but mine.

"You're still walking behind me," I said.

You looked at the gap between us. At me. At the gap again, measuring it with the precision of a man for whom measurement was a profession.

"You walk fast," you said.

It wasn't a complaint or an excuse. It was a statement of fact, delivered deadpan, your humor living in objectively true observations that happened to be entertaining. Your face almost cracked, a ripple that appeared and vanished.

You, the scholar who had followed an immortal into the mountains because your concept of a reasonable life decision differed fundamentally from that of ordinary people.

The moment was lighter than anything since you followed me through the woods. Not light yet, but lighter, the difference between a stone you carry and a stone you carry well. The weight was all still there. But the carrying was better. The load had not decreased. The shoulders had adjusted.

"Come beside me." I motioned you closer.

You looked at me, then walked the three steps and stood beside me. The gap closed.

We walked.

Side by side, the path barely wide enough for two. Your shoulder near mine, not touching, the margin of a man who treated physical proximity as a calibrated variable. Xiǎohú trotted ahead, tail a low curve, at an efficient pace.

A sheet of snowmelt lay across the trail, thin and still, the temporary mirror spring makes from winter's leavings. My face was in it for a single step. The water didn't care whose reflection it held. For the first time in a long time, neither did I.

The blossoms. The air. The beauty of a mountain in spring that existed independently of anyone's capacity to appreciate it but was better when appreciated.

I had the tools.

The knowledge sat in me like the cold sat in the air, present, clear, available. I knew what needed to happen. But the millennia stood between knowing and doing, layers upon layers compressed by time into something geological, the conviction-strata of immortality, each layer a century of being-this-way, each century a weight that made the layer beneath it harder. You didn't dismantle geology overnight. You didn't dismantle it by wanting to.

You dismantled it as it was made: slowly, through forces that didn't care about timelines.

"The blossoms are early this year," you said.

"You track that?"

"I track everything."

Of course you did. The man who tracked blossoms, temperature fluctuations, and the migration schedules of birds you could name in three languages. Data was devotion. Devotion was how you loved.

We walked beside each other through early blossoms, through cold air, through a morning that carried the weight of everything we knew and was still lighter than the mornings before it.

Xiǎohú found something in the underbrush and went after it.

The path climbed.
