We were walking. The trail wound through pines, as ambiguous as mountain paths always are, somewhere between road and suggestion. Xiǎohú trotted ahead, tail low, on the casual patrol of an animal that hadn't consulted the humans about direction. We followed. We always followed.

You walked next to me. Your stride was the measured, three-quarter pace of a man who had calibrated his walking speed to match mine despite longer legs and more efficient biomechanics. As if I were the slower one. I wasn't. But I walked deliberately at the pace I chose, so you could match it, and neither of us had ever discussed the arrangement.

"The second voice," you said. "How does it sound?"

You said it casually, tossed over the shoulder of a more ordinary sentence, curiosity in the tone of someone making conversation.

The trail narrowed. Pine roots crossed the path like knuckles, and the ground was uneven. I stepped over a root while I considered.

"It sounds a lot like me."

You nodded. Walking. Looking past me.

I adjusted. "More precisely, like I sound when I'm not thinking."

You were quiet for four steps. I counted without meaning to, a hyper-awareness the field had installed and that hadn't fully worn off. Four steps, pine needles, dry ground crunching, Xiǎohú's tail disappearing around a bend.

"That makes sense," you said.

You were watching the trail, but the hand not holding your walking staff was doing the curl-and-release. You would write it down tonight.

"How so?" I asked.

Xiǎohú reappeared on the trail ahead, paused, and looked back at us. The slow creatures were still following. She turned and trotted on.

"Even though it is a separate part of you. It is still you."

"It was never separate," I said. The thought arrived as speech before it finished as thought, the mouth acting as a pressure valve while the interior landscape reconfigured. "None of it was separate. It was a deeper part of me, the part that knew. But it was me. I just didn't know."

You listened.

"The part that knew and decided I wasn't ready. So it packaged the knowledge into fragments, compressed them, pushed them through whatever cracks the wall allowed. Just a few words at a time. *Not now. Keep going. Enough.* It was—she was—I was protecting myself from collapsing under the weight of what I am. What I did to myself."

My voice was steady, the steadiness of someone describing something they'd accepted rather than performing composure. The metallic taste was there, faint, the low background hum that had been almost constant for weeks. I'd stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing a river when you've walked beside it long enough.

"She sounds like me when I'm not thinking because she is me when I'm not thinking. Not just a part. Me. The deep self. The original. The one who existed before the wall, before the whole apparatus of self-protection was constructed. She's been talking this whole time. I just attributed the voice to something external. Something other. Because that was the easier assumption."

Four more steps. A bird. The silence that follows an admission that has been building for years.

"You said she *decided,*" you said.

I looked at you.

"You said the deeper part decided you weren't ready. Decided to package the knowledge. Decided to protect."

"Yes."

"That implies agency."

I considered. The trail climbed. The pines thinned into something less managed, the tree line pulling back to reveal a wider sky.

"Agency, yes, but internal," I said. "Below conscious decision-making. The way the body decides to flinch before the conscious mind registers the threat. The intelligence of the organism. Faster than thought, deeper than thought, operating on protocols the surface self doesn't have access to."

You nodded and walked. Your hand stilled, the curl-and-release resolved, the processing complete or at least paused.

"Self-organization," you said.

I waited.

"The whole of you, organizing around the need to survive, allocating knowledge and experience to different levels based on what each level can carry."

You said it as if you were commenting on the weather or the quality of the trail.

"Yes," I said.

We walked. Xiǎohú was waiting at the next bend, sitting, tail wrapped around her paws, after several hundred heartbeats of being ready to move while two bipeds talked.

You reached for your water, drank, and offered me the skin. I drank. The water tasted of leather and mountain, the mineral bite of streams that ran through granite.

"I have to write that down tonight," you said.

"I know."

A smile started on your face. It was there and then it wasn't, like a ripple.
