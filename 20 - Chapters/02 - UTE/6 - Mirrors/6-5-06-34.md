The day had become night and you were asleep.

You'd come back shortly after dusk, pack heavier than when you'd left, expression closed. No explanation offered. None requested. You'd built the fire, eaten something from your pack without conversation, and shut down with the efficiency of someone who'd found what they were looking for and needed time to absorb it.

Good. What I was about to do needed to happen without an audience. It was an experiment, and experiments conducted under observation produce different results than experiments conducted alone.

I picked up the knife.

Your knife. Small, practical, the blade kept sharp because you maintained your tools the way you maintained everything: carefully, regularly, on the principle that the condition of your instruments reflected the condition of your thinking.

I didn't hesitate.

Hesitation was for people who weren't sure what they were testing. I was sure. The hypothesis was clear: *My belief in my own invulnerability operates automatically, involuntarily, continuously. This belief will prevent the wound from persisting. The wound will close. The blood will vanish. The skin will be unmarked.*

I drew the blade across my left index finger.

Pain.

The pain was real, sharp and immediate, the honest signal of nerve endings meeting an edge. My hand flinched. Since the wall of numbness had opened, everything arrived unfiltered. The blade's edge. The skin's resistance. The moment when resistance failed, the blade went in, and the blood began.

Blood.

Red and warm. Arterial, bright, oxygenated, the color painters never quite reproduce because blood's red is a living red, a red that comes from chemistry and motion.

The wound closed while I watched. The edges moved, found each other, and joined. The gap the blade had opened sealed. Not scarred, not healed, SEALED, as if the wound had been an error and the error was being corrected. The blood on my finger thinned, paled, vanished, absorbed back through skin that was no longer broken.

My finger was perfect. The same lines, the same nail, the same unremarkable anatomy of a digit that had never been cut. Retroactively undone.

I hadn't healed. I'd been un-wounded. I sat with the distinction and turned it over. Healing was a process. What my body did was a revision. The wound had existed and then had never existed. The blood had appeared and then had never appeared. My body didn't repair damage, it denied damage, with the same absolutism that denied trees their original positions, water its original direction, and reality its original configuration.

I couldn't stop being immortal.

The cliff had told me. The flame had told me. But the knife made it undeniable. Closing flesh and vanishing blood showed with brutal clarity that my immortality was not a status I maintained but a condition I was.

Not doing. Being.

You don't change what you are by wanting to change what you are. You don't become mortal by willing mortality. You don't override bedrock by painting the surface. The belief was not in my mind. It was in my structure, in the foundation that preceded thought and exceeded will and constituted the self that thought and willed.

I held the knife up to the firelight. The blade was clean. No blood, because the blood had never existed. The knife was the same knife it had been before I'd used it, because my body had retroactively denied the use.

I put the knife down.

The experiment was finished, the hypothesis confirmed, a truth tested that didn't need tests because it was older than the tools used to test it.

I went to the stream.

In the dark, by starlight, I knelt and washed my hands. The water was cold and bit at my skin, always biting now, the cold the crack had given me. I washed the blood from my finger. The blood that wasn't there. I washed anyway, the way you wash after contact with something that leaves no visible residue but changes you nonetheless.

The washing was ritual, not sanitation. I needed the water to carry something away. Not blood. The knowledge. The certainty. The experimentally verified understanding that I could not stop being what I was.

The frustration was different this time.

It was not the empty frustration of the early days, the hollow, post-guilt numbness that had frozen the engine and stopped the anomalies. This frustration was hot. It was the frustration of someone who had been given a puzzle and tried every piece, and every piece fit everywhere except the place it needed to fit.

I knew everything: what I was, how it worked, why it worked, when it started, what maintained it. I had the complete map and the full diagnosis. I had every piece of information that should have been sufficient to produce change.

And nothing changed.

Knowing wasn't enough.

Understanding wasn't enough.

The gap between comprehending and transforming was wider than the cliff, hotter than the flame, sharper than the knife. I could know myself completely and still be unable to change what I knew. Self-awareness is not self-transformation. The map is not the territory.

I washed my hands in the stream and the water carried nothing away because there was nothing to carry. I knew this and I washed anyway.

Morning came. You found me at the stream, still awake, wet to the elbows, dark circles under my eyes from a night spent at the intersection of knowing and not being able to.

"Have you slept at all?" you said.

"No."

You nodded. You'd known. Or guessed, the way you guessed everything: pattern, inference.

You sat next to me. The stream ran past us in the clear, cold light of a mountain dawn.

"If knowing isn't enough," I said. "What is?"

You didn't answer. The question wasn't for you. It was for the stream and the morning and the woman sitting in both.

You sat there and let the stream be loud enough for both of us.
