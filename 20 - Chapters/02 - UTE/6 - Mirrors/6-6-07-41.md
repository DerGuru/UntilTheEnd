The rice burned. You'd been distracted, notebook open, the pen moving with the urgency of a man who needed to capture something before it evaporated. You'd positioned the pot wrong, or the fire had shifted, or the universe had arranged a minor domestic catastrophe despite the effort you'd put into preventing exactly this.

The smell reached me first: acrid, caramelized and then charred, starch meeting too much heat for too long. The pot, when you pulled it off the fire by its cloth-wrapped handle, had a dark crust at the bottom. Still edible, but definitely burned, the kind of burned that announced itself in every bite.

"Zāo Gāo!" you exclaimed, staring into the pot like a man who had committed an error of calibration and suffered a localized reduction in pride. It was objectively funny.

Still I did not laugh.

I looked at the burned rice and I was in a different place.

Wei's kitchen. Our kitchen, the small room in the house we shared. Wei, crouching, held a bowl of rice burned worse than yours, darker and crustier, the catastrophe of a fourteen-year-old boy who didn't pay attention and stoked the flames too hot.

His grin. That grin took up too much of his face and was disproportionate to whatever had prompted it. It made you want to grin back.

He ate a bite. Chewed. The crunch was audible.

"Not bad," he said.

"It's burned."

"It's crunchy."

He pushed a bowl toward me. Burned rice. Both hands, a gesture somewhere between offering and challenge, the physical language of a boy who expressed everything through motion because words were too slow.

"Be quiet," he said.

That be quiet meant I should stop analyzing, stop evaluating, stop being the person who finds the flaw before tasting the thing. Stop thinking. Eat.

I'd eaten it.

The memory was complete, every detail preserved in the amber of an immortal mind while time dissolved around it. His hands. The bowl. The grin. The crunch. The silence between a boy who had burned rice and a woman who was eating it.

Wei had never tried to be anything but himself.

The recognition arrived and sat. Unlike the philosophical one at the river, this one was personal and exact: I looked at a dead boy and saw the thing I'd been trying to understand, held by someone who understood it without trying.

Wei had never gripped. Never held. Never willed himself into a shape. He was stubborn, the most stubborn person I'd met in any era, stubborn enough to bend reality through sheer refusal to bend himself, but the stubbornness wasn't will. It was nature. He was stubborn the way the river was wet.

He had just been.

Alive. Present. Unanalyzed. A boy who burned rice, ate it anyway, grinned, said be quiet, and meant it. Not once in all the time I knew him did he ask what he should be. He was what he was. The water flowing because flowing was its nature. The open hand that didn't know it was open because it had never been a fist.

That was what I had to learn.

The practice. The lived, daily practice of being what you are without gripping yourself into shape. Wei had known this at fourteen. He had known it while burning rice. Or maybe he hadn't known it at all and just didn't care to be anything else. The result was the same.

And Wei was dead.

And the letting-go might show me why.

The question of whether my belief had killed him was still open, a stone in the river that the water flowed around but could not move.

"You're smiling," you said.

I looked at you. The burned pot in your hand. The rice, salvageable. The evening, ordinary.

"No," I said, surprised by myself.

But I was. The reflection in the river had smiled like this, with something underneath.

I was smiling because the universe, despite everything, despite eons of loss and the unbearable weight of immortality, still contained moments where someone burned the rice, then turned that expression on you.

"Eat," I said.

You served. The burned parts were distributed without comment, some to your bowl, some to mine, a democratic allocation of failure. We ate. The crunch was there. It was terrible, and it was fine.

Xiǎohú appeared from wherever she had been and sat near the fire. She looked at the pot and sneezed.
