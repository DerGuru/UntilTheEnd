On the fourth evening, we sat by a fire you had built. I watched its light on your hands, resting on your knees. You waited until I looked up before breaking the silence.

"May I say something?"

The question was formal, deliberately and precisely so. You had learned that the things worth saying needed permission first, space first, consent for the silence they broke.

I looked at you. The looking was the consent. You read it. Months together had taught us to read each other's silence.

"If you did it," you said.

Pause.

You paused over the words. Your mouth settled before you spoke again. You used words with a surgeon's care, aware of the damage they could do. The same question might open something or close it beyond reach, and you took time with the one you had chosen.

"If you did it, did you know?"

Seven words. You waited while I considered them.

I looked at the fire. You had built it from dry tinder, feeding it carefully until the larger pieces caught. Since the changes stopped, all our fires had been yours. You made them patiently, without requiring me to believe anything about the flames.

"No," I said.

"But everything I love dies."

I said it in the voice I had used for the stone's position. It was a fact to me. I had believed it longer than most civilizations had existed, long enough that I couldn't hear how it sounded until I saw your face.

Your face changed.

Your eyes stayed on mine. You had heard that certainty by the stones, then watched the ground change. You were returning to something you recognized. I waited for the next question, but you let me finish the first answer.

"No," I said again. "I didn't know."

You were quiet for some time. The fire crackled. You looked toward it, weighing what I had said against months of observation.

"Then it wasn't murder," you said.

Pause.

"It was a pattern."

I held that word against the question that had occupied me for days. Pattern. Water follows a contour without choosing where it goes. Had I followed one?

I could remember wanting him alive while certain I would lose him. If the two were connected, the connection might have existed under any intention I could name. I sat with my hands closed, trying to see the difference you were asking me to see.

"Murder requires intent," you said. Your voice was precise, a physician's voice delivering a diagnosis. "You had no intent. You didn't know you were doing it. You didn't know you could do it."

"That doesn't—"

"It does," you said. "Not because it erases the result. Wei is still dead. The result is the same. But the category is different. And categories matter. Because murder is a destination. You arrive there and there's nothing to do except carry the weight. But a pattern—"

You leaned forward. The firelight moved across your face. You had been considering what to say for four days. Now you held my gaze until you were certain I was listening.

"A pattern can be broken."

The words gave me somewhere else to look.

"I don't know if you did it," you said. "Neither do you. Neither does anyone. That's the truth. The honest, ugly, permanent truth. You don't know. You might never know. And that, carrying that, is terrible. I understand that."

You paused.

"But carrying an unanswerable question is different from carrying an unforgivable answer. One you can walk with. The other buries you."

I breathed.

Breathing was easier than it had been in four days. It still hurt, but I could complete a breath without holding it halfway. The question was still there. Wei was still dead. My belief might have killed him, with all the cruelty that uncertainty carried. I could hear you while bearing it.

But a pattern offered something that guilt couldn't. A direction. A pattern could be broken.

I chewed on your words.

"Patterns, not murder," I said, testing the words in my mouth.

"Patterns, not murder," you confirmed.

"That doesn't absolve me."

"No. It gives you something to do."

You had left the question of forgiveness alone. I looked at my hands. You were asking what I could do with them now.

Your fire burned. Wood settled into coals; heat reached my knees. You had made something useful while I struggled with the impossible.

Something loosened in my shoulders before I took another breath. It was small, but it held when I breathed again. The weight was still there. I could also imagine standing up with it.

I could breathe.

I could walk.

"Break the pattern," you said. "That's your next step. Not guilt. Not answers. Breaking the pattern."

I looked at the fire you had built, burning steadily through the wood you had gathered.

Xiǎohú appeared from the dark. She pushed her head under my hand, lifting it until my palm rested between her ears. Her fur was cold from the night air. Her ears flicked against my skin.

I stroked her. Once. Twice. The motion was easy once I began it. My fingers passed through the cold fur while she held still under my hand.

My breathing eased.

Xiǎohú settled against my knee. Warm now. Present.

I nodded.
