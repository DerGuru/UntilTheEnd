Morning mist still clung to the lower branches. The fire had burned down to white ash overnight; you'd rebuilt it without waking me. The smell of reheated congee pulled me out of sleep. Xiǎohú was somewhere upstream, hunting or not. I knew little about what fox-spirits did when they left the camp.

"I've been thinking," you said.

"Didn't you do that yesterday?"

"I've been thinking more."

You had arranged three stones on the packed earth, from a small one to one that would fill your palm. An arm's length separated each from the next. You had cleared enough space around them to make any movement visible.

"Tests," you said.

I looked from the stones to you.

"You want me to move stones."

"I want you to try to move stones."

You had the intent look you wore over your notebooks. Their careful observations had proved more reliable than my explanations.

"First," you said. You pointed at the smallest stone. "Will it to move."

"That's not how it worked."

"Try anyway."

You wanted to establish what would fail before testing what might succeed. I had watched you make those comparisons in your notes. This morning I was the one being asked to supply the failure.

I looked at the rough gray stone, about the size of a child's fist. I gathered my intention toward it as cultivation had taught me. I wanted it to move. I held that want firmly, pressing it toward the patch of earth past it.

Nothing.

The stone sat where it was.

I concentrated harder. If strength of intention were enough, the stone should have moved. The same shadow lay under it when I relaxed.

Nothing.

"Maybe louder?" you said. One eyebrow went up.

"It's not a qi attack."

"Okay. Try it the other way."

I remembered the certainty I had felt in the village square. Thirty cultivators had vanished when I believed them gone.

I breathed out.

I let the effort go with the breath and simply looked at the ground.

I looked from the stone to a patch of bare earth two paces away. It lay lower than the spot you had placed it on.

I became certain that the stone was on that bare patch.

I blinked.

The stone was there.

It sat two paces from its original position, settled firmly into the bare earth. The soil around its base was undisturbed. Moss showed along its underside, green at the edges with older brown growth beneath. That pattern had taken years to form. I had seen a clean stone a moment before.

Every mark around it said that the stone had always been there. I examined the old moss against the undisturbed earth. Its new history was more thorough than its old one. I had known cultivators with less commitment to their cover stories.

You walked to the stone.

You approached slowly, keeping your eyes on the new moss. You knelt, lifted the stone, and turned it over. Your fingers pressed into the growth on its underside. You rubbed a little between them before looking back at the earth.

"The—it was always—" You stopped, shaking your head. You looked toward the original position, then down at the moss in your fingers. For once, you had kept hold of both versions. I waited while you compared them.

You set the stone down, stood, and looked at me.

"Now me," you said.

You planted yourself in front of the second stone, took a deep breath, and let your shoulders drop. You watched it with the concentration I had used.

"The stone is there," you said, pointing to a spot one pace away. Your voice was firm. Your finger stayed on the empty ground, holding the claim with everything you had.

Nothing happened.

The stone sat where you had placed it. You lowered your hand to the spot and straightened again.

You tried again. This time you glanced at the first stone before fixing your attention on the second. You had seen it happen. You had touched the proof.

Nothing.

You stared at the stone. Then at me. Then at the stone.

"The universe," you said, "does not care about my opinion."

I looked at the unmoved stone while you considered this unfairness. You had followed my example, but the same act had failed in your hands.

It was mine.

A small brown warbler landed on the branch above us. It preened a wing feather and left.

"Unfair," you said.

You let your hand fall beside your knee.

The corner of my mouth moved. You noticed, which nearly spoiled it. Since the village I hadn't come that close to smiling.

"Try the big one," you said.

I looked from the largest stone to a point three paces away. I believed it was there.

It was there.

Moss edged its base, another old history around its new position.

Your notebook lay closed. You looked from one moved stone to the other without reaching for it. This from a man who had once attempted to catalog fog.

"It's not will," you said.

"No."

"It's belief."

"Yes."

"Your belief. Not mine. Not anyone's. Yours."

I looked at the second stone still in its original place. We had tried the same thing. Only my certainty had changed the ground.

The mist had lifted. Sunlight warmed my neck from above the ridge. Xiǎohú barked once downstream. I couldn't tell whether she had caught something or only startled it.

You picked up the congee pot and scraped the last of it into my bowl without asking. Metal rasped against metal in the quiet after the experiment. I took the bowl. Whatever we had learned, breakfast was getting cold.
