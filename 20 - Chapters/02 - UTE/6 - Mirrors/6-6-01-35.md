You were sewing your pack.

The strap had torn during the approach. When I'd sent you back and you'd gone reluctantly, you had gripped it with strength that exceeded the strap's engineering. The leather had split in a clean, diagonal tear, the failure line of material subjected to stress it wasn't made for.

You sat by the fire with a needle and thread and the focused expression of a man performing surgery on luggage. The needle went in and out. The thread pulled. The strap's edges came together under your hands.

I sat by the fire too, scratching Xiǎohú behind her ears, and felt no pressure.

The absence was strange. Since I met Wei, there had always been something external that gave shape to the internal process. It had pushed. Now there was nothing. The sect was no more. The anomalies had quieted. The wall was transparent. Every outside pressure had been removed, and what remained was the internal landscape, unobstructed.

Just me, the question, and a man sewing his pack.

You finished your repairs and glanced at me sideways. The glance said, *You're sitting there doing nothing.* You held out the needle and a length of thread. I took them without thinking. My hands found my sleeve and the tear at the elbow I'd been ignoring for a while.

I could have closed the tear without thread. A thought, a moment of conviction, and the fabric would knit itself, like my clothes had dried after the river. But that felt like cheating. Pushing steel through cloth felt more honest. Also the needle was already in my hand and the thread was already cut. Go for it.

With no more scritches on offer, Xiǎohú decided it was time for a little hunting and took to the underbrush.

I hadn't sewn in decades. Centuries. I didn't know. The action was not in my recent inventory of behaviors. It belonged to an earlier version of me, one that performed domestic tasks and maintained clothing, before the walking became permanent and made maintenance meaningless.

The needle went in and out. The thread pulled. The tear's edges met. I was repairing.

Fixing what was torn. Closing what was open. The domestic version of what I'd been trying to do to myself: close the gap, fix the thing that was broken. Except that sewing a sleeve was simple. You saw the tear. You had thread. You put the thread through the fabric and pulled. The fabric came together. Done.

Why couldn't the self be sewn?

The question was absurd, which was kind of an answer in itself. Selves aren't fabric. Beliefs aren't tears. The needle that closes cloth doesn't reach down to where convictions live.

Yet the sewing was real. The thread was real. The repaired sleeve was real. And the work itself, hands busy with a broken thing, the quiet satisfaction of edges meeting, was something.

I was productive. Without deciding to be.

I'd worked on your meridians that morning. The routine had shifted, shorter sessions now and less resistance, the channels holding their shape between cleanings instead of collapsing back. Your breathing was deeper afterward, the breathing of a body whose pinched channels had been released.

You sewed. I sewed. The fire cracked. Xiǎohú came back and napped at the fire's edge, curled tight, eyes closed. The afternoon was warm, quiet, and domestic. The domesticity was not a distraction from the work but the work itself.

"There's a story they tell in the lowlands," you said, not looking up. You filled silence the way you filled your notebook, methodically, without urgency. "About a woman who made a hundred warriors vanish. Swallowed their souls, supposedly. Consumed them. They say she opened her mouth and the soldiers disappeared into her."

The needle stopped in my hand.

"Who tells this?" I said.

"Traders. Innkeepers. The version varies. In one, she's ten feet tall. In another, she has silver eyes. In all of them, she consumes."

I pulled the thread through the fabric. The stitch was tight. Too tight.

"I moved them," I said. "Not consumed. Moved. From one place to another. Most of them anyways. And there was no mouth involved. No swallowing. No souls."

"Besides." I continued. "It was yesterday and I saw no other witnesses."

You looked at me. It was the long, still look of a man who had stopped sewing.

"The story I was talking about is over five hundred years old," you said. Quietly.

"Oh." I recognized my mistake.

"I guess it was a border dispute then. One of many." I said. "Two villages. Maybe sixty men between them, not a hundred. They had farming tools, not swords. I darkened the sky over the valley and created shadows around them and me, thick enough to blind. Then wind. A column of it, strong enough to lift a man off the ground."

The needle went through the fabric. The stitch was steady.

"I dropped them across the countryside. Some of them over a fair distance." I paused. "They were farmers. They weren't cultivators. They didn't know how to land."

The fire cracked. You said nothing.

"Not all of them died," I said. "But enough. Enough for a story about a woman who swallows souls."

You held the needle, forgotten. The leather strap hung between your fingers.

"You're telling me," you said.

"Yes, that was me, too. Unless there was another woman who really swallowed souls centuries ago."

You returned to your pack. The needle went in and out. The strap's edges continued their reconciliation. Your face was the face of a man who had carried a theory for years and was hearing it confirmed not with evidence but with a voice that said *I* and meant it.

Xiǎohú slept. I sewed my sleeve and listened to the afternoon.

I wondered how many versions of me were being told around how many fires, and how many of them involved mouths and ten-foot women with silver eyes. And how many of those stories had started the same way, with a woman who thought she was solving a problem and found out afterward what the solution had cost.
