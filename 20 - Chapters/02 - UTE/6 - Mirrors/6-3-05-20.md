For the following days, I followed your boots along the trail. I ate when you put food in front of me. I slept after dark. I occupied each day without inhabiting it, present enough to perform the small acts that had kept me going for millennia. I couldn't say what they meant beyond getting through another morning.

The trail dropped into a valley. Thin white birches shed strips of bark. A creek ran alongside the path with the constant, low sound that fills silence without interrupting it. Xiǎohú walked ahead. Her tail swayed steadily with each step. I watched it as we descended.

Guilt left a space in the center of everything I experienced. My mind returned to it as a tongue returns to the hole left by a pulled tooth. I could look at the trees or follow a thought about the trail. Sooner or later I came back to the same absence, unable to leave it alone for long. The creek ran on around the question.

Had I killed Wei?

The question didn't need to repeat in words. I had asked it, and I could no longer remember the comfort of not asking. It was as permanent as the evidence of my power. I passed another birch. The creek ran on. The question was as constant now as the metallic taste or the vibration in my teeth had been. The vibration was silent. I had only the question to carry in its place.

You cooked with the determined precision of a man who had decided that the world's most essential response to catastrophe was rice. You made it three times a day. You seasoned it with whatever you found on the trailside: wild garlic one morning, young nettles the next. You pinched off the green tips with your thumbnail and dropped them into the pot without ceremony. You left the other herbs alone.

Xiǎohú ate next to me from your second cup, which now served as her bowl. It held scraps of whatever you'd cooked. She ate neatly, methodically, licking the rim clean before returning to her spot by the fire. The spot was always the same distance from me. Close enough to touch if I reached. I didn't reach.

You talked about weather.

"Rain tonight," you'd say. Or, "The wind's shifted east."

You counted provisions.

"Three days of rice. Five of dried meat. We'll need water tomorrow."

The mornings were cold. Mist pooled on the valley floor and our breath made smaller versions of it. You packed with your usual economy: bedroll tight, pot nested inside the pack, and firestones in the side pocket where your hand could find them without looking. I watched you do it without offering to help.

Two people continuing to exist needed food and shelter. You counted our provisions as though we must inevitably use them up. Could I believe us fed? Could I believe the rice replenished, the provisions permanent?

Could I believe Wei alive?

The thought arrived and the hole received it, unchanged. The walking continued.

The anomalies stopped. This was the detail that frightened me more than the guilt itself. Trees stood where I had seen them the previous evening, their roots under the same patches of ground. Nothing new appeared with a history I couldn't remember.

My beliefs had been changing the world around me constantly. Now grief occupied me so completely that I could summon no other certainty besides the question. Whatever had made those changes had gone still.

The sect on the ridge waited.

They were still there at a patient distance, a hundred and twelve figures holding their parallel path. But the feeding had stopped. The changes that had nourished their cultivation had ceased. They followed a source that gave them nothing new.

I registered them as I registered everything else. They were on the ridge, waiting. They would go on waiting. I turned my eyes back to the path.

A leaf detached from the birch above us. It spiraled down slowly, catching light on each rotation. Xiǎohú followed it with her head tilted, her ears swiveling toward something that made no sound. It landed on her nose. She sneezed, shook it off, and looked at me. I had watched the whole incident without reaching to help.

I had no explanation. For anything.

I combed my hair.

I didn't know where the gesture came from. I sat by the dead fire, pulling my fingers through the strands. I counted each stroke. One hundred. Two hundred. Three hundred. The counting was the point. I could assign a number to a movement, finish it, and start again. Here was control I could exercise without deciding what it meant.

You watched me count. You offered neither a comb nor a comment. You had seen me look at my hands often enough over the past days. Now I had found something to do with them.

Four hundred.

I stopped. Four hundred was as arbitrary a place to stop as the first stroke had been a place to begin. I had chosen both.

I put my hands in my lap. The hands that shaped reality. The hands that might have shaped Wei's death.

The hole received the thought. The hole was unchanged.

You started a fire with flint and patience. You repeated the same movements until the tinder caught. I watched. The flame needed none of the certainty I couldn't produce.

The fire caught.

We sat.

We sat with the fire between us. The rice would need cooking again; our water skins would need filling. The question sat beside those small necessities. Your breathing carried across the flames. We had nothing to say, but we were there together.
