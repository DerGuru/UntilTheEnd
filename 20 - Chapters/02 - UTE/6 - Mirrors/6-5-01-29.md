The trail climbed steeply between rocks, narrow enough that a goat would have found it easier than either of us. We went up in silence for half a watch. Your breathing deepened behind me. Each bend demanded more of our legs before showing the next stretch.

"I want to try something," I said. "At the top."

"What kind of something?"

"The kind where I stand at the edge and tell myself I'm mortal."

You climbed three steps behind me. Always three. "You think that will work?"

"No."

"Then why?"

"Because I need to know that it doesn't."

"Why can't you do it somewhere else? Does it have to be a cliff?"

"I don't know. It just feels right." I looked up at the ridge. "And the view must be amazing."

You were quiet for a while. The trail switchbacked through pine and scree. Then you stopped, reached into your jacket pocket, and took out a small gray stone, worn round by water. A child might keep it simply because it fitted a palm perfectly.

"I've been carrying this for a while now," you said. "It's from Wei's garden."

I knew that gray-blue surface. Wei had found it at the shrine in the hills and placed it between the dock grass and the lemongrass. "Good stone," he had said, patting it. Smooth, palm-sized, the right stone in a garden of wrong plants.

I took it from your palm. It was warm from months against your chest. Small and heavy, as things are heavy when they carry more than their weight.

I closed my hand around it.

You sat on a flat stone at the side of the trail. From there the cliff face rose above the rocks below, the whole sheer drop I was walking toward. You took out your water skin.

"I'll wait here," you said.

You didn't explain. You didn't need to. Some things need to be done alone. And some things need a witness who watches from a distance.

I kept climbing. The trail narrowed, switchbacked through loose scree, and opened onto a small plateau with a sheer drop on one side. Strong, cold wind pulled at my clothes.

I stood at the edge and yelled into it.

"I. Am. Mortal."

The wind carried my declaration away and went on blowing over the plateau after my voice faded. I stood waiting for some change. The words had been loud enough. They had been clear. Nothing about the ledge changed.

Below the cliff, water moved among rocks. A falling body would cross that distance and strike them. A stone would. A mortal would. One more step made that ending available to anyone standing here, as plainly as the ledge was beneath my feet.

Except mine.

I opened my hand to look at Wei's stone. Its gray-blue surface was smooth and warm. He had found it, patted it, and placed it among his herbs. A space he had decided was important deserved something beautiful.

I let it go.

It fell. Two paces. Three.

Then it was in my hand again. Warm. Smooth. The same weight pressed my palm, the same rounded shape. As if it had never left.

I hadn't reached for it. My arm hadn't moved. The stone had returned to a certainty deeper than the act of letting go: some part of me believed I would keep it. That was the same hold that kept me alive.

I closed my fingers around it. Wei's stone. Still warm.

It was the same as at the other cliff. I still believed I wouldn't end. The belief lay under the declaration, the words, and the intention I could summon. I could alter what I said without reaching what sustained me.

But you can't change a building's foundation by repainting its walls.

I stepped back from the edge and put the stone in my pocket.

You waited on the trail fifty paces below. From above you looked small among the rocks, a mortal figure with a water skin.

I sat down. Cross-legged, looking over the drop. The view was worth the climb. Valley floors in green and brown, a river threading silver through the distance, ridgelines stacking behind ridgelines until the farthest ones dissolved into haze. A view that makes you forget the size of what you are and reminds you of the size of what you're looking at.

The wind pulled at my hair. The stone pressed against my thigh through the fabric.

If declaring didn't work, stillness might. I could settle past the argument, follow my breathing, and reach the belief beneath it. Meditation could take me somewhere words couldn't. I wanted to touch the certainty where it lived, before turning it into another declaration.

I closed my eyes.

The rock was firm under me while the wind moved. The stone pressed against my thigh. I remembered a boy patting it in a garden. I still believed some things were worth keeping, even while trying to understand why I couldn't let them go.

I sat for a while, but nothing changed. I couldn't focus. Eventually I walked down to you. The path smoothed ahead of me, making the descent easy. My belief in smooth paths continued to work regardless of my frustration with it.

"Well?" you said.

"Nothing." I touched my pocket. The stone was there. "Declaring doesn't work. Meditating doesn't work. I can't even let a stone fall without catching it."

Your eyes went to my pocket, to the shape pressing through the fabric.

"Interesting," you said.

"Frustrating."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive."

We walked south. The trail stayed suspiciously smooth.
