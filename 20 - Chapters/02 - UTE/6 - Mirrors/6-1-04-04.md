Late light caught a rock face at the trail's edge. Its rough surface had become polished, almost glassy. I turned my head before I could see myself in it.

Farther on, I glanced at yellow wildflowers before looking for Xiǎohú. When I looked back, the petals were deep violet, streaked almost black at the edges. I stopped over them long enough to make certain of the color.

Then a low, resonant bird call sounded in the trees. Its three-note pattern was unfamiliar. Xiǎohú turned toward it, holding still until the final note faded.

The rock face looked worn smooth by millennia. Every flower matched the others, right down to the dark edges. You walked past them without a glance. When the bird called again, you kept your eyes on the trail. I began to wonder whether you remembered hearing it here before.

My stomach turned. Before, you had noticed. The scholars had noticed. When the ground cracked, everyone could point at the damage. We could disagree about what had caused it while still remembering the ground before it broke.

I looked back at the violet flowers until a bend hid them. Each alteration came with a history. The stone's smooth face showed its age; the tree had given you a memory of planting it. Even the witnesses could be changed.

How would I prove anything if the evidence changed with them?

You made camp at sunset. You placed the fire, laid out the bedrolls, and checked the perimeter. Since the sect had stopped being ignorable, that last task had become part of the routine. I watched you return to the fire without having to search for anything you'd set down. For once I could follow an ordinary sequence through to its end.

"The flowers had been yellow," I said.

You poked the fire with a stick without looking up. "What flowers?"

"At the trailside. The ones that are violet now."

"They've always been violet."

I knew you would say that. The flowers had always been violet for you. The tree had always been there. The stone had always faced east. The rock face had always been smooth.

Your memory agreed with every change. Mine contradicted it. I glanced at Xiǎohú, but she could give me no answer.

"Something is happening," I said.

You looked up from the fire. Light flickered across your face as the flames rose. "You've been saying variations of that sentence for quite some time now."

"This is different."

"You've said that too."

You sounded light, but you had stopped stirring the fire. Your eyes were on me. You had always been able to observe without a pencil.

"The changes are retroactive," I said. "When something changes, it doesn't just change now. It changes always. Everyone remembers the new version. Everyone's memory gets rewritten. Except mine."

You were quiet. The fire cracked. A log shifted, sending sparks upward.

"How many?" you said.

"Today? Four I can point to, if you count the rock face."

"What rock face?"

"Exactly."

You lowered the stick. This morning you had sat under a tree that hadn't been there yesterday evening. You remembered me planting it. Now you couldn't recall a rough surface on the rock face we had passed. I watched you turn toward the trail, then back to me.

You sat with that.

I liked that about you. With the stick idle in your hand, you gave an unwelcome idea room before answering.

You trusted my version over yours.

"The tree too?" you said.

"The tree too."

"I remember you planting it. I was there. Spring before last."

You said it as certainly as you'd recall an ordinary morning. But spring before last, you hadn't met me. You had never visited that clearing with me. Whatever had changed your memory had put us together before you knew I existed.

Now you remembered standing next to me with a sapling.

"I know," I said.

You looked at the fire again. The light deepened the lines of your face. The years showed in the creases at the corners of your eyes and in the shadows under them. Thirty-five years, visible in a face I had grown used to thinking of as young.

"That scares you," you said.

"Yes."

"Not the changes."

"No. No one knows except me. Reality changes; everyone remembers the new version. I'm left standing in the clearing saying that tree wasn't here yesterday. That makes me either the only one who sees the truth or the only one who's wrong."

Sparks rose from the fire. Xiǎohú lay at the camp's edge with her eyes half-closed. Occasionally her gaze shifted above the trees. I still couldn't see whatever she had been tracking all day.

"You're not wrong," you said.

"How do you know?"

"Because the things you describe as changes make sense as changes. A tree doesn't grow overnight. Flowers don't change color. Stones don't rotate in undisturbed earth. The fact that I remember them differently doesn't mean you're wrong. It means my memory was changed and yours wasn't." A pause. "Or the other way round. But the logic holds either way."

You had no memory of the yellow flowers or the bare clearing. Still, you could follow my account far enough to question your own. I watched you press the tip of the stick into the ash.

You stared at the fire and started again. "I remember planting that tree with you. Eighteen months ago. A sapling. This morning I sat under it. The trunk was wider than my arms could reach; the bark looked a century old. I didn't—I didn't even notice the contradiction. I just sat down." You looked at me. "That should bother me. The fact that it doesn't bother me is what bothers me."

"So it's my memories that have changed? But why you?" you said. "Why can you see through it?"

I didn't answer. The hands from this morning moved faintly across my vision, their light passing over the fire. Metal coated my tongue. I had grown used to the taste, though each swallow made it fresh again.

The images had come through the crack. I felt toward it, searching for the pressure that had kept so much from me for longer than I could measure. I suspected your answer lay somewhere past that barrier.

I almost connected the hands with my unchanged memory. The thought was half-formed: the two belonged together, but I couldn't yet put it into words.

The wall did nothing.

I looked down at the ash instead, dismissing the thought out of habit.
