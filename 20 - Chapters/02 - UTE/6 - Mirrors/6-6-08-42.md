Morning came slow and soft, the light filtering through the canopy in stages, as it had since the river.

Xiǎohú was already gone, hunting probably, or patrolling whatever perimeter she'd decided was hers. The fire was embers, so I rebuilt it. Found water. Set rice. The pot still wore last night's burn, and I scraped it with a stone before filling it.

You woke to the smell, sat up, and blinked like a man assembling consciousness one piece at a time. You pulled on your boots, stretched, and looked at the pot.

"I'm watching it this time," I said.

"I didn't—"

"You did."

The morning was warm. The air smelled like pine resin, damp earth, and the freshness of forests near rivers, humidity that made everything softer.

After eating, I gathered what needed washing and went to the river.

My own robe. The under-layer. Your spare shirt. The sash, creased at the knot, soft where the tail hung, the autobiography of repeated use. The cloth we used as a pot holder. Everything that could be washed, I washed.

The river was the same one. We hadn't moved far, half a day's walk downstream from yesterday's washing spot, the water wider here and shallower, the stones smaller and rounder. Xiǎohú had found a patch of sun on the far bank and lay in it with committed indolence.

I stood in the water up to my calves. The fabric was heavy with water. My hands were full.

I wrung out your shirt, set it on the flat rock I'd designated as the drying surface, and picked up the sash. I scrubbed. The motion was coming back, not the muscle memory of eons but the fresh satisfaction of a skill relearned, dormant and waking.

The sash was clean. I held it.

Wet fabric, heavy, dripping. Both hands. The weight of it pulled down, not much, the sash was light even wet, but enough. Gravity on waterlogged cloth, the most ordinary force in the world.

I opened my hands.

The sash fell into the river.

Quickly. Wetly. It hit the surface, sank halfway, caught the current, and began to move downstream. A piece of wet fabric in a river, doing what wet fabric in rivers does.

It went.

My hands were open. Empty. Air on my wet palms, the fabric's weight gone, the lightness of having held something and now not holding it. I had been holding a piece of wet cloth. I had opened my hands. The cloth had left.

The letting go was that simple.

The letting go was that hard.

Because the impulse came: reach, grab, retrieve, the automatic response of a mind and body calibrated for holding. The grip-reflex. The fist wanted to close because holding was the default, because millennia of holding had made holding feel natural and opening feel like an aberration. The impulse was strong. The impulse was screaming.

I did not close my hands.

The sash floated. Caught on a stone, spun, slipped free, continued. Two paces downstream. Three. Moving at the unhurried pace of water that had nowhere to be and eternity to get there.

"Yun."

You. Downstream. You'd been sitting on the bank with your notebook, the usual arrangement. Now you stood watching the sash approach, your expression the precise combination of concern and bemusement that accompanied most of my unconventional moments.

"We still need that," you said.

I almost laughed.

You waded in, boots already off from sitting on the bank, the water to your knees after two steps. You plucked the sash from the current with an easy motion and held it up.

"Got it," you said.

The sash dripped. You dripped. Xiǎohú on the far bank lifted her head, looked, and put it back down. Not interesting enough.

"You could have caught it," you said.

"Yes."

"You chose not to."

I looked at my hands. Still open. Still empty. The palms cooling in the air, the water evaporating, the skin tightening as it dried.

"I chose not to close my hands," I said. "It's different."

You considered this. Standing in the river, holding a wet sash, water around your knees.

"Is it?" you said.

"Choosing not to reach is still choosing. Letting go is... not choosing. It's the absence of a grip. If I had chosen to let the sash go, that would have been will. Directing the outcome through decision. But I didn't let go of it. I stopped holding."

You looked at the sash. Looked at me. Then you wrung it out, efficiently, practically, like a man who intended to use it again and had no interest in letting it complete its symbolic journey to the sea.

"You stopped holding?" you repeated.

"And it left."

"Because that's what wet sashes in rivers do?"

"Yes."

You waded back to the bank and spread the sash on your drying rock. You studied the arrangement of wet fabric, your shirt, my robe, the pot cloth, the sash, like a man assessing whether the cloth-to-sun ratio was optimal.

"Hm," you said. Looking at the sash drying on the rock. Then at me, still standing in the river. "I guess you did stop holding it. It did leave."

Not quite true, but close enough. The impulse to reach was still there, would always be there probably, the fist's memory of being a fist. But the impulse had not become action. My hands had not closed. The sash had gone.

Xiǎohú yawned on the far bank. The sun continued its slow, indifferent transit. The wet fabric drying in the warmth.

I stood in the river with empty hands and thought about the emptiness between letting go and whatever came after, the pause between one state and the next that could only be occupied by waiting.

I waited.

The water flowed through.
