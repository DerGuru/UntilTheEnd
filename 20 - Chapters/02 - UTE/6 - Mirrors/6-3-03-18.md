For three days I walked south beside you, ate what you cooked, and lay down to sleep when we stopped. The path opened ahead of us. Each newly clear stretch of ground brought me back to the question I was avoiding.

It needed only a few common words. A child could have formed the sentence. I found reasons to delay it: the next bend, the evening meal, or a place to rest. Whenever we stopped, I pressed my hands together hard enough to feel the bones.

You noticed when I broke off mid-sentence. Sometimes your eyes followed my hands as I gripped them together. You cooked, counted our provisions, and discussed the weather. I answered what I could. You left room for the rest.

On the third evening, the fire was burning.

I had believed it should be burning; there it was. The wood was already charred. I sat across from the flames, watching them move through branches that had been whole moments before. My certainty had supplied a fire with a past.

It had gone out earlier that day without a decision from me. Perhaps I had been doing that for millennia: putting fires out, then believing them back into existence. My attention wandered between one small task and the next. A certainty might change just as quietly.

Now the fire burned again. I held a hand toward its established warmth, unable to recall making it return.

The logic arrived.

What if my belief shapes reality?

I had always believed that everything I loved died. I had carried that certainty so long that I no longer thought to question it.

Wei died.

The connection became clear as I watched the flames. If I could believe a fire into existence without noticing, what else might follow a certainty that old?

My lips moved.

I formed the sentence without sound, each word shaped in my mouth. My throat tightened before I could give it breath.

I didn't say it.

You saw.

You sat on a log across the fire with your hands on your knees. When my lips moved, you leaned forward. I looked down at your hands, then back at your face.

"Yun?"

Hearing my name let me draw a breath.

"Did I kill Wei?"

Quiet. Barely audible.

Had I killed Wei?

Me?

If belief shaped reality, my certainty that everything I loved died might have reached him. I had loved Wei. If that conviction had shaped the world around him, then I had killed him.

I followed the connection without looking away from you. I had carried that certainty through helping him and watching him grow. It had been there even when I had a reason to stay. I could remember caring about him without finding a moment when I had truly believed I would be allowed to keep him.

The fire burned.

You held your breath. Your chest didn't move under your clothes. You had heard the question after watching the same tests I had. Your hands tightened on your knees.

You didn't answer.

Smoke drifted between us. I waited for you to find an objection, something I had missed. The fire crackled, and you said nothing.

Had my belief killed Wei?

Maybe.

Maybe not.

Wei had been mortal. His body bruised, broke, and could die. He would have died eventually, as other mortals did. The cultivator who struck him had his own reasons for being there. I held those facts apart from what I believed about loss.

Could I be certain of that?

Perhaps my belief had helped create the circumstances. I had just watched water change its course to match a certainty. A stone had acquired years of moss. If a place could change around me so completely, I couldn't dismiss the possibility that danger had reached him for the same reason.

I didn't know.

I pressed my hands together under the edge of my sleeves. I could find reasons to accuse myself, then reasons to doubt them. Neither gave me an answer I could keep.

An answer would at least have given me something definite to bear. Instead, every explanation left room for the other one. I looked at the fire I had brought into being. I would carry the question wherever I went, with that ordinary warmth to remind me why I couldn't dismiss it.

Across the fire, you were still silent. I loosened my hands. The question held.
