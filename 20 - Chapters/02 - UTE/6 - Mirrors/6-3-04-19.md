The fire was still burning when the memory broke through that night. The grass was wet at my knees while Wei's death came back in its entirety. This was whole. This was complete. The wall had finally lost the fight.

The self-protection didn't intervene. Perhaps it couldn't. Perhaps the wall had been built to prevent that question, and asking it had broken something. The question had weakened it more than the creation memories, more than the convergence, more than anything else. The memory came through faster than I could turn away.

Wei died at the settlement gate.

The settlement's eastern gate, wooden and weathered.

He had been standing. I hadn't seen that; I'd been on the west wall, back turned, drowning in qi that wasn't mine. The Hermit had flooded everything. His presence saturated the settlement, overwhelmed every sense, and buried the subtle signals under it. I couldn't feel Wei's core through that. I couldn't feel anything through that. But the bootprints told me where he'd stood. Shen told me how it happened. I knew Wei well enough to imagine his posture between the words of that account: stubborn, upright, and committed to staying where he was. A child who had decided to stand would hardly be persuaded by an argument he couldn't outtalk. The technique struck him above the collarbone. Shen saw the impact. He saw the force travel through Wei's body. He saw Wei fall.

The fall took time, but barely any. There was so little distance between a standing child and the ground. Wei was not tall.

He hit the ground.

I didn't hear the sound. I was on the west wall. The Hermit's qi was still everywhere, thick enough to make the air itself opaque to sensing.

But his core screamed.

The scream cut through the saturation, through the interference, and through the noise of a battle I'd already lost control of. I felt it through the Hermit's flood: something small and bright tearing itself apart at a distance I couldn't close in time.

I ran.

The saturation thinned. Sensing returned in fragments: the wall, the gate, and the field beyond. My feet found the steps, then the ground. I ran toward the place the scream had come from.

He was not breathing when I reached him.

His eyes were open. Those dark, sharp eyes had fixed on me from a forest path while he shouted for help. That voice had arrived in my life as a demand and never stopped demanding. He saw everything. He forgave little. Now his eyes didn't move when I came into view. Still. I looked into them as I had a thousand times, waiting for him to notice me.

"Wei."

His name in my mouth. The taste of it. Too late.

He smelled like blood. Like herbs, the sharp ones, too much of them, the ones he always used too much of. Like earth. The three smells combined into a scent that was only his. A child had lived briefly but loudly here, stubborn enough to make room for himself in a world that made little room for children.

His face was too young. That was the detail I couldn't move past while the fire warmed my own face. Too young. He hadn't finished becoming what he was going to become. The jaw was still forming. The cheekbones were still emerging. The unfinished face held something of the adult he might have been.

He was quiet.

That was wrong. Wei was never quiet. Wei was noise. Constant, irritating, relentless noise. He had opinions, objections, and questions. His commentary continued at a volume that matched his conviction that the world owed him an explanation for everything it did. Wei was the loudest thing in any room, any forest, and any conversation.

He was quiet now. He had been quiet before I arrived. The quiet had started without me.

I tried to heal him. Or was I trying to bring him back?

The belief. Whatever it was, it had moved stones, redirected water, and shifted thirty cultivators. It had warmed tea. By the fire I remembered how I had wanted him alive. Whole. I wanted him breathing, his body intact, and the damage repaired. I wanted him to stand. To walk. To be loud again.

I wanted it with everything I had.

It didn't work.

I hadn't known what lay under the wanting. I had simply wanted him back, with the full force of millennia spent getting what I wanted without knowing how.

Now, in this clearing, with the memory finally whole, I saw what had been underneath. I had carried a certainty deeper than the thoughts I could reach. Over millennia I had come to accept it so completely that I no longer noticed it when I tried to believe anything else.

Everything I love dies.

The belief had won.

I remembered kneeling at the gate with my hands on him. His body was still warm, still present, still occupying the space that a living body occupies. His shape lay under my palms. His chest didn't move. The warmth was already leaving him, cooling toward the earth.

I tried again. I wanted it again. I meant it again. I put everything I had into that wanting, every desperate thought, and every refusal to let this be the end.

Nothing.

I hadn't understood then. Now, with dew cold under my knees, I understood the difference. Wanting him alive had never reached the certainty below it. The deep belief held. It had always held. I couldn't remember a time before it, before the wall, before the walking through forests that outlived whole civilizations.

Wei was dead.

And maybe my belief had killed him.

I looked at the fire again. The clearing was dark beyond its light.

I was on my knees. The grass was wet with dew. My face was dry. No tears. Grief required understanding and I didn't understand.

You were beside me, your hand on my back. The weight of a human hand, the warmth of a mortal palm. I could stay with that.

Xiǎohú pressed against my knee, warm through the cloth, her head lowered. She leaned into me while I shifted my weight in the wet grass.

The metallic taste was gone. The teeth-vibration was silent. For the first time since it started, the hum had stopped. Whatever happened in me when the world changed had fallen quiet. I parted my teeth, listening for the vibration. Only the fire made a sound.

I rubbed my arms, passing my hands over the same stretches of cloth. I had run out of other things to do. A bitter taste had replaced the metal. Everything was bitter.

Your hand was still on my back.

You said nothing.
