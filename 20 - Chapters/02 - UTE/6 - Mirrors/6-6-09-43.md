Another morning a few days later, the kind that had existed before language, before someone named it dawn, before the word morning was invented to domesticate the raw fact of the world becoming visible again. The light was gray-gold. The mist lay low in the valley like something spilled. The air smelled like dew, pine, and the mineral coldness of ground that had been dark for four watches and was only now warming.

I had gotten up, left the camp, and walked to the edge of the clearing. My feet were bare, as always. The grass was wet, the dew between my toes cold, the sharpened, genuine cold that the breach had returned to me and that still surprised me, like a guest back after years who knows where the cups are.

I stood in the grass. The morning was happening, and I was in it.

Xiǎohú was awake at the treeline, facing the valley, still and alert. Her eyes caught the early light and held it. Her tail was wrapped tight around her paws.

You were still asleep, a shape in the camp behind me, indistinct in the mist, human-sized, breathing.

I pressed my feet into the grass.

I basked in the sensation that had once been overwhelming. Each blade a separate pressure, each drop of dew a distinct temperature, the soil beneath giving with my weight, the whole foot a sensor array. My feet in the grass. Wet. Cold. Present.

How do you let go of something you've held for millennia?

How do I stop holding onto immortality, rather than becoming mortal? How could I open myself, rather than gripping? I understood the mechanism. I understood the theory. I understood the shape of it, from a distance, as one understands a mountain.

But understanding the shape of a mountain is not the same as climbing it.

How do you open a fist that has been closed so long it has forgotten it's a fist? How do you release a grip that has become the structure of the hand? How do you let go of the thing that is doing the letting? The paradox wasn't philosophical. It was practical, my daily problem of standing in wet grass, knowing exactly what I needed to do and having no idea how.

I didn't know that yet. But I knew the question's shape and its weight. I knew it would not be answered by thinking, or by will, or by another dramatic confrontation. It would be answered, if it could be answered, by the same slow, physical process that had brought the insight in the first place. By hands in water. By feet in grass. By the daily, unremarkable practice of being a thing in the world without gripping the world into shape.

The mist was lifting. The gray-gold becoming gold. Xiǎohú turned her head.

"Breakfast," I said.

To no one. To the morning. To Xiǎohú, who didn't care. To myself, who was, for the first time in a very long time, one self, speaking with one voice, hearing clearly.

I turned and walked back to the camp. The dew on my feet picked up pine needles, a fragment of leaf, the small accumulations of walking barefoot on forest floor. The fire from last night was embers. I crouched, added kindling, and blew, not too hard, not too soft, the breath a fire required.

The fire caught.

You stirred and opened your eyes. You sat up. Looked at me. At the fire. The quick, comprehensive evaluation of status, environment, companion. Everything accounted for. Everything acceptable.

You put the pot on. Water from the skin. Rice from the bag. A handful, a splash, the ratios of someone who had cooked rice long enough not to measure. The water warmed. The rice sat in it, doing whatever rice does before cooking begins. Absorbing, softening.

The rice cooked slowly, inevitably, without requiring belief, will, or conviction. The heat did the work. The water did the work. You held a stick and stirred while I watched.

The rice was done. You served two bowls, set one near the fire for me, and took yours.

We ate.

The rice was adequate, the adequacy of rice cooked by a competent but uninspired, or sleepy, chef. Each bite was a bite. Each swallow was a swallow. I didn't need to eat. A body that ran on conviction rather than biology required no fuel. But it felt good.

I ate because I wanted to.

Not needed. Wanted. The distinction was clear and whole. I wanted the rice. I wanted the warmth. I wanted the morning, the fire, the taste, and the act of eating with another person. Xiǎohú watched. The mist lifted. The world continued its patient, unrequested operation.

Wanting was a beginning.

The question was still there, the how of letting go, the shape of the mountain from the base. You could want rice, eat it, finish it, set the bowl down. The wanting was complete. The letting go was natural.

I set the bowl down.

The mountain was ahead.

Xiǎohú watched us both, her amber eyes holding the early light.
