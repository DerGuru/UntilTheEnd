Smoke drifted through the places where thirty cultivators had stood. Through it, the far side of the square showed plain. None of them had taken a step before vanishing.

The child still cried against its mother's chest. Behind her, flames ran along the thatch. I looked from the well to the road, searching for even one of the missing figures.

You stood at my side. The villagers waited in their doorways or behind their walls, staring at the open ground. Nobody tried to cross it. The mother's grip tightened around the crying child. The fire crackled through everyone else's silence.

The fires still burned.

The nearest fire climbed a house wall. Through the broken doorway I saw part of a kitchen. Someone had stood there that morning, making a meal.

The fire is out.

I held that certainty as I had held the empty square. I made no gesture toward the flames. The statement was as plain as the ground under my feet.

The fire went out. All of them. Every flame in the square, simultaneously, as though they had never been lit. The smoke stopped. The heat withdrew. The coals went dark.

Blackened beams hung above the collapsed wall. Scorch marks covered the stone around the doorway.

The wall is standing. The house is whole.

The wall stood, and with it came the certainty that it had always stood. Scorch marks faded from the stone. The beams straightened; the thatch settled back across them. The house had its weathering back, the slight irregularities of an old roof. It was whole.

I did this for each house in turn. I held its wholeness in mind until I believed it. With each correction, something thin drew around me. The cost registered without a wound or any strain in my hands.

Somewhere between the second house and the third, a thought arrived. Every revision fed the sect's instruments.

So be it.

A woman in a flour-stained apron came out of a doorway. She touched her wall. Her fingers traced the place where the scorch mark had been, then rested flat against the unburned surface.

She turned to me, her hand still on the wall. Her mouth trembled before she pressed it shut. She didn't leave the doorway.

She bowed low. I didn't know how to receive thanks from someone still afraid to approach me. I nodded, turned away, and left the village.

I found the cultivators two hundred paces away in the forest. They were scattered among the trees: some standing, some kneeling, and some flat on the ground. One was vomiting. Another touched his own face slowly, running his fingers along his jaw before looking at them.

I checked them for injuries. They were whole. I had believed them elsewhere; here they were, outside the village, with nothing between those two places that I could remember doing.

The vomiting cultivator was braced on all fours. His confusion was as distinct as the heat of his body, and I didn't share it. He looked up at the trees, then down at the earth under his hands. He had been in a village square a moment before. Now he had to find his bearings in a forest he hadn't walked into.

I looked at my hands.

They were steady. I flexed my fingers, searching for the aftermath of exertion. I had used no force. In the square I had been utterly certain that those people were gone.

All my life, I had pushed against resistance. I had pressed against the wall, tried to hold thoughts as they were erased, and forced the world to yield where I could. My hands knew that work. They had done none of it here.

They are not here.

I turned those words over, looking at the cultivator on the ground. For that moment, their absence had been as unquestionable to me as sunrise. I had believed it before it was true. Then the square had been empty.

I was the thing that had made thirty people disappear.

I was the thing that could make anyone disappear.

I was the thing that reality rearranged itself to accommodate.

Xiǎohú moved first. I followed her; you came behind me. We left the cultivators among the trees, walking quickly. I kept my eyes on the fox-spirit ahead. I had no wish to look back at what I had done.

The taste of metal lingered in my mouth as we left. For seven days afterward, the village's well water would taste bitter. They would find no contamination they could remove. I had made their houses whole, but the cost had settled into the water they needed every day.

Bitter water, drawn from the well where the mother had held her child.

I'd saved them. And they would never forgive me for it.
