I opened my eyes with cold water moving against my hips. The memories had settled enough for the river's southward current to register again. Wet cotton clung to my skin and carried the river's cold into every fold.

It was all as sharp as everything had been since the breach. Cold pressed through the cloth. Water weighed against my waist. River stones pressed their separate shapes into my knees, then air moved over my wet arms. I had no distance from those small sensations.

Sunlight fell on the river, the bank, the trees, and you. It was the clear light of a day in a mountain valley. The bark of the nearest pine stood out in it, and the cloth at your knees. The light showed things as they were.

As they are.

The phrase was different now. Before I found my true self, it had meant a world I received as given. Now it meant a world made through belief, sustained by a conviction I had held without knowing I held it. I could change things while continuing to believe the rest would remain. I wondered what happened if that conviction ceased. If I stopped believing in the river, the trees, or you, would they disappear? Would the world continue without me? It would, if only because, deeper than I could reach, I believed it would.

The same pine stood on the bank, its roots in the same earth. My belief was in its standing. I had accepted its rootedness as something outside myself while sustaining that acceptance through every passing moment. Its reality was bound to mine. Was this godhood?

The same cloud-streaked sky stretched above us. It was the ordinary blue of an ordinary afternoon. I had believed it would be blue. I had believed the clouds would move. Now I could no more separate sky and clouds from my certainty than the river's cold from my skin.

I expected to be terrified. I had thought the world existed independently of me, a place on which I could stand while asking who I was. Now my own belief showed wherever I looked. What was left outside it?

I was too tired to chase that question far.

My muscles ached. My head hurt from trying to hold too much at once. My eyes were dry. I blinked, still kneeling in the cold, and the enormity of what I had understood settled into those small discomforts.

You sat cross-legged on the dry bank. Your notebook was closed now, the pen in your breast pocket. I had spent months learning to read that attentive expression. Even now I couldn't fully translate the concern in it, or the something else behind the concern.

"Yun," you said. Quietly.

I rose with effort. My knees ached from the stones, and the cold had stiffened my muscles. I waded toward the bank, sending small ripples outward with each step. They looked as ordinary as any ripples I had made while crossing water.

Or were they? Was any ripple I produced ordinary anymore? Did I produce ripples because my body displaced water, or because I believed in displacement? Was there a difference? Had there ever been?

At the bank, you held out a hand. I took its mortal warmth. Your palm was callused from practical work. You pulled, and I stepped onto dry ground.

My clothes were wet. The fabric heavy, clinging, cold against my skin.

I looked down at it. At the dark cotton pressed against my thighs. At the water dripping from the hem.

I knew how this worked now.

My clothes were dry.

The fabric was suddenly light against my skin. I touched the hem that had been dripping a moment earlier. Every fold was dry. Warm cloth lay against my legs as though I had stayed on the bank, the river only something I had considered entering.

Your eyes went from my clothes to the river behind me. Your hand moved toward the pen in your breast pocket and stopped short.

We stood there quietly. I knew what I had done, and that was enough.

The trees, river, stones, and sky looked as they had half a watch earlier. Every shape was the same as before. Yet I understood differently why they existed. I had changed while the world stood unchanged before me.

"Shall I make rice?" you said.

The question was perfect: its timing, its tone, its absolute refusal to treat this moment as anything other than what it also was. A day on a mountain, two people who needed food.

I nodded.

You stood, went to your pack, and took out the pot, the rice, and the water skin. You set the pot over the fire, measured the water, and poured in the rice.

Xiǎohú lay curled near the firestones, nose tucked under her tail. She had lain there the whole time I was in the river. She lifted her head when I stepped into the clearing and laid it down again. After days of her cautious inspections, that quiet glance was enough.

I sat beside you at the fire, closer than usual. My shoulder nearly touched yours. Through the dry cotton of your shirt, the warmth of your living body reached mine.

And I waited for rice.

Like every other day. Like every other meal. Like the thousand meals before this one and the however-many meals that would come after.

I could know the truth and still wait for rice. It was with me while you tended the pot. We would speak differently now, but we could still sit together like this.

I am it. I was always it.

The recognition hummed in my chest, where the crack had been. The wall was transparent now, and on the other side sat the same woman by the fire.

That recognition sat in me while the rice cooked. My belief carried more weight than I had ever dared to consider. I could know that, then look back at an ordinary pot of food.

The rice cooked.

Xiǎohú trotted over, sat by my knee, and fixed her eyes on the pot. Whatever I had learned in the river, she would still need her share of dinner.

We ate.

Then I told you enough to explain what had happened in the river. I described the belief, its effects, and what I now understood. The millennia could wait. So could the strange shape of the place before places. For now I told you what I could hold.

You listened. You didn't write.
