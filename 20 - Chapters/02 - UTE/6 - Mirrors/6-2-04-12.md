I worked on your meridians while the water heated. The blockages had formed again in the same damaged places. I cleared what I could, checking the secondary channels as I went. They were cleaner now. Progress came slowly enough that I had to compare each session carefully with the last.

You tended the pot as I worked. First you had set it over the fire, then filled it with water. The rice went in next. The herbs waited at your knee until the end, when they would give the meal the most flavor. Even on an evening like this, you observed that order.

The herbs smelled wrong.

You had brought wild rosemary, a bitter root, and something sharp-smelling that grew high in the mountains. You mixed them without measuring. Necessity had taught you to cook; you had become good enough to trust your hands.

But the proportions were wrong.

There was too much of the sharp herb, too little of the bitter root. I knew that mixture. Its smell carried another campfire with it, one tended by a boy who insisted on cooking despite repeated evidence that he shouldn't.

Wei.

The smell brought him back as clearly as the images of the creating hands. The wall had been quiet since the village. I had expected it to intervene when I deliberately changed the square. Now it let me remember him too.

The memory ran from one moment to the next with nothing missing between them.

I stopped eating.

The herbs steamed in my bowl. I held it close, breathing in the sharp smell. I remembered that scent on his hands before I remembered it in his cooking.

That forest had been farther south. Its canopy closed overhead, letting damp green light reach the trail. I had been walking without a destination, as I had walked through thousands of forests before it. Wet leaves brushed my sleeves. Another path, another stretch of ground I would leave behind.

Wei had been sitting on a rock.

He was twelve: big enough to go out alone, small enough that I kept noticing it. His clothes were torn. His hair was a catastrophe. He had his hands in the basket, sorting what he had gathered, as he had done for the past few days.

I almost walked past him.

My fingers tightened around the warm bowl. I had nearly continued along the trail. Walking past had become easy after centuries of practice. The wall took away things that might have held my attention; I supplied the habit of moving on.

That morning, I had stopped at his rock. Such a small decision. The arrested step was still with me, the weight on my back foot while I looked down at him.

He had looked up.

His jaw was set with the defiance of a child accustomed to being told no. His dark eyes missed very little. Dirt smeared his left cheek. A half-healed scratch crossed his right hand, probably from a fight with something inanimate. I could have pointed to every mark.

He had smiled at me. "You're standing in my light."

As if I were the intrusion. As if the forest belonged to him and the relevant issue was the shadow I was casting on his basket.

I lowered the bowl. The wall had let me keep that moment. It could have taken it, left me walking with another gap I wouldn't think to examine. Instead, I remembered choosing to stay.

I had wanted to see what he was sorting. For the first time in millennia, a dirty child on a rock interested me enough to interrupt my journey. I hadn't needed an explanation then.

Steam dampened my face above the bowl. I had spent centuries passing people whose work mattered fiercely to them. Usually I left before learning what it was. With him, I had looked into the basket and noticed his care with things he could barely identify. I had time to help. I had always had time.

I had stepped out of his light.

He had gone straight back to his basket without thanking me. Once my shadow cleared the mushrooms, he had everything he needed. His fingers dug among the herbs.

I had sat on a rock of my own, ten paces away. I could have kept walking. There was as little reason to stay as there was to leave; I had all the time I would ever need. Yet I had settled there where I could see his basket.

"You're still here," he said, without looking up.

"Yes."

"Why?"

I didn't answer. I didn't have an answer.

He had shrugged and left the mystery alone. His small hands returned to their sorting. Each mushroom went into its place with care.

His hands smelled like herbs. The wrong ones. Too much of the sharp. Too little of the bitter.

"You stopped eating," you said.

I looked up at you across the fire, then down at my untouched bowl. Steam still rose from the herbs. Your mixture was too sharp. I wanted another mouthful.

"The herbs," I said.

"What about them?"

"He mixed them the same way."

You didn't ask who. You knew.

You spooned more rice into my bowl, covering some of the herbs. The bowl grew heavier in my hands.

"Eat," you said.

I ate. Under the plain rice, the sharp herbs still tasted of his cooking.

You added another spoonful.
