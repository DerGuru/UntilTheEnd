I woke with the taste of clean, cold metal on my tongue. I ran it against my teeth. The metal was as distinct as if I had been holding a coin in my mouth.

The fire was gray ash, cold for more than a watch. You were still asleep on the other side of the pit, your head on your notebook. Its waterproof case made an uncomfortable pillow, but you'd run out of clothes to fold under your skull.

A flat overcast covered the morning sky. I looked for the edges of individual clouds, but the dimness was uniform from one ridge to the other.

I sat up. The cold was there immediately, a parting gift from the crack you had caused with a question I did not remember. Once, I could have told you precisely how cold the morning was without caring. Now the air stung my face. The ground chilled my hands. The mountain wind pressed my clothes against my skin.

And then there was the humming.

It was in my teeth: a low, steady vibration under the sounds of the camp. It reached my molars, the bones of my jaw, and the base of my skull. I parted my teeth. The humming continued through the bone, too deep to hear.

This was new. The nameless feeling behind my sternum was not new; I'd carried it since the moment I couldn't remember. The vibration in my teeth was higher, more insistent. Whatever I had felt deep in my chest now reached into my jaw. I rubbed it with the heel of my hand.

The metallic taste thickened as the vibration grew. I swallowed, but that only spread it to the back of my tongue.

I stood and stretched. Sleeping on the ground had stiffened my shoulders. My lower back ached. The headache pulsed behind my eyes in time with my heartbeat, as persistent as whatever I carried inside me.

Xiǎohú was awake at my bedroll, where she had lain every morning for the past seven days. Her amber eyes followed me as I straightened. A fox might have been waiting for breakfast. With a fox-spirit, I was less certain.

I walked to the river.

The water was cold and clear. Its constant murmur over the stones filled the quiet bank. I knelt, cupped some water, and brought it to my face.

The cold hit on contact, sharp enough to count each droplet. Every rivulet that ran down my cheeks and dripped from my jaw carried its own exact coldness.

I opened my eyes.

My reflection lay in a still pool at the river's edge. The sheltered pocket resembled the one that had shown me the smile. The calm water held my whole face.

I turned away. Fast.

I had seen only my own tired, thin face. But water still dripped from my chin, recalling the smile that had preceded mine last time, the warmth that had come with it. I fixed my eyes on the bank. I might feel something if it happened again.

I finished washing my face without looking down.

You were awake when I returned. You added kindling to the ashes and breathed on them with the stubborn determination that had gotten fires lit a thousand times before. I stood back to watch this particular test of your optimism.

"Good morning," you said.

"Good morning." I looked at the fox-spirit. "And good morning to you too, Xiǎohú," I added.

Against my prediction, the fire caught. A small orange flame licked along the dry bark. You fed it pine needles, then twigs, and finally the larger pieces you arranged carefully above the flame.

You gave me the quick inspection you'd performed every morning since the clearing. Your eyes moved from my posture to my face, then to my hands.

"Something new?" you asked.

"I taste metal."

You nodded, reached for your notebook, and stopped. The notebook was new, still unused. You had left it closed since buying it, after the day you'd decided that taking notes had reached its limits. Whatever came next was mine.

You opened it now. Something in what I'd said must have overridden the decision. You wrote one line and closed it again.

We ate cold rice from last night, because the fire was still too small for boiling water and I was too hungry for patience. The rice tasted of nothing. I appreciated being able to rely on that.

The humming pulsed in my teeth, already stronger than when I'd woken. Metal lay under each mouthful. It coated my tongue so thoroughly that I kept swallowing after the rice was gone.

Something had changed. The crack in the wall was still there when I pressed inward. It gave, as it had before. Under my sternum the nameless feeling persisted, past whatever took my thoughts away.

But overnight it had spread through more of my chest and grown stronger where it had already been. Sitting still made the change impossible to ignore.

I didn't know what it meant. The wall was quiet. Whatever was happening had drawn no response from it.

Xiǎohú stretched, padded to my side, and sat close enough that her fur brushed my leg. I started petting her. She leaned into the touch, as she had on the first day, when she'd curled against the one person who could feel her. Her body warmed my leg through the rough fabric of my pants. I rested my hand in her soft fur. I had come to rely on that small weight. With you I usually had to answer questions.

The overcast thinned gradually. Light spread across our bedrolls, the ash pit, and your closed notebook. The brighter it grew, the less excuse we had to go on sitting there.

I packed. You packed and killed the fire. We left the camp, taking the trail away from the river where I had seen my face.

My teeth hummed. My mouth tasted like metal. The world was too sharp, too loud, and too cold. Deep inside, below the wall's crack, the feeling pulsed harder with every step.

Something was coming.

Was I ready for it?

Almost.
