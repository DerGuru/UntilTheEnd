Blood tastes like copper, memories like distance.

The metallic flavor was in my mouth because the sleeve-wound had sent a fine spray that reached my lips. Blood. Mine. The taste connected something older than thinking.

Two thousand years ago, or close to it, with the imprecision centuries impose on the timestamps of experience. Two thousand years, give or take the lifespan of a civilization.

She was a warrior. In Zhèn, or was it Zhēn? The tones decayed first when a name died. A province that no longer existed under any name, in a conflict that no longer existed in any record. The cause I no longer remember. Something about borders, or grain, or an insult that required a generation of corpses to settle. The war consumed a region for a generation and was forgotten within three, because the next war was already happening.

She was blind.

This was the detail that survived when most details hadn't. Blind: eyes that did not receive light, ruined from birth or by damage. She fought without seeing, with the compensatory awareness blindness produces: hearing, spatial memory, and sensitivity to air movement that told her where bodies were.

She hit me.

The only swordstroke in two thousand years that I hadn't seen coming. She was different. Her attack came from a vector that vision-dependent organisms do not anticipate, an angle that existed only in the perceptual framework of someone navigating the world without the primary sense everyone else relied on. She cut where sighted fighters didn't cut. She moved where sighted fighters didn't move. And she hit me.

My left side. Below the ribs. A sighted fighter would have avoided that location, too difficult to reach from standard combat positions. For her the approach was natural: it was where her perception put the opening.

Blood flowed. Mine. The same copper taste that was in my mouth now, millennia later, in a clearing with two unconscious spirit-beasts, two disabled bandits, and a researcher with a notebook.

The wound healed. Then too, the same involuntary closure. But I remembered the moment before it closed, the instant when the pain felt like a connection, a bridge between my body and the fact that it existed in a world that could touch it.

The blind warrior died three days later, in the same conflict, killed by someone less interesting. The mundane death warriors meet when the war is large enough and probability catches up. In a conflict of this scale, everyone dies except me.

I had watched her die from a distance, close enough to see, far enough not to intervene. The gap between observing and intervening was the space I inhabited. It was wide.

The memory closed. The copper faded. The blood on my arm dried.

You watched me from ten paces. You had been watching throughout, the signs plain to read: unfocused attention, a body here and a consciousness elsewhere. You recognized memory-states. You had learned to.

You wrote. Then you spoke.

"How often," you said, "have you done that?"

"Done what."

"Let something hit you."

The question was so like you: the operational version, the one that treated the behavior as data rather than trauma.

"Enough," I said.

"But you could have avoided it?"

"Yes."

You wrote the answer. Then you put the notebook down. Put it down. The gesture marked a shift from documenter to person, a rare one, which came when the data exceeded the emotional capacity of the collector.

"You're spiraling," you said. "You know you're spiraling."

I did not answer. The spiraling, if that was the word, was the escalation my recklessness followed: from letting a wolf-beast approach, to moving toward cultivators, to allowing a blade past the guard. The trajectory pointed toward more. More contact. More pain. More of the only thing that felt real.

"I'm not going to tell you to stop," you said. "But I'm going to write what I see."

"You always write what you see."

"Yes. And what I see is someone who is testing how much damage she can feel before the feeling stops."

The accuracy was uncomfortable. You were right, and being right required understanding me at a level I preferred not to be understood at.

That evening, I cooked.

Poorly, without any of the competence you brought to the task or the knowledge millennia should have provided. I did not know why I cooked. The impulse arrived and I followed it, as I followed the direction you set. Without resistance, yet without being in it. Cooking required attention. Choices. A directed engagement the disconnection still impaired.

The rice burned. The vegetables disintegrated. The ginger, your ginger, borrowed without asking, was cut too thin and dissolved in water that was too hot. The heat destroyed the volatile compounds that made ginger ginger.

The smell of burned rice hung over the camp like an indictment. Acrid, persistent, the olfactory equivalent of a failed argument.

You said nothing. Your face said everything. The compression-face, somewhere between clinical observation and personal offense, aimed at the pot with the intensity of a witness to a crime in progress.

I said nothing.

We both knew.

The meal was inedible. I did not eat it, because the product was not food, appetite suppression aside. You did not eat it either, for the same reason, expressed through diplomatic silence.

You cooked a second meal, quietly. You cut the ginger at a proper thickness and distributed the heat properly. The result was edible.

I sat six paces away and watched you cook the replacement for my failure. The food was irrelevant. The attempt had been made, the first voluntary domestic action since the forest. Another data point: I tried.

You wrote it down. Of course you did.

That night something stirred me up.

I did not sleep. I was in the interval between wakefulness and something else, neither sleep nor full consciousness: body still, eyes open, sight unfocused. The closest approximation of rest available.

Something had changed.

Ten paces away, you slept, your breathing even, deep, efficient. The fire had burned down to embers. The clearing was dark. The stars stood in their late-watch positions, the temperature dropping, the moisture thickening, the darkness of a watch and a half before dawn.

Everything external was unchanged.

But internally, something was different.

There was an impulse, the same pre-verbal, pre-thought event as at the bramble wall, the one that existed before language, before decision. The one that *should*.

I had been listening to the river. It lay beyond our clearing, forty paces east, water over stone, the sound a shallow bed makes under a persistent current. It smelled of mud, mineral, and the cold that moving water releases at night.

The river was in the wrong place.

Wrong differently than the branches or shadows had been wrong, wrong in a way only millennia of traversing geography could detect. Rivers in this terrain followed north-south valleys. This one flowed east-west.

The impulse demanded to move it.

A tingling pressure arrived in my fingertips. Warmth under the skin. The quiet gathering of qi that meant power was awake.

But the direction it wanted was shift.

I caught myself.

Like waking inside a dream. Like the jolt that interrupts falling. I caught the impulse mid-formation, in the space between wanting and action, the only space where catching was still possible.

What was the action? If I had not caught the impulse, if the qi had moved, if the direction had been followed: would the river have moved? Would I see tomorrow a river that had always flowed north-south, its banks eroded by decades in the new direction, its stones polished by centuries of a current that had existed for one night?

The question was not theoretical. The paths had happened. The cairn had happened. Reality had been edited, and the edits were tied to my frustration, a close neighbor of the impulse. And the impulse had felt right.

The worst part was simpler. The impulse to reshape reality had felt correct. As ordinary as breathing. As if the world had always been something my hands could adjust.

It felt familiar.

I had enough of those already. The last thing the world needed was more of what I could do.

I clenched my fists. The qi receded. The tingling stopped. The impulse dissolved like a sound fading.

The river continued flowing east-west. Wrong and real.

The not-sleeping for the rest of the night was different: vigilant. I monitored the internal terrain for the return of the impulse that had felt so right. Its rightness was the most frightening thing I had encountered in centuries.

The river murmured its wrong direction. The frogs upstream had stopped when I noticed the wrongness. They hadn't started again.

Morning came. You woke. You opened the notebook.

You wrote the date, the weather, the first observations.

"Something happened," you said, without looking up. You had learned to read shifts in the subject's baseline without requiring the subject's cooperation. "You're different this morning."

"Nothing happened."

"Mm." You wrote that down.

"Your handwriting is getting smaller," I said.

You looked up. The compression-face, surprised, recalibrating. "That's... observational."

"I've been staring at your notebook for weeks. It was inevitable."

We moved. I moved ahead. You followed. The ten paces. The arrangement.

The space where the impulse had been was empty for now. I waited for the next time my fingertips would tingle and the *should-be* would arrive feeling like remembering.

The river remained wrong behind us. The river remained real.

So did I.
