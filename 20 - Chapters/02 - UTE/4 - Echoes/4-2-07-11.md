You packed the ginger next to your notebook for another day's walk. Twenty-three days of small disturbances had accumulated, silently and measurably, until little room remained for one more.

First there was you.

You were still there. Always. For twenty-three days now, according to your precise count. This was your contribution to the shared reality. Twenty-three days of the ten-pace distance, the notebook, cooking, your loud meditation, and the questions that I sometimes answered but mostly didn't. Your persistence occupied every waking moment. You worked. Everything was data. Every silence was data. Every word I produced was data. Their count had risen from zero to single digits per day. You tracked the increase.

You were there, and every day of your presence kept my isolation from being total. The crack widened.

Then there was the wrongness.

Other trees in different locations had the same anomaly. Their branches were oriented at odds with the growth around them. It registered as unease rather than alarm. Other rocks cast shadows at the same wrong angle. Once there was a sound. A bird call echoed when there was no surface for it to echo from.

The echo repeated twice before it stopped. You even stopped writing and looked up at the space where the echo should not have been. The skin between my shoulders tightened as if someone were watching from that empty air. Then you resumed your writing. Your pen moved faster.

Echoes.

You documented everything. Three pages of anomalies. The documentation was your fortress. If it was written, it was real. If it was real, it was not imagined. The world was doing something you might not understand yet, but that made it worth understanding.

"The pattern is spatial," you said, reviewing your notes. "The anomalies cluster near you. Within fifty paces. Outside of that, nothing."

So the wrongness was also my doing? The same as with the death zone.

There was silence.

You slept early, exhausted from walking, from the terrain's increasing difficulty, and from the physical cost of following an immortal at a ten-pace distance for twenty-three days with a broken core. You slept deeply, and your sleep was silent.

I was alone.

This aloneness included the awareness of someone else's presence, temporarily withdrawn. I knew you would wake again, and I waited for it. The difference made the night worse.

Your temporary absence was interference. I had adjusted to the light of a candle in a dark room. For the night, the candle was out. The dark was deeper than before. Twenty-three days of cooking, meditation, and writing had shifted the baseline. Returning to the old silence was now noticeable.

The awareness that sound should be here and wasn't made me realize that expectation had returned. Wanting was closer than it had been before. Closer than it should have been.

I sat by the dead fire. Brown grass. Stars. The humming in my chest.

"Wei would have hated this."

One sentence. Quiet. Into the clearing, under the stars, in the space where your noise usually existed. You stirred. Your breathing changed. You looked toward me and closed your eyes again. Ten paces away, breathing evenly once more. You seemed to be asleep again, but I thought you had heard it.

The sound of my own voice in the silence was the most alarming thing that had happened since the hut. The words themselves were true enough. Wei would have liked the forest; he would have hated sitting through this passive deterioration. But I had spoken to the absence. I had addressed some nothing as if it were available for conversation.

Preference was leaking through the crack that your words had made and the question had widened. The irritation had confirmed it. The almost-smile had implied it.

The alarm was quiet: the internal recognition that a state had irreversibly changed. The glass could not un-crack.

I was alone and the aloneness was unbearable. And I had enough of it.

The fire was ash. The stars moved. Your breathing was the only sound. The even, unconscious respiration of a body maintaining itself while the mind was elsewhere.

I sat. In the unbearable. In the enough.

The three triggers were simultaneous. Together they multiplied three insufficient forces into one sufficient force.

But sufficient force for what? I did not yet know. That would come in the morning. Morning would come whether I participated or not. My participation was what was changing.

I sat until dawn. When you woke, I was sitting where I had been, but something was quietly altered, in a register only careful observation could detect.

You detected it.

You opened the notebook. You studied me. You wrote one word. Then you closed it.

The sun cleared the tree line and the mist burned off.

I stood.

Standing was the default transition between sitting and moving. But this standing contained a direction, categorical rather than geographic. I could name no compass bearing, destination, or location the body faced. Just not here.

The disconnection held: the gap, the absence of will that had characterized the days since the forest. I felt neither hunger nor the need for sleep. Particular outcomes held little interest. But below the severed line, in the layer where need operated, the body breathed and the heart beat. Somewhere under that involuntary maintenance, something had shifted.

The closest word I had for it was a direction. My current position had become impossible to sustain. Any other position was preferable. The necessity reached me even through the disconnection.

I had no possessions to pack. Oddly, that came easily. I had often left with nothing. When I met Wei, I had nothing. Leaving with nothing was the oldest form of departure, the form that predated luggage.

I moved. For days I had walked whenever standing was exhausted. Now the movement had a direction, a departure from something. The body followed it. Away from here.

You sat at the fire, watching through the steam of the rice, notebook open, pen hovering over the page. You had been writing but stopped when I moved.

"You're packing," you said.

"I have nothing to pack."

"You're making the motions."

You were right. Countless departures had worn these motions into the bones. I adjusted my shoulders, shifted my weight to the balls of my feet, and raised my gaze to the path ahead. The motions of packing without the possessions to pack. A ghost of departure.

You closed the notebook, deliberately, as if the phase had changed. You did not put it away. The notebook was never away, never inaccessible, because inaccessible produced forgetting, and forgetting was the thing you fought. You held it closed against your chest.

"Where?" you asked.

The question was valid. I only knew that I needed to move away. Toward what, I couldn't say. Away was the beginning, not the destination.

I did not answer. I genuinely didn't know. The question of where would fill itself as the movement continued, as terrain determined a river's course. I would go. I would encounter geography. The geography would lead me somewhere.

You stood and stepped away from the fire with your rice untouched. You looked at the rice. The rice steamed. Then you looked up. You left the rice.

You did not abandon rice lightly. You did not abandon routine lightly. Routine was your defense, and you were setting it aside.

"Forward then," you said.

Two words. A direction stated as simply as an entry in your notebook. They contained everything about you. They opposed stopping. Whatever happened now, it would happen further along, in the only direction available.

Forward.

You picked up your worn pack with the patched seams, the ginger pouch, and the notebooks. Plural, I now realized. You gathered the scratched pot and the provisions that sustained a body that needed sustaining. You put out the fire. You did it all with the practiced economy of long departures, as automatic as my standing.

The morning air was cold. Your body creaked when you straightened with the pack. Your left side paid the compensation, the cost of carrying a broken core through mountain terrain for so many days. You did not complain. You adjusted the pack on your shoulders, favoring your right side. The injury talking.

I faced east, toward the break in the tree line. It was the path of least resistance, the route I could choose without yet having a destination.

I moved. The first step matched every step since the hut. The second differed, because I chose a pace you could keep. Even. Sustainable. A line that could be followed. Ten paces behind me, you walked too, holding the distance with the precision of every day of your presence. Ten paces. The gradient. Brown to yellow to green, the ecological distance between what I was and what lived.

You followed. Following was your verb, your choice. You had accepted it the moment you picked up your pack. I told myself that left me free of the responsibility of leading.

Two figures on a path. One ahead. One behind. The morning light between them, casting shadows that fell in the correct direction.

For now.
