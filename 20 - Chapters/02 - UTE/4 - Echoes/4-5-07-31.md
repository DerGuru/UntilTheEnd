The forest changed as we descended.

The volcanic soil from the crater region gave way to older, darker loam, the ground of a woodland that had grown undisturbed for centuries. The trees were taller here, the canopy dense enough to turn afternoon into evening. Light came through in columns where the cover broke, dust turning in the beams, the air smelling of damp bark and the faint sweetness of decay that healthy forests produced.

You were walking better today. The slow descent helped, easier on the legs and the breathing, the gentle gradient doing what your broken core couldn't. Your pen scratched steadily as you walked, locomotion and documentation in one operation you had perfected over weeks of shared travel.

"The mushrooms are different here," you said.

I looked. You were right. The fungi on the trunks had changed species: latticed white caps instead of the flat brown shelves we'd passed for days. Different altitude, different moisture, different substrate under the bark. The trunks marked how far we'd come, in the only language forests had.

"You should eat something," you said.

"No."

"You haven't eaten since—"

"No."

You wrote it down: the refusal, the date, the time, another entry in the catalog of things I did not do that a body should. The list was getting longer. You did not comment on its trajectory.

The trail widened where a stream crossed it, a shallow ford with water clear enough to show the stones at the bottom, rounded by the friction of current against mineral. I stopped at the bank. The water was cold, it was moving, and looking at it required nothing from the parts of me that were hurting.

You sat on a rock, opened the provisions pack, and ate some dried meat, then a piece of old bread, with methodical efficiency, treating meals as maintenance rather than pleasure. You chewed while you wrote. You had too much to document and too little time between events.

Birds filled the canopy, their territorial calls from species I could identify but didn't bother to. Normal birds, the right species for the altitude and the season. It was a small relief, fauna behaving as fauna should after weeks of wrongs: wrong stars, wrong winds, wrong everything.

The stream ran on while you ate. The forest went on around us, an ecosystem indifferent to substrates, memory erasure, and the headaches of an immortal woman who couldn't stop investigating herself.

Everything was good, until it wasn't.

Southeast and northwest. Pincer formation. Professional.

I turned toward them.

I wanted this.

"Eight," I said. "Formation."

Your pen stopped. You looked where I was looking and saw nothing; your broken core couldn't read qi signatures at this range. But you had learned to trust the count.

The four from the southeast emerged from the tree line in coordinated formation, equipped with spiritual weapons and qi-armor. They were practitioners who had been assigned a target and told the target was dangerous.

I moved.

The first cultivator's halberd came at me from the right. I went under it, just enough to avoid it, and it passed above me. My hand passed through his guard. The meridian junction was there. I touched it. His qi failed. He fell.

The second attacked in the same instant. Her sword was faster than the halberd. They had timed the attack well. It came close; I guided it aside rather than blocking it. The blade's momentum carried her forward, past me, off-balance. A finger against the qi point at her shoulder finished what the redirection had started. She went down.

A smile appeared.

I smiled. The real one, the one that used everything.

The third and fourth had the same fortune. I turned and pushed my fingers into their meridians. The southeast group: disabled in what you would later describe as "breathing twice."

The northwest group arrived as the southeast group went down. Another four. They had watched the first four fall and adjusted: wider formation, greater distance.

The smile widened.

The warmth came back. The old warmth, the one I remembered from battlefields.

I moved through them faster than even their cultivated eyes could follow. The first of the northwest group came at me with a spear. I caught it with one hand, put the other on his chest, and pushed. The spear was not a problem. The cultivator was. He went down. I used the spear's dull end to strike the second's chest. When I hit the third's knee, it snapped. Splinters flew around us. The fourth took the short stick that was all that remained of the spear across the neck. The northwest group: disabled even faster.

My hand went to my mouth. Wiping.

The disgust that followed was aimed at the warmth, at the pleasure, at the fact that the pleasure was genuine.

I picked something up: a pendant that had fallen from the leader of the northwest group, who lay on the ground, conscious. It was small, metal, and warmer than it should have been, as if it held its own heat.

It bore marks like insignia, the same ones I had seen on the first group's equipment. The same organization.

And a scratch. Added recently. After the original inscription.

*The new one.*

I put the talisman in my robe.

You stood at the clearing's edge. You had seen the smile.

"You enjoyed that," you said.

I didn't answer. There was nothing to say that you hadn't already written in your head.

You did not write. You stared at me, your face empty of judgment, surprise, even curiosity.

It was the loudest thing you had ever done.
