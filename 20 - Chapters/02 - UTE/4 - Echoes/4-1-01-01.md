I sat on the exposed root of an oak, listening to water somewhere west of me. Wind moved through the canopy. Farther off, insects rasped in the undergrowth. Each sound came from outside the patch of bare silence around the tree, the silence of exclusion.

The oak was centuries old. I had known younger forests than this one, though I couldn't have said which of them still stood. Thick ridges of bark pressed against my back. My hands rested on my knees. I kept my eyes open.

Dead grass formed a circle around me, three paces from where I sat to its edge. Yesterday it had been green. Now the blades lay brown against the roots, brittle enough to crumble. Outside it the ground was damp. Whatever had dried this small patch had come from me.

An ant trail reached the edge of the circle. The insects approached in a straight line, one after another, until the first turned sharply aside.

Each ant behind it turned at the same place. They continued along the brown edge, around the obstruction. From my root the detour was visible almost all the way back to the undergrowth. They had found a route that excluded me. Sensible of them.

I couldn't have said how long I had been sitting there. Sunrise, meals, sleep: ordinarily something marked one stretch of a day from another. Now light moved across the root under my knees, and I watched it without adding up what it meant.

A ripe red berry hung within arm's reach, its small weight bending the stem. I would hardly have needed to lean. My hand stayed on my knee. It was some time before it occurred to me that I could eat it.

Even then I left it hanging.

I usually went without sleep. Years had passed that way, with other things to occupy the darkness. Here I kept my eyes open because I could find no reason to close them. Night reached the oak. Eventually light returned. The wish to rest was somewhere out of reach.

Overhead, a bird crossed the canopy from east to west. It banked before it reached the branches above me. Its wings flickered through the leaves farther off, and then it was gone without landing.

Sunlight slanted lower through the canopy. Late afternoon, or early evening. Several watches had passed, though I had kept no count. The root was still hard.

A branch cracked in the distance. Something large enough to break wood was moving through the forest. An animal, probably. I listened until the sound faded. It never came near the circle.

Somewhere close, another branch cracked. Then a nearer one. The steps came at regular intervals: someone was walking toward me on two legs.

A young man appeared at the edge of the circle. His robes had been scholar-white before the road reduced them to the color of used bandages. A pack too large for his frame rode on his shoulders. He carried an open notebook in his left hand, ready for whatever he found.

He stopped at the brown line. His gaze moved from the dead grass to my face.

I had seen him before, at an inn, paying for soup with pieces of his cracked core. I remembered Wei across the table, watching me watch him. For months we had shared shelters with this man, making room without quite offering friendship.

He looked worse. Or better. The distinction required a baseline I no longer maintained.

"The kill-radius is approximately three paces," he said. He studied the line at his feet, assessing whether it was safe to cross.

I heard him clearly. An answer was out of reach.

He crouched, set the notebook on his knee, and wrote something with a brush that moved faster than his arrival had.

"You haven't moved for a while," he said. "From the shadows across these roots, I would say at least four watches. Possibly longer. Are you stationary by choice, or by constraint?"

I looked down at the root under my knees. The question reached me after a delay.

"Yes," I said.

He waited. The answer had not specified which. I had not specified because the distinction no longer applied.

He wrote that down too.

He looked at the ants rerouting along the circle's edge.

Xu Ran. I keep calling you that while you sit here listening to me. Your hand was on the notebook even then. This is absurd. I am talking to you.

You looked at the ants, the berry, and the brown grass. "Observable phenomena," you said. The same phrase you had used at the inn. You could find something worth recording even in me. "You qualify more than most."

I sat. You stood at the border.

The circle held. The ants continued their detour. The bird did not return.

You told me later you almost turned around. That you stood at the edge and considered leaving. You should have.

You wrote until the light failed, then lay down on the green side of the border. I stayed on the root until rising seemed as ordinary as another breath. I had no better reason than that. When I stood, I left you sleeping.

To the left of the oak, a path ran through the undergrowth. Repeated passage had worn a narrow line into the forest floor. Deer had probably made it, or other animals using the same route often enough to leave bare earth behind.

I followed it. The path supplied a direction. That was enough to keep me walking.

The brown circle moved with me. Three paces in every direction. Grass grew ahead of my feet; by the time I passed, it had withered. A broad strip of dead blades followed me through the undergrowth, far wider than any track my feet could leave.

Large trees lined the path. Where their trunks came within reach, the bark dried and leaves drooped overhead. Farther out, the branches were green. The boundary was sharp enough to follow through the canopy as I walked under it.

I walked.

A berry hung from another branch. This was a different bush from yesterday's, but the fruit had the same red skin. My hand swung at my side, and I did not reach for the berry.

It was still red when I passed its branch.

Night came gradually under the trees. Sunset lit the upper canopy while the path was already dim. Soon I could see only the gaps between the branches.

I walked.

I continued through the night. To stop, I would have had to choose somewhere to stay. The path offered no such difficulty; I could simply follow it.

Stars appeared in patches overhead. I kept my eyes on the ground.

A rustle of feathers brought them up. A small dark bird perched an arm's length away, close enough that the ants would have diverted. It turned its head. Its bright eyes caught what little light reached the branch.

Every feather shuddered at once. It shot upward through the leaves.

For the first time in days, something alive had come within reach. I looked at the empty branch, then walked on.

I walked.

The brown circle did not change size. Past it, the forest ran on farther than I could see.

And the bird, gone.
