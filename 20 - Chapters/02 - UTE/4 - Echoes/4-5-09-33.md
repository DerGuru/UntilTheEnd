You showed me the notebook on a morning when the headache was absent. The air was cold and moist, like rain that hadn't arrived yet.

It was a second notebook, bound in clean leather. The passages you read to me each morning had outgrown the margins of the first, and your handwriting had shrunk with each addition. Here you had given the same material room to spread across new pages.

You set it on the dirt at the edge of the fire ring, between us, and turned the open pages toward me so I could read them from where I sat.

The handwriting was larger than your usual script. You had grouped the observations under headings, with space between sections. I could follow the lines without leaning close. You had arranged them for someone else to read.

You had been writing two notebooks. One for the record. One for me.

"Read it," you said. "From the beginning."

You sat cross-legged beside a cup of tea balanced on a flat rock, steam rising. You looked tired. I had seen you writing late by firelight. Now I knew what had kept you awake: whole passages copied into this larger, generous script, for me.

The beginning was a timeline. My events, my experience, documented through your eyes. Day 1: no response. No food, no direction.

A sparrow landed on the rock beside your tea while I read. It turned its head toward the cup. You sat still and let it stay, waiting for me.

Patterns emerged from the timeline.

You had marked an involuntary qi pulse three days into the record. On the tenth day came another, stronger one. Later entries came closer together, and each pulse was stronger than the last.

Then the emotional markers. Your analysis, written in the neat handwriting that was yours: *"Every strong emotional event precedes a qi-pulse by 3-7 heartbeats. Anger: strongest correlation. Grief: moderate. Frustration: moderate-to-strong. The emotional events appear to trigger the autonomous qi-response, not the reverse."*

I read that sentence twice. The sparrow flew away.

I remembered the path that had appeared when I was frustrated. It had looked years old, though there had been no path shortly before. I had wished for easier going. Then we had found it. The stone had cracked when I pushed against the resistance inside me. Warmth had risen in the clearing when I enjoyed the fight.

I read your sequence again. In each case I had felt something before the world changed. I had never set those moments side by side. Your record did it for me, and the order was impossible to overlook.

I was the trigger.

The headache hit.

Pain drove behind my eyes as the pattern began to make sense. I knew this obstruction by now. The open notebook blurred.

I closed my eyes. Pain crowded out the morning: the tea, the page, and the tired man across from me. Nothing was left but the point behind my forehead.

"Breathe," you said from a few steps back. You had learned to give me space when a headache began.

I counted fifty heartbeats, the longest attack yet. Each number was small enough to hold while the pain went on. Beyond it, you were still waiting.

When I opened my eyes, your tea was still steaming and the notebook lay open between us. The larger handwriting was readable again.

I reached for it.

The headache returned.

My hand stopped half a handspan above the paper. I hadn't even found the passage yet. Reaching for it was enough to bring the pain back behind my eyes. I held my fingers there, unable to lower them.

Something inside me could read my intentions.

I had meant to read the passage again, and that was enough. Whatever opposed me could interfere before I reached the words, while I was still deciding.

The page lay under my extended fingers, perfectly legible. I still couldn't make myself return to it.

"What did you see?" you asked. The first notebook lay open in your lap. You had begun another entry.

"Before the headache. What did you see?"

I reached for what I had understood. Something about emotion. A sequence, an order I had finally recognized.

It was gone.

Pain still lingered behind my eyes. Somewhere in the counting, the understanding had slipped out. Your pen hovered above the smaller notebook. You were waiting for me to tell you something I had known a moment ago.

"I saw something," I said. "I don't remember what."

You nodded, unsurprised. The second notebook lay open on the dirt between us.

"I'll show you again tomorrow," you said.

I could picture it: the same notebook, the same careful handwriting, tea steaming beside you while I read. If the headache came again, you would still have the pages the morning after.

You closed the second notebook and slid it into a waterproof case inside your pack. You had bought the case in a town we passed through, for these pages alone. Whatever happened to my memory, the record would stay dry.

"The direction holds," you said. "Even if you forget the details, the direction holds."

I still wanted to understand what lay inside me. The cliff had given me that direction. The passage was gone, but the need to keep looking wasn't. I held to it while you stowed the notebook.

You would keep the details. I would keep looking. After so many days of failure together, we had arrived at that arrangement without discussing it.

I stood. The headache's residue was a bruise behind the eyes. The memory of the pattern was gone. The direction was intact.

When we moved, your tea had gone cold on the rock. The sparrow had not returned.
