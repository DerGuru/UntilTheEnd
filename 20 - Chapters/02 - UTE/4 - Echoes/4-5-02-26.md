Still, dark water filled the crater below us. Four hundred years of rain and groundwater had gathered in the bowl, making a lake fifty paces across. Erosion had rounded the rim; new growth softened its edge. Small temples lined the eastern shore, with stone foundations, wooden roofs, and copper ornaments green with age. Incense drifted across the water. Below the ridge, pilgrims followed a worn path toward the shrines.

None of them knew they were praying on a grave.

I knew what had made this place. The last ascension I had stopped had released enough energy to carve the bowl into the mountainside. Lava had followed. I had been caught in it, under what was now dark water. My body should have ended there.

It didn't.

We stood on the western rim, away from the temples. Wind moved across the lake, carrying incense and the mineral taste that volcanic ground never fully lost. Sulfur still showed under the smoke, faint though it was.

You stood fifteen paces behind me, breathing heavily from the descent. My quicker pace had stretched the gap. The slope taxed your damaged core more than the road had.

"This is one of the places?" you asked.

"The first."

"When?"

I looked at the lake. At the temples. At the green copper.

"The crater is old. The lake took four hundred years to fill. The temples came after."

Your pen moved as you recorded the date, the place, and what I had told you. Then you looked at the temples across the water. You added something else that I did not ask to see.

I descended to the waterline.

The western shore was rockier than the temple side. Nobody had worn a path through it. Dark volcanic basalt broke through the soil at the water's edge, dense under the weathered surface.

I knelt to put my hand on the stone.

I jerked my hand back. The cold of the stone lingered in my palm. I looked for a mark on the skin, for whatever had made the contact so hard to bear.

My hand looked ordinary. So did the basalt. Whatever I had felt had lasted only an instant. I held my fingers above the surface. The sensation would not come back without touching it again.

I knelt there for a long time. Water lapped against the basalt. Across the lake, a temple bell called the afternoon prayers. Its sound carried cleanly over the water, lingered, and faded.

I put my hand on the stone again.

My hand recoiled even faster this time. I had meant to hold it there. Instead I crouched with my fingers drawn against my chest and stared at the place I had barely touched.

On the third attempt, I pressed my palm flat against the surface. Again I needed to pull away. I forced my hand to stay through two heartbeats, then three. The smooth stone chilled my palm.

Under that cold lay a pressure I could scarcely locate. It lasted as long as I held the contact and came from a direction I could not point toward. I had no name for it. Its texture recalled what had happened here.

Four heartbeats. Five. My fingers began to tremble against the basalt.

I lifted my hand. The trembling stopped immediately.

The basalt was smooth. No mark or residue accounted for what I had felt. Water washed over the place where my palm had rested.

I moved along the waterline, touching other stones. All were basalt from the same formation. Some I could hold easily; others made me snatch my hand away. The strongest response came from those nearest the crater's old center, beneath the deepest water now. Farther away, I found it easier to keep my fingers against the rock.

I worked from stone to stone, remembering the strength of each contact. Gradually a rough boundary took shape along the shore. I still could not explain what I was tracing.

You watched from the rim, your pen moving over the page.

When I climbed back, my hands were wet from the lake and shaking. I pressed them against my thighs until they stilled.

You had the notebook open.

"The first time you touched the stone. You pulled your hand back."

"Yes."

"Why?"

I opened my mouth, closed it, and tried again.

"It wasn't hot. It wasn't painful. It was—" I looked at my hand. "Like the stone didn't want to be touched. No. Like something between me and the stone didn't want the contact. A—" I stopped. The words were wrong. All of them. "A direction. A pressure. But not from the stone. From—"

I gestured toward the lake, then toward myself. Neither direction quite matched what I meant.

"From inside?"

"I don't know."

"From the stone?"

"I don't know."

You waited with your pen still.

"The third time, when I held on. There was something underneath. Not qi. Not heat. Something that had—" I pressed my palms together. "Texture. Like a memory that wasn't mine. Or was mine but I couldn't—"

I stopped to shake my head.

"Sorry. I can't explain it. Not now. But there was something there. I know it was there. It was real."

You wrote that down. Then you read back your observations: three contacts, the five heartbeats I had held the third, and the tremor that followed. I had spent longer than a quarter watch testing stones along the waterline. You had recorded the weakening response as I moved away from the center.

The clinical language filled the gaps my words could not.

"Now what?" you asked.

I looked at the lake. The temples. The pilgrims on the far shore, carrying offerings to a place whose real history would terrify them.

One old site. Multiple contacts. A pattern I could map but not interpret.

I pressed my palms against my thighs again. We had found something. Now I needed to learn how to reach it.
