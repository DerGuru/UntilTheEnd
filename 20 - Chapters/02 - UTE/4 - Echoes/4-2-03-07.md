You stopped under a pine we had passed the previous day. I halted ahead of you, and you kept your usual distance while looking up at the branches. Something about their shape held your attention.

"Was that already like that?"

Yesterday I had walked this same stretch of forest without finding anything unusual. I remembered the trunks beside the path, though I had not attended to them. Now I looked where you were pointing.

The pine was a century old or more. Its thick trunk carried lateral branches that had grown heaviest toward the southern light. Years of uneven growth had given the crown its settled shape.

Now those branches reached farther east than I remembered. The change was small enough to overlook in passing, but standing under the tree I made out the angle. It did not match yesterday's.

"You're seeing things," I said.

The words came out flat. They were the first full sentence I had offered you since you returned, if this counted as offering.

You wrote it down.

"I'm not," you said, to the notebook. "The branches. They were different yesterday."

"They're trees."

"Trees don't move."

"You've been in the forest too long."

You looked down from the branches. Apparently my speaking deserved as much attention as their change in direction. You had waited days for more than a word from me. Now you had several sentences.

You recorded them without comment, then walked closer to the tree. One hand touched the bark. You held the other up to estimate the distance from trunk to branch-tip, checking the angle against your notes.

"It could be nothing," you said.

I looked past the tree. After the night at your fire, I found no interest in a branch having changed direction. You were still examining it when I spoke again.

"Trees grow," I said. "Branches shift. Wind. Weight. Snow."

"Not overnight."

I let that pass. Those few sentences had cost more effort than the walk. You waited, then turned back to the pine.

The branches were wrong. I should have paid more attention to wrongness.

You sketched the tree with quick, precise strokes. Branch angles, compass directions, and a date filled the page. You were still keeping track of which day we occupied. Your pen moved through the small details I had already begun to dismiss.

"I'll check tomorrow," you said. "If the branches have moved again, it's not wind."

You wanted to return tomorrow. Once I might have shared that curiosity and stayed under the pine to test my memory against its shape. Now your voice carried an interest that found no answer in me.

I started walking. You followed at ten paces, leaving the pine behind. More trees lined the path ahead. I gave their branches no more attention than I had yesterday.

After a quarter watch, we passed a shadow between two rocks. A tree stood next to them, with the sun clear enough overhead to make its shade distinct.

The shadow fell off its proper angle, and the trunk gave the discrepancy away. Like the branches, it was a small enough difference to dismiss.

I dismissed it.

You stopped. Your gaze moved from the shadow to the sun, then back to the ground. You wrote something.

"Did you—" you began.

"No."

"You didn't hear the question."

"The answer is no."

You wrote down the exchange anyway. Your pen moved while I stood by the rocks. You probably thought I was being defensive. At the time, admitting what I had seen was more trouble than it was worth.

The shadow still lay at the wrong angle. Thousands of years of watching sunlight had made that clear without any measurement. I could not have overlooked it if I had tried. Explaining it would have taken more attention than I could bring myself to give.

Was the shade different from yesterday, or from what the tree should have cast in any season? I left the question where it was. You were still looking from the ground to the sky when I moved on.

We walked.

Behind us stood the altered pine. Farther along lay a shadow that failed to match the sun. I could still picture both as the path took us through more ordinary-looking forest.

You wrote while you walked behind me. Whatever happened to the branches tomorrow, you would have a record of their shape today.
