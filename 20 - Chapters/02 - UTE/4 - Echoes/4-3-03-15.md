Spirit-beasts came at dawn. The bandits came moments later. The combination was coincidence or design or the universe being occasionally efficient in its distribution of problems.

You were awake for it so you saw it. You were hunched near the embers with your notebook already open, pretending you were only recording the weather.

Two spirit-beasts of different species. One was a cat-type built for forest terrain, the size of a large dog, qi-armored and fast. The other was something bovine, heavier, slower, a charger that relied on bulk and certainty.

Neither was dangerous to me. They were problems I had solved before problems had names.

The cat-type moved first. Fast enough that you flinched. Fast enough that the thought *it's-fast* formed and dissolved in the same breath.

Fast for what it was. Slow for what I was.

I stepped aside. Less than a body's width of lateral movement, using the beast's own momentum as the mechanism of its failure. It passed me. Its claws found the space I had occupied. The space was empty.

The bovine charged. Direct. Honest, like most heavy things.

It relied on the assumption that I would still be where I had been when it committed.

I wasn't. The horn cut air. The air was cooperative.

Two fingers on the cat-type's flank as it skidded past, a tap on the qi-meridian behind the shoulder blade. The bovine got the same, palm flat against its skull mid-charge, a pulse that found the central channel and closed it like a pinched candle. They folded. The sound of large bodies meeting ground.

I had dealt with both in the interval you later described as "less than I could track," which was your way of saying your perception, cultivator-enhanced but still fundamentally human-speed, had lost the sequence.

The bandits arrived as the beasts were falling. Four of them. Armed. Swords and spear, the armament of opportunistic predators at the margins of civilization. Perhaps they'd been tracking the beasts. Perhaps us. Perhaps both.

They saw the beasts, falling, unconscious, limp with a qi-channel shutdown applied with an efficiency older than their species' cultivation.

They reassessed.

The reassessment took about as long as the fight. A breath, maybe two. Engaging the person who had just incapacitated two spirit-beasts in a fraction of a heartbeat was not a favorable calculation.

Two of them ran. The other two, stupid enough that survival pressure hadn't yet eliminated them, advanced.

The first one reached me and I let him.

He swung. A sword, functional and maintained, cared for like a professional's tool. Horizontal arc. Standard. An attack that worked against targets unable to avoid it.

I could have avoided it as I had avoided the cat-type, as I had avoided the bovine, as I avoided everything that came at me, with the casual ease of a body doing this for millennia. I could have stepped aside. The sword would have found air, the bandit the ground.

I didn't.

The sword caught my sleeve. Left upper arm. The fabric tore with the sound of woven material separating under a sharpened edge. The blade went through the fabric and into flesh. My flesh.

Pain.

The pain was clear. Unfiltered. The nerves fired, and it was the first thing in weeks that was completely, undeniably, unambiguously real.

I breathed it in. Literally. The warm scent of my own blood. Mineral, old, the scent that predated humanity.

Blood. My blood. Inside me a moment ago, outside now, warm and wet on my arm. Real.

The bandit saw the blood. For one breath his face showed triumph: he believed the blood proved mortality.

Then it healed.

With a short delay. For one second the wound gaped, the blood flowed, the pain lasted. The delay was mine. I held it. Not consciously. Like a held breath, the suspension between intake and release.

One second of pain. Then the flesh closed. The skin sealed.

The bandit's expression changed, from triumph to something without a standard name, somewhere between disbelief and religious terror.

He swung again, the terror still in his eyes, the arms committed, muscle memory overriding what the mind had already understood. The second arc came faster, desperation's contribution to technique.

I caught the blade. Two fingers. The edge stopped between them like a door against a frame. The steel vibrated once and was still.

His eyes went from my face to my fingers to the blade between them, and the sequence was a world rearranging itself.

A tap on his wrist. Same principle as the beasts: the meridian found, the channel closed. His hand opened. His knees followed. He folded next to the spirit-beasts, the three of them arranged like a lesson in the consequences of engagement. The second bandit was already running.

I was faster.

The same tap. The same meridian. He folded next to the first, the fourth body in a neat arrangement that the clearing had not requested.

I stood in the clearing. Two beasts unconscious. Two bandits disabled. My sleeve was torn, my blood drying and the pain already fading.

The fading was the loss. The pain was leaving, a subtraction I had tried to delay, the only thing that had been completely real.

Gone again.

You stood at the clearing's edge. Notebook open. Your face was pale: your data had outgrown your framework for the third time this week.

"You let him hit you on purpose," you said.

"Yes," I said. "I did."

You wrote it down.
