The pressure left the soles of my feet. For the first heartbeat of the fall I was weightless. My muscles tightened for a foothold that was no longer there.

Past that useless effort, there was quiet. I fell clear of the rim, the stone close at my side, moving freely through the air.

The cliff wall moved slowly at first. Bands of sediment crossed the gray face, laid bare by ages of weather, and each one stayed distinct as it passed. It was absurd to have time to examine stone on the way down.

One.

Wind began to press against my face. My robes caught the air, fluttering around my chest. The cliff wall slid upward faster as I dropped.

Two.

The fall quickened. Wind pulled my robes against me in brief, hard jerks. Loose fabric snapped against my skin while the striations blurred. Above me, the high blue sky receded.

Below me, gray rock spread across the base of the cliff.

Three.

The wind roared. My hair whipped upward. I could no longer pick out the bands in the cliff face. Tears streamed across my temples and vanished into the air behind me.

I closed my eyes.

Four.

Air pressed against every exposed patch of skin. My robes clung like wet cloth, their loose folds flattened against me. The roar filled my ears. I had heard storms from shelter often enough. Here there was nothing but the wind around my head.

Under the roar, something returned to me. The weightlessness thinned. The air was bearing against my body, taking back a share of the weight that had vanished when I stepped off the rock.

Five.

The roar deepened and vibrated through my ribs. I was still gaining speed, though each heartbeat brought less change than the one before.

Six.

The wind stopped increasing. The cliff streamed past at a steady pace. I had reached, or nearly reached, the speed at which I would meet the rock.

My full weight was back, as clear as it had been on the ridge, with nowhere to put my feet. The air was the ground, and the ground pushed back.

Rock dust reached my nose, a dry mineral smell carried through the wind from the ancient face beside me.

Seven.

Constant. The wind held. The roar held. One heartbeat followed another with scarcely any change in the fall around them.

Eight.

Nine.

I had fallen a little over a third of the way. The rest lay below me, and at this speed it came to ten heartbeats, give or take. That was the interval left.

I stopped counting and turned inward.

Qi circulated through its channels as clearly as my pulse, sustaining my body without any effort from me. Its rhythm continued through the fall unchanged.

But there was something else, too.

Under the channels your cultivation theory described lay something I could not place. The familiar structures rested on it. I had no proper name for it. A substrate, perhaps.

And the substrate stirred.

The movement was faint at first, small and deep beneath the qi. I held my attention there while it gathered, like something approaching wakefulness.

Warmth bubbled up.

It expanded from beneath my core and reached outward through my body, entering the channels I knew before spreading along paths I rarely noticed. It felt older than the qi I had learned to circulate.

Wind, pressure, and the smell of rock held. Inside me, warmth reached one meridian after another. I followed its slow progress while the cliff streamed past my closed eyes.

I waited for the ground.

The rock should have reached me. Had another dozen heartbeats passed since I stopped counting? I had expected less time than that.

The warmth became pressure beneath my breastbone, a hand pressing against my chest from within. The pressure grew.

Each heartbeat stretched impossibly long. Wind roared while the warmth grew into burning. Rock dust thickened on my tongue and caught at the back of my throat. It felt as though I had waited through whole watches. Under the burning something went on gathering, though I could not name what it was preparing to do.

The drop was finite. I had counted enough heartbeats to reach its end. The rock should have met me.

Why hadn't it?

The wind changed.

Pressure eased against my face. The roar diminished, enough to hear the change. My speed had been steady for some time. Now it was decreasing.

I opened my eyes.

Something was stopping my fall.

The rock approached more slowly now. Whatever held me came from within, opposing the fall a little more with each heartbeat.

I slowed further. The gray rock rose toward me, near enough now to show its rough patches. The warmth in my chest intensified. The inward pressure bore hard against my heart, doing something I had never learned to do.

I drifted toward the ground as slowly as a falling leaf.

Floating.

For two or three heartbeats I hung motionless just above the gray rock. My robes settled around me. Even the wind had fallen quiet.

Then I descended the last short distance. My feet touched stone gently. Weight returned to my soles.

I stood.

I stood upright at the foot of the cliff, intact.

I brushed my robes with my hands. The gesture came easily, though the long fall had already shaken the cloth clean.

The silence was back, as complete as at the rim. I listened for wind along the cliff face. My own breathing sounded loud at its foot.

Then the warmth withdrew from my chest. Cold spread into my joints, my fingers, and places under the skin that weather never reached. It came from inside. Whatever had caught me had taken the heat with it.

My hands shook. My knees trembled; my jaw shivered hard enough to make my teeth click.

I stood alive on the gray rock. That much I knew. The trembling was new.

This time I had felt what accompanied my survival: the first stirring, the spreading warmth, and the pressure against my chest. The ground had slowed toward me until I stopped.

I had observed how I survived.

Something inside me had refused the fall its ordinary end. I had never asked for that refusal. Yet here I stood, its aftermath shaking through my hands.

The shivering continued. The cold held.
