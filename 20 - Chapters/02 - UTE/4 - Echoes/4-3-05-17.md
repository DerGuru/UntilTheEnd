The teahouse stood at the intersection of two paths that had probably both been there before we arrived. Probably. The distinction between geography-that-existed and geography-that-seemed-to-have-always-existed had become unreliable.

It was a small wooden teahouse that served travelers. Low ceiling, open front, the smell of boiled tea, wood smoke, and old food ground into the floor by a decade of feet. It existed because intersecting paths justified tea.

You entered first, pack on your back, notebook in your hand, greeting civilization like a desert traveler greeting water, with practical urgency rather than relief.

You ordered tea, nuts, and bread, the combination your preferences and your budget could stretch to. I assumed the budget was not extensive.

I followed you to the teahouse but not into it. The open front showed me the interior: low ceiling, earth floor, patrons at wooden tables. Small enough that three paces covered most of the room.

I sat on a stone outside. Ten paces from the entrance. The arrangement that the arrangement required. The grass around the stone began yellowing within moments, the circle expanding outward like a slow stain.

The houseplant by the window, just inside the circle's reach, began browning.

The proprietor, middle-aged, shaped by daily labor, with the social pragmatism of a livelihood that depended on reading people, glanced at me through the open front. At the woman who had chosen stone over chair, outside over in. His expression turned careful. He did not approach.

"And for the lady?" he said to you.

"Don't ask," you said.

The proprietor nodded. He went.

You sat at a table near the open front, close enough that voices carried and I had you in view through the entrance. The distance between us: ten paces, held. Your knees under the table. My feet on dead soil, in the open air. The stone was cold and adequate.

The stone's surface darkened where I sat, the slow aging my contact produced.

An old dog lay by the entrance, long since tolerated on the threshold by the proprietor. It lifted its head when I sat down on the stone, sniffed, stood, and moved inside. Elsewhere.

You ate. Methodically. You tore the bread into pieces, ate them in a fixed order, took the nuts one at a time, and sipped the tea instead of gulping. All with the disciplined efficiency you brought to biological necessities.

Food appeared at the edge of the entrance. The proprietor had set a bowl on the threshold: rice, pickled vegetables, and tea. It was rural hospitality that assumed every traveler was hungry, placed at the greatest distance his courtesy would allow. The bowl lay between inside and outside, between his world and mine.

I did not touch it. Food had stopped registering with me.

You opened your notebook and wrote your miniature letters, the pen in its rhythm.

The notebook was visible from outside, its pages illegible at this distance in your miniature hand. Leather-bound, worn from constant handling, filled from front to back with the density of months of daily writing.

I registered the notebook as an object, the first time I had been conscious of it as a physical thing rather than a behavioral symptom. You cared about it, wrote in it, and protected it from rain. Its thing-ness was new, though its function had been observed since day one.

Something. A question, below the disconnection, in the space where questions formed without the mind's permission, as the river-impulse had. What do you write in there?

The question had no urgency. It existed as the food existed: present, unacted-upon, occupying space in the perceptual field without generating response.

Voices came from inside, carried clearly through the open front: farmers, travelers, the midday population of a rural teahouse.

Conversations about weather. About crops. About the rumor of withered forests in the mountains.

"—touched a tree," one of the farmers said. Not to us. To his companion at the adjacent table, in the quiet, comfortable voice of a man passing on secondhand knowledge polished into certainty. "She touched a tree and it died. The whole forest. Everything she touched."

His companion nodded and sipped tea. He had heard this before, or a version of it, and was satisfied with its shape.

"She didn't touch anything," I said.

Both men looked toward the entrance, at the woman on the stone outside who hadn't been part of their conversation and had strong opinions about a legend she shouldn't know.

"She just walked through the forest. That's all. She didn't touch the trees. She walked. And everything around her withered."

The farmer blinked. His companion said nothing. The silence had the texture of a social miscalculation: the woman outside, on a stone, correcting their legend through the open front.

"I have it on good authority that she touches them," the farmer said, defending his story.

I said nothing. The farmer returned to his companion. Their conversation resumed at a lower volume. In their version, the withered forests were still a matter of touching.

Your pen had stopped. You were turned toward me through the open front, and recognition crossed your face: a behavior you hadn't seen before, filed in a category you hadn't known you needed.

You didn't ask. You wrote something. One line. The pen resumed.

A child laughed. The sound, clear and careless, cut through everything as only children's sounds can.

The sound entered. It registered. It produced almost nothing. The almost was new.

An old man at a table near the entrance leaned toward you, the lean of rural sociability that precedes unsolicited observation. He glanced at me outside, then back at you.

"Your wife doesn't eat much," he said.

Your pen stopped. Your mouth compressed, the expression you made when reality handed you something you hadn't ordered.

"We're not—" you began.

"Keep moving," I said. From outside.

Silence. A long one. The old man leaned back, farther than he'd been before. Your pen hung suspended. The wind brought the smell of earth floor from the entrance, of old tea, dried mud, and the minerals of ground walked on for years.

Your pen resumed.

I remember this teahouse for the old man's comment, the silence that followed it, and the fact that neither of us corrected him.

We left shortly after. You had finished your bread, nuts, and tea. My rice sat untouched, my tea gone cold.

The notebook was between us now, a third party: the record of our coexistence had become a participant in the coexistence.

You wrote. I moved. The writing followed my movement as my movement followed the path that followed terrain, which may or may not have been edited by an impulse that may or may not have been mine.

The path continued. We continued with it.
