We stopped beside a narrow stream carrying cold water down from the mountains. You squeezed it through your filtering cloth into the canteen. I sat on a flat stone. It was comfortable, a distinction I had begun to register again over the past several days: no root pressing into me, no uneven ground. Earlier I wouldn't have given it a thought.

The second notebook lay in your pack. You had shown it to me that morning, the headache had come as usual, and once it passed the pattern was gone again. You had put the notebook away for tomorrow. Water dripped into the canteen. I considered what else I could try.

I still intended to find what was hiding inside me. Losing the morning's understanding had done little to change that.

Water splashed softly through the narrow channel. You squeezed the cloth again. Nearby, a beetle crossed the rough surface of a rock between small hollows.

I reached for your pack.

You looked up from the filter. Your hands stayed on the cloth. You watched me take something from the pack and left me to decide what to do with it. I had grown used to that restraint, though your silence could still surprise me with how much room it gave.

I took the second notebook. I opened it to a blank page at the back.

The blank page brought no pain behind my eyes. Whatever opposed my reading had left this empty paper alone. There were no patterns on it to follow, and I could look from one edge to the other without losing the small intention that had brought my hand to your pack.

Good. I tore the page out.

Paper tore loudly against the quiet stream. The fibers parted along the binding. At least this was an action I could finish without losing it halfway through.

You set the filter down. The dripping slowed as you turned your attention to the torn page.

I placed the page on my knee. At the bottom of your writing kit lay a brush, clean despite how rarely you used it. You preferred the pen. I took the brush and tested the soft bristles. You maintained even the tools you seldom needed.

Ink stood ready next to the kit. I held the brush above the page. For many days I had called the thing inside me a substrate, a provisional name for whatever lay beneath the qi I knew. I understood little more than when I first used the word.

Since the cliff, I had asked what it was. Reading your notes brought me closer to an answer until pain intervened. I needed another question, one I could hold long enough to follow.

I wrote: *Why.*

I set the brush down.

Why. The wet word stood clear on the torn page. Why would something hide from me? Why would it remove what I learned? What made understanding dangerous enough to prevent? Its reasons might tell me what it was.

The headache did not come.

I waited through five heartbeats, then ten. The page was still legible. My head was quiet. This question didn't provoke the pain that had stopped my reading. For now, asking about its purpose was possible.

That gave me somewhere else to begin.

I folded the page twice into a rectangle small enough to hold between two fingers. It was warm from my knee. Wet ink smeared along the fold and blurred the word's edges, but the question stayed readable when I eased the paper open. That mattered more than neat handwriting.

I slid it under my belt, against my skin. If I forgot the question, I could take it out again. Your notebook had survived my failures of memory. This scrap might do the same.

You picked up the filter again. Water dripped from the cloth. You said nothing about the page. After so many failures, we had learned to give a small success room before explaining it.

"I need to test something," I said.

"Test what?"

"Directly. I keep reading your notes, then losing the patterns before I can hold them." I touched the folded page under my belt. "I need to reach the source."

Your hands stilled on the cloth. You had seen what happened when I provoked a response. Your eyes went from the folded paper to my face, working out what I might mean by reaching the source.

"How?" you asked.

"I don't know yet."

That was the honest answer. I had a question now, and a direction. The cliff had shown me one way to make the thing respond. I could arrange another provocation, carefully enough to observe the result. I just hadn't worked out how.

You capped the canteen and stowed it in the pack. Each item went back to its usual place. You could find anything without emptying the whole pack, a skill perfected on the road.

"Tell me before you do it," you said.

I looked toward the stream. The beetle had crossed its rock and was climbing down the other side. Light moved on the water next to it. The folded page was warm against my skin.

"I'll tell you," I said.

I didn't.
