The path ended in a tangle of bramble. We had been moving east, or the direction your map called east. You carried it and consulted it; I left that directed engagement to you. The forest had thickened as the foothills gave way to the lowland density of old growth near rivers.

The thickening ended in a wall of competing growth, interlocked: impenetrable bramble, layer upon layer of hooked stems across our direction of travel.

"Dead end," you said, studying the wall. You ran your hand along a branch; thorns caught your sleeve. "We need to go around."

"Which direction?"

You glanced at me. The glance lasted longer than the question warranted. You were measuring the fact that I had asked. I did not ask things. Asking required preference.

"North is uphill," you said. "South follows the river drainage. Efficiency favors south."

South or north. Choosing required evaluative attention that was available but expensive, and the outcome didn't justify the cost, because north and south were equivalent in the absence of preference.

The obstruction frustrated me, clearly, despite the disconnection: a wall between me and forward. Forward was the only direction I had held to since departure. Now it was blocked. The wanting persisted below the severed line, simple enough to reach me where more deliberate desires failed.

I wanted forward. Forward was blocked.

Here should be a path.

The words formed around an impulse in the weight pressing against my forward foot: here should be a path.

The bramble opened.

I was looking straight at the branches. Their positions were fixed, the thorns sharply visible.

The path was there.

The path had always been there.

A gap in the vegetation, narrow, leaf-littered, wide enough for one person at a time. Old. Established. The edges were worn by years of use, the ground compressed by feet. It lay in front of my eyes, as real as the bramble.

Except it hadn't been there thirty heartbeats ago.

I stood at the entrance. I looked from the gap to the thorn still caught in your sleeve. We had both been looking at the same wall. Now there was a path through it, as ordinary as a tree I might turn to inspect. My eyes supplied no evidence of the moment before.

I stepped onto it. The ground was firm. The leaf litter was years' worth, the slow accumulation a forest lays down under its canopy. Years. On a path that had existed for thirty heartbeats.

Retroactive.

The word surfaced coldly, from behind glass. I had met it in different forms across different centuries. Applied after the fact. Back-dated. It was the nearest word I had for the old leaves under my feet.

The path had been made true backward.

I went on through the gap in the bramble.

Behind me, at ten paces, you followed, boots crunching on the litter. You stepped where I stepped. You followed the path that hadn't been there.

"This leaf litter," you said. Your voice held the strained neutrality of professional composure. "It is consistent with years of accumulation."

"Yes."

"The path was not here forty heartbeats ago."

"No."

"What are you?"

The question. The same words from the same mouth, in another place, another year, and another context. Damp leaves scraped my soles; I remembered the alley behind an inn, when you still had reasons to be afraid. Now you asked on a forest path, after nearly a month of ten paces and silence.

"You asked that once already," I said. "The answer hasn't changed."

You were quiet after that. The quiet showed in the absence of your usual commentary and in the closed notebook, the silence you produced when the data exceeded your framework.

The path ran forty paces through the bramble wall. Past it, the forest was open and navigable, the terrain the bramble had blocked.

We emerged. The forest ahead was normal. The path behind existed. That was as far as I could make myself think.

I did not look back. Looking back would be checking whether the impossible thing was still there. I was not ready for that confirmation.

"I need to document this," you said. Your voice had recovered its analytical register, but the notebook trembled when you opened it. "The path. The litter. The temporal inconsistency. All of it."

"Document what you want."

You wrote fast, the pen moving as if the observations might evaporate unless you trapped them on the page. Given what we had just walked through, not an unreasonable fear.

Almost severed. Almost disconnected. Yet I had felt frustration, and a path had appeared. Something was still getting through.

The forest opened ahead. I moved into it. Behind me, you. Behind you, the path that looked as if it had always been there, though we had found a wall of thorns only moments ago.

You studied your notebook over the fire the next morning, the page angled clear of the smoke.

"That path wasn't there yesterday."

You delivered the conclusion with the forced calm of a night spent processing it, as if it were a weather observation.

"Then you weren't looking," I said.

The lie came out cleanly. Automatic. A denial the mouth produced without requiring the mind's permission. I said it because it was what one said to keep the world intact longer. Plaster over a crack.

You knew I was lying. The compression of your features, that narrow almost-smile, told me. You wrote in your notebook. Later that morning, you tried something.

"There should be a bridge here," you said.

You said it to the stream we were crossing, ankle-deep, the kind that needed no bridge but would have been more convenient with one. You said it like an experiment, a hypothesis applied to a test condition.

Nothing happened. The stream was a stream. The banks had no bridge.

You shrugged. "Worth testing."

You stepped into the stream. Water poured into your boots. You made an involuntary sound as the cold reached your feet. Your face took it personally.

I said nothing and watched you wade. Both boots. Full immersion. You cursed your way across with the systematic thoroughness I had come to recognize as your response to the universe's indifference.

Your attempt annoyed me. You had presumed the thing with the path was replicable by anyone, a technique you could learn. I had to live with it.

Your testing, the stream, and the delay accumulated into an irritation I could distinguish from the background stillness. We were ankle-deep in cold water, picking our way between slippery stones. This should be easier.

Later, we climbed the far bank while you wrung water from your lower robes. Your broken core gave you none of my protection from moisture. Ahead of us, a path appeared.

It ran through dense undergrowth that should have required a detour. Narrow. Old-looking. It had worn edges, compressed earth, and growth patterns indicating years of use.

On the path stood a cairn.

Three flat stones, stacked and mossy, the trail-marker travelers built at intersections to show direction, navigational shorthand as old as walking. Three stones. Moss. Old.

The path had not existed six hundred heartbeats ago.

You stopped. You stared at the cairn. I studied your face, the color draining from it as you took in the moss. Your mouth tightened. The evidence was an arm's length away.

You opened the notebook. The pen moved fast. You filled the margins with miniature script, fitting more onto each page than it should hold. You captured the observation before you could question it, before explanations more comfortable than the truth could intervene.

"It's tied to you," you said. Not a question. "The trees. The shadows. The paths. They're tied to your frustration."

I stood on the path, its years of wear under my feet, looking at the newly appeared cairn. Moss covered the stones. I saw what you saw. Whatever confirmation you took from my looking, I left unspoken.

"Not your wishes," you said, writing. "Your frustration. I wished for a bridge. Nothing. You were frustrated about being slowed. Path." You looked up. "Is that correct?"

I recognized the analytical precision. You were testing a hypothesis. On me. On the reality that altered around me when I was sufficiently irritated.

I had been studied by scholars before. Dozens of them, across centuries. None of them had ever thought to test the scenery.

"I don't know what that is," I said.

That was true. The path had a past; so did the cairn. I had no framework for this insertion of geography into days we had already lived. I had frameworks for most things. Millennia of frameworks. This was outside all of them.

"But it happened," you said.

I said nothing.

You took one more look at the cairn. Then you laid your hand on the mossy surface, testing its solidity, its temperature, and the physical reality of an object that should not look this old.

"I'm going to document everything from now on," you said. "Every anomaly. Every path. Everything."

"You already document everything."

"More."

You closed the notebook. We moved on the path that looked old and was new to us. Both of us knew. Neither of us discussed it. It had happened, and we continued.

The cairn fell behind us. Three stones, mossy, years old to the eye and a few hundred heartbeats old in our experience.

The forest closed around us. Behind us lay the evidence. Ahead lay the question of what else was possible when geography altered around the person doing the moving.

You carried the question. I carried the frustration. The division of labor established itself as everything between us did: without discussion, through proximity.

You had questions. I had answers I didn't want.

The path continued.
