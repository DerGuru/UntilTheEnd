You stood at the edge of the forest when I came through a gap between two pines. Four days had passed since the clearing, perhaps six. Twenty paces separated you from the path I was following.

You waited quietly under the trees. You were visible well before I reached the gap. You made no attempt to attract my attention; you simply stood where I would pass.

Your shape stood among the trunks. You carried the same pack you still wear, its patches worn thin by many days on the road. As I approached, dried sweat reached me, and under it a medicinal bitterness. You had come a long way on foot.

The notebook in your left hand was already open.

I walked.

I continued toward the gap between the pines. You stood well clear of it, so I did not alter my course. Even when I recognized you, I found nothing to do with the recognition.

I passed you at twelve paces. You stood on the green grass, in the world of the living.

Your gaze followed me. You were entirely occupied with what you could observe: my pace, the grass at my feet, and the distance I kept from the trees. You let me pass without a greeting.

Then your footsteps sounded behind me. You matched my pace at twelve paces, keeping the gap steady. Your gait was uneven. You compensated on the left with each step, carrying your old damage with a practiced economy audible in the rhythm.

You followed for several watches, through stretches of forest and open clearings. Whenever the path changed under my feet, your steps adjusted behind me. You had apparently decided to follow until I gave you a reason to stop.

Most who followed me stopped when the grass died under their feet. You treated this as encouraging rather than concerning.

At nightfall I stopped in a clearing. You sat ten paces away, beyond the reach of whatever escaped me. The grass under you was still green.

Near my feet, blades browned against the soil. Farther out they faded to yellow before the damage stopped. You had chosen a place comfortably beyond that edge.

You opened the notebook and wrote.

I stood with my gaze on the space past you. The pen moved at the edge of my sight.

"My name is Xu Ran." Maybe you weren't sure if I would remember.

Your voice carried clearly across the clearing. You had given me your name at an inn once, when Wei was there to hear it. Your pen paused above the page. I could not tell whether you were waiting for me to remember that meeting.

But I said nothing.

"I've been searching for you for three months. I lost you again at the oak."

Three months. I looked at the pack next to you, its worn patches evidence of days I had never counted. You had spent them looking for me. I heard the number without being able to fit it around the distance I had walked.

"The withered forests were not difficult to follow."

You described the dead forest like a river useful for finding your way. Brown grass had led you for many li through the trees. Where that trace thinned, perhaps the quiet birds had helped you find it again. I had left you a broad trail.

I looked at the circle around my feet. In a living landscape there could hardly have been an easier trail. Each place I had stopped marked the ground more clearly than a footprint.

You continued writing.

"I'm going to sleep," you said, after a silence that lasted long enough for the stars to appear. "I'll still be here in the morning."

You said this to the clearing, or to me, or to the notebook. Then you curled onto your side on the green grass at the safe distance and closed your eyes.

I stood.

Beneath my ribs the low humming went on, as steady as it had been at the stump. Ten paces away, you slept on green grass. Even in the fading light the difference between your patch of ground and mine was plain.

Your breathing soon became deep and even. You had learned to sleep wherever you lay down. Here you lay in the open, within sight of something that killed grass, beetles, and spiders. Ten paces apparently satisfied you. Your judgment was either precise or poor; at the time, I could not tell which.

I did not sleep.

Morning came. I was still there.

You opened your eyes and sat up. I stood where you had left me, my eyes open. You looked me over, then opened the notebook. Whatever you thought of my unmoving vigil went onto the page.

Then you spoke.

"You don't eat."

You glanced toward some rice you had set four paces away during the night. It lay in a container on the yellow grass, safely beyond the brown. Only now did I attend to it. I faintly remembered you placing it there, though it had meant no more then than your setting down the pack. The sight of it woke no hunger.

"You don't sleep."

You recorded this as carefully as you had recorded my answer at the oak. Your attention was methodical. I could give you silence for a whole night; you would still find something worth noting.

"And you don't leave," you said.

You wrote.

I stood.

Ten paces separated us. Brown grass lay at my feet, yellow farther out, and green where you sat. The boundary had held all night. So had your place beyond it.

You were still there.

Morning light reached us among the trees while you talked about the soil. Another day had passed. You probably recorded its date in the notebook; I told it apart only by where we had stopped.

You spoke whether I answered or not. The weather occupied you for a while, then the terrain. This region's soil differed from that of the eastern provinces. You explained how its quality changed with qi density, which in turn affected cultivation. Walking ten paces behind me, you could apparently discuss an entire province without assistance.

You talked and I did not respond but that did not deter you.

"It's colder than yesterday," you said one morning. "Your radius seems tighter in cold weather. Have you noticed that?"

I looked at the brown edge. I had not attended to its distance since the clearing. Now you had given me something specific to look for, and I still could not tell whether it had changed.

"Or maybe it's not the cold," you said, writing. "Maybe it's the humidity. The correlation isn't clear yet."

You were studying me.

You made no attempt to conceal it. You measured the dead grass, noted the weather, and compared what you found in the morning with what you saw at dusk. If I walked, you watched the damage move. If I stopped, you recorded how far it spread.

"I will understand what you are," you said once, to the notebook more than to me. "And if you wish, I will leave afterward. Not before."

The notebook snapped shut. When I looked back you were still ten paces away, waiting for me to move or reply. I found no preference in myself for your staying or leaving. Your presence had become another part of the path.

But you were not Wei.

The difference was plain whenever I looked at you. You were angular where he had been compact. Your questions sought measurements he would never have thought to take. Where he had simply stood next to me, you found something to investigate. The comparison did not hurt the way I expected.

And your core was broken.

I had sensed the damage at the mountain inn with Wei. It was still clear under your breathing: a core healed crookedly, like a bone never properly set. The remnants of Core Formation cultivation were legible in it. You worked around the break, drawing less from the channels than they should have carried. The old damage had settled into a permanent shape.

You cultivated every night anyway, murmuring mantras as you pushed qi through damaged channels. You made more sound than the work required, and even between exercises you seldom left the clearing quiet for long.

"You can hear it, can't you," you said one night, between breaths. "The break?"

It was audible. After so many years among intact cores, I picked out the wrong note under each breath. You could soften it with care but never make it disappear.

I said nothing.

"Did you know," you said, opening the notebook, "that a broken core produces a resonance pattern? Different from an intact core. The harmonic is shifted, about a quarter-tone flat. Every broken core I've studied shows the same shift."

A quarter-tone flat. You had listened to other broken cores closely enough to know the sound. I looked at the open notebook and wondered how many pages that work had taken. You had probably studied them with the patience you now spent on me.

"Yours isn't broken," you said. "But it isn't normal either."

You wrote three lines before closing the notebook.

"I don't know what it is yet."

Your thumb stayed between the pages. You had found something you could not explain, so you would give it more time. Your voice carried no doubt.

You said "yet" as though the answer were simply farther along the road. You would still be there to write it down when we reached it.

Each day you set food six paces away, where you could find grass still safe enough to kneel on. Rice, dried fruit, and strips of preserved meat came out of your pack. You had provisioned yourself for months on the road.

I did not eat.

Rice dried in its container. Fruit darkened next to it, and the strips of meat stiffened in the air. You replaced each untouched portion with another. I had no response to give you for the notebook.

"Still nothing," you said, to the notebook. "She doesn't eat. Consistent. No variation."

You wrote down what I did, which was more honest than most people had managed, though I doubt honesty alone kept you there. You could study my refusal to eat without asking me to account for it.

I did not yet understand what silence meant to you. Looking back, I can hear it in the loud meditation, see it in those crowded pages. A record preserved what you had observed. You filled the space because you feared what might disappear if you left it empty.

You were still here.

I stood. I walked. I stopped. I stood. The cycle repeated and you repeated with it, a ten-pace shadow that catalogued it without trying to change it.

After several more days, you asked me something. Five, perhaps seven; you would have the date.

"Does it bother you? That I'm here?"

I looked at you across the usual stretch of grass. Your staying stirred no preference I could put into words.

I did not answer.

You wrote.
