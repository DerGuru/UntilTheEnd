The granite outcrop held the warmth of the fire. The last light was bleeding through the trees in long, amber strips, and the first moths had come to the flame, three of them, copper-winged, circling. I had cooked the rice, the routine, the serving. Your bowl sat next to you. You ate slowly, the careful rib-governed bites I had stopped counting. Somewhere below the outcrop, a cricket started. Then a second.

There was rice left in the pot. And for the first time in longer than I cared to measure, I wanted some. I took the pot and sat down with it near the fire, near you, near the warmth. I ate from the pot with the wooden spoon.

You saw it. The arrested motion of a man mid-bite. Your eyes went to the pot, to me, to the absence of ceremony.

You resumed eating. The corners of your eyes did something, the suppression of a smile. Respect. Don't name it. Don't comment. I held the spoon against my lips.

The eating continued. The fire settled: a log shifted and sent a brief column of sparks upward, and the moths scattered, then returned. The air had cooled. The granite under me was still warm from the afternoon sun, holding it the way stone does, slowly, reluctantly. The silence was simply present. Two people who had walked together and now sat together.

The grief was not gone.

The question, the *Why not protect Wei, too?*, the clamped-off condition that had defined my state since he died, was still there. But the search, the investigation, the direction, the inward pull, had given the grief a container. A vessel. A purpose. The grief was not less. It was housed, a structure with boundaries, so that it no longer occupied the entire cognitive space. There was room for other things now. For eating. For sitting. For walking to the pot and taking it.

The balance was progress and loss at once. I reached for a Wei-memory, deliberately, the voluntary recall that should have produced the image of Wei cooking, Wei's hands, Wei's way of cutting vegetables with a blade too large for the task.

Static.

A signal that existed but could not be received. A room where someone had turned off the light and left noise behind. The static was new. The memory-gaps had been silent, empty rooms. This was a room with something in it that I could not see.

The static lasted two heartbeats. Then it was gone. The memory did not arrive.

I could not tell if the memory was being erased or protected. The static was ambiguous.

That was all of it: the static, the two heartbeats, the absence. I did not analyze it. I did not tell you. I returned to the rice. It had cooled. The wooden spoon clicked against the rim of the pot, a small, domestic sound in a forest that was filling with night.

The eating continued. The fire burned. The balance, progress and loss at once, was the condition of all my existence. I did not know if the sum was positive or negative. I did not try to calculate it.

The fire burned lower now. One of the moths landed on the rim of your bowl, rested there for three breaths, and left. You went to sleep, and I took watch.

The fire needed tending: small branches, placed carefully, the geometry of airflow. The crickets had multiplied. A chorus now, rhythmic, overlapping, the sound of a forest settling into night.

I softly placed my hand against your ribs. Qi. Enough to ease the inflammation around the fractures without waking you. Your breathing deepened, the exhale losing its careful edge. A hundred heartbeats. Then I withdrew.

An owl called from the ridge above us, a different species from the one two nights ago, with a lower pitch and a doubled note. Something rustled in the undergrowth to the east. Stopped. Continued. Too heavy for a mouse, too light for a deer. A badger, perhaps, or a fox.

The moths had gone. The fire had shrunk to a core of embers, orange and gray, and I fed it another branch. The wood was damp and hissed before it caught. The smoke changed direction twice, following currents I could not feel.

Half a watch passed. I worked on your shoulder, where the tissue was still swollen and hot under the skin. Qi in small pulses, timed to your heartbeat, encouraging the body to do its own work.

The forest shifted. The crickets stopped, all of them at once, as if a conductor had lowered a baton. Three heartbeats of absolute silence. Then they resumed. Something had passed through.

Your breathing was steady. The scab at your temple had darkened further, the skin around it lighter. Good. Dry, closed, no infection. I checked the meridians near the wound, the minor channels that fed the scalp. Bruised but open. No qi needed there.

The deepest watches were the quietest. The owl had gone. The rustling had moved west and faded. Only the brightest stars showed through the canopy, framed by oak branches that looked like cracks in a dark ceiling. The fire was embers now, and I let it be. Your body heat and the residual warmth of the granite were enough.

I placed my hand against your ribs again. The same thread of qi. The inflammation had receded, measurably. Progress in fractions. I held the thread longer this time. Your face did not change. You slept the way injured people sleep, deeply, completely, the body commandeering all resources for repair.

Then the air changed.

The air cooled a notch, maybe two. Moisture was gathering, rain's precursor, humidity that settles into fabric and hair and makes everything heavier. And with it, rising from the forest floor, from the granite, the moss, the dead leaves, and the soil beneath, came the best scent in the world: petrichor.

This was the petrichor of anticipation, the earth exhaling in advance, the oils from the leaves and the minerals from the stone releasing into air finally wet enough to carry them. It was the smell of four in the morning in a forest that had been dry for days, specific and unrepeatable, filling the camp with something that was part scent, part temperature, part memory.

I breathed it in, the forest itself.

The gray came gradually. No sunrise yet, only the absence of full darkness. The canopy turned from black to charcoal. The oak branches became visible as shapes rather than silhouettes.

You stirred. The gray light found your face: the bruise at the temple, the line of the jaw, the careful way you held your left side even in sleep. You opened your eyes. They went to the sky, then to me.

"Morning," you said.

I had water warming on the rekindled embers, and fresh rice, enough for two portions. I served yours first: the bowl, the spoon, the routine. You sat up slowly, ribs announcing themselves, and ate.

I ate from the pot again. The second time was less remarkable than the first. That was the point.

The night was gone. The moisture was burning off, wisps of mist curling between the tree trunks, thinning as the light strengthened. The birds resolved into a proper chorus. The forest was awake.

We packed. I took the pack. You stood without assistance, slower than before the cliff, faster than three days ago. Progress in fractions.

We walked south.
