Cold stone pressed under my fingers as I climbed and brought back a night with Wei at the mountain settlement, during the wet season. Damp had come through the gaps in the walls. We had sat close to a fire that barely warmed the space around it.

Pine smoke hung over the smell of wet stone. Wei watched me across the fire.

He had asked a question.

He sometimes questioned me with the persistence other children reserved for avoiding work. That night he had given himself an assignment. At fourteen he had already observed enough to ask things I would have preferred him to leave alone.

"What is the worst thing that ever happened to you?"

His voice had been changing for months. Its unevenness was as clear to me as the question. Against the cold cliff, that small imperfection brought him close.

I considered my answer. He was asking me to choose one thing from a life too long to recount by a single fire.

"Waking up tomorrow."

Three words. I had a habit of making large truths portable.

He laughed.

He had expected a battle or some great historical catastrophe. I had offered him the following morning.

I didn't laugh.

My fingers found another hold, and I climbed on with his question in my head.

I climbed through the bands of gray stone I had passed during the fall. Weather had worn handholds into them over centuries. My fingers settled into those hollows while my feet found purchase below.

Waking up tomorrow was the worst thing.

Now I was climbing the stone after something inside me had stopped me from dying at its base. I held the same answer up against what had happened below.

Another morning would arrive whether I wanted it or not. Whatever had caught me would go on preserving my body. The low humming under my ribs followed me up the rock.

But my relationship to the worst thing had changed.

Before the fall I had endured each return of light because it came regardless. In the forest, morning had reached the trees and I had found nothing to do with it. The same hands now carrying me up the cliff had rested idle on my knees.

Tomorrow I could investigate what had happened. Another day was still bitter. At least I had something to ask of it.

I moved my hand to the next hold. I would be alive to see morning, as always. This time I intended to use it to pursue an answer.

What am I?

My fingers pressed into rough stone. The question gave me a reason to finish the climb, then to choose where we would go afterward. Everything that had made those days in the forest gray was still with me. But knowing where to look changed what I could do with the next one.

I climbed on.

Wei's laugh was still in my ears. Understanding what I was would not explain why he had died, though the two questions might touch somewhere. He had sat close enough to share a fire with me. Whatever kept this body intact had left him out.

In the forest I had asked why and found nothing to follow. Now there was a stirring beneath my qi, and I could return to it, however faint it was. For the length of the climb, having somewhere to begin was enough.

The top approached. The climb had left me without fatigue. You waited at the rim. My hands reached the edge where my toes had rested, the place I had stepped past with only those two words in mind.

I pulled myself up.

Wind touched my face as I stood. Insects, birds, and distant movement sounded among the trees. Sound had returned around the rim.

I looked down. Insects moved near my feet, within the radius. Birds called from perches I could pick out.

For so many days, small lives had failed within three paces of me. You had measured that distance by the oak. Brown trails through the forest had led you back to me. Now a bird called close by while insects crossed the grass at my feet.

You sat cross-legged three paces away, the notebook open on your knee, the pen moving in careful strokes. Recording what had happened gave you something to do while you waited.

I went to you. The grass stayed green between us. The radius had disappeared. The world had returned to normal.

I left the insects alone. This would need examining too. Something had changed during the fall; the grass was green where it should have browned.

You looked at my feet, then at the grass between us. Three paces, far less than the distance you had carefully kept through the forest.

You sprang backward. The notebook dropped as you unfolded your legs and scrambled out of reach. Months of keeping clear of the brown grass had made it a reflex.

Then you stopped.

One foot hung lifted while the other held your weight. You looked at the patch where you had been sitting.

The grass there was green. Your eyes went to your planted foot, then back toward mine.

You looked at me.

I said nothing.

You took in the grass, the insects, and a bird perched on a stone two paces from me. Then your gaze came back to my face.

Slowly, you sat back down, watching the ground as you settled onto it, testing whether anything would change. Then you retrieved the notebook and found your pen.

"Nearly half a watch," you said. "From landing to your return. You stayed at the base for less than a quarter watch. The climb took a little longer than one. Your pace suggests no significant injury."

"You timed it."

"I document." You closed the notebook, then opened it again. "The fall lasted about twenty heartbeats." Your pen hovered. "I was uncertain at first whether to use past tense. But then you slowed."

"Present tense," I said. "Apparently."

You studied me with your usual attention. Your knuckles were white against the notebook, the binding pressed deep into your fingers.

"What did you find?" you asked. "At the bottom."

"A question."

You waited. I did not elaborate. You wrote.

I stood by the same edge I had stepped from. Under my ribs the low humming went on. This time I was listening to it.
