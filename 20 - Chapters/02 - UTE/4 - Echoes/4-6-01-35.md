The poison was called Yinshi, Shadow-Eater. It ate the meridian structure from within, the way shadow eats light, and left nothing in its place.

I had known about it for centuries. I had found it long ago, passing through cities that no longer existed, abandoned sect libraries that had since become forests, a ruined apothecary whose ruins had been ruins even then. I had read scrolls that crumbled at my touch. The information lived in the gaps between official records, which told me the subject had been suppressed.

Yinshi killed Immortal-level cultivators. Reliably. Completely. In about ninety heartbeats.

One scroll documented its work. I had found it under a collapsed shelf in the ruins of the Qinzhou Apothecary, a sect destroyed eight thousand years ago for possessing, among other things, this knowledge. The scroll described the mechanism in precise, clinical language. The compound entered the bloodstream, identified the qi-meridian structure, and dissolved it from the periphery inward: the peripheral channels first, then the secondary, then the primary. The dantian last.

For an Immortal-level cultivator, dissolving the meridian structure was fatal. Mortal bodies survived without qi permanently. But at Immortal level, what held the body together had been rebuilt around the qi-channels themselves. The channels bore the load. Remove them and the body collapsed.

Ninety heartbeats. Complete dissolution. Unrecoverable. The cliff had been impulse, accident, discovery through falling. This was method.

You had the poison.

You had carried it in your pack, in a small ceramic phial wrapped in oil-cloth and set against your hip, where body heat kept the compound liquid. You had carried it since before you found me.

One evening we stopped at a stream. The routine of it: water, fire, the small negotiations of campsite geography. You swung your pack off your shoulder and set it against a boulder. The pack tipped sideways, the weight shifted, and a phial threatened to crash onto the rock.

You reached for it, but I was faster and caught it. The ceramic was cold and smooth in my hand. The phial was small, the size of a teacup or smaller, and heavy for it. I knew what it was before my hand closed around it. You don't carry a thing like that for centuries without recognizing its siblings.

I held it up.

Your face did what it did when you were calculating: a brief stillness, an assessment of options, the rapid discarding of lies in favor of the version of truth that preserved the most.

"How long have you carried this?"

"Since before I found you."

"For me."

You met my eyes. No flinch. "I didn't know what I would find. A cultivator at your level, alone, after what happened. I didn't know what state you'd be in. Whether you'd recognize me. Whether you'd be hostile."

The honesty was clinical. You had packed a poison beside your notebook and considered both tools of equal necessity.

"You didn't know it wouldn't work," I said.

"No." A pause. "I know now."

He did. He had seen the cliff. He had seen the substrate respond. He understood, better than anyone alive, that whatever held me together operated on a level that Yinshi could not reach. The poison dissolved meridian structures. The thing inside me was not a meridian structure.

You took the phial from my hand and put it at the bottom of your pack, under the cooking pot, the spare cloth, and the second notebook. You buried it thoroughly, like a chapter you meant to close.

I marked where you put it.

That night, while you slept, I took it. The pack lay by your head. I threaded a thin line of qi through my fingers, just enough to dampen the vibrations and absorb the small sounds fabric and ceramic made against each other. The technique was old. Older than sects. You learn that sort of thing when you have lived long enough to steal from people who could hear a heartbeat change rhythm.

The oil-cloth came out without a sound. The ceramic was cold now, watches away from your body heat, the compound inside thickening toward its storage state.

I put it in my belt, against the skin, beside the folded page with its single word.

I did not tell you.

I chose a location: a clearing south of our camp, open ground, no structures within the range I had estimated from the cliff data. The cliff-jump had produced the substrate response at about the moment of lethal threat. The poison would produce the threat chemically rather than kinetically. The response, if it came, would be observable.

The clearing was sufficient. Flat. Open. The nearest trees at thirty paces.

The calculation was wrong.

But I did not know that yet.

The folded page was against my skin. The question, *Why*, pressed into me through belt and cloth, the stubborn persistence of paper against flesh. Since the substrate stopped my fall I had wondered not just *what* it was but why it had bothered. The *why* had lived in the shadow of the *what* for weeks, patient, waiting for the *what* to exhaust itself against the wall. Writing it down had not created the question. It had given the question a body.

It was the beginning of the mechanism that would produce an answer.

I did not sleep that night. The wakefulness was different from my usual not-sleep: anticipation, the patched-together will holding, planning, alive.

The poison needed some warmth to liquefy first.

At dawn.
