The fire burned low, its smoke drifting east through the trees. Damp wood smoldered under the flame. I knew that smell from fires I had shared with Wei. It reached me while you were still opening your notebook.

"I came through the settlement," you said.

Your voice came through the hiss of wet wood. Smoke caught in my throat.

"The boy fought at the front." Your voice stayed level. "I'd heard about the settlement, rumors mostly. A cultivator protecting civilians. Unusual enough to track. I arrived the morning it happened."

You paused to write something before continuing.

"He was good. Better than he should have been, with that core. He moved like someone who had been trained by someone who understood movement at a level that most cultivators never reach. His footwork was—" You stopped. "Efficient. I'll say efficient."

Efficient. I pictured his bare feet finding purchase, his stubborn practice made visible in a movement. He had learned imperfectly. Even where he copied me, he was himself in it.

"I heard he killed one of them. Roughly. His cultivation wasn't at that level. But he killed one, and two others adjusted. That was the beginning of the end."

Smoke drifted past my face. The damp, green smell brought back a hut with a low ceiling in another season. The fire under that ceiling rose in my mind before your next words reached me.

"A light came from the west. Something, I still don't understand it. Something enormous. A presence. It distracted you. Two, maybe three breaths."

The Hermit on the western hill. His two-finger salute. I had turned toward him while Wei stood at the line. The Hermit could not have known what that glance would cost.

"Then they attacked on three fronts. They set a fire, endangered civilians, and placed a scout where the boy had been holding the line. He'd moved to help with the fire."

You were recounting a military failure. The settlers must have joined the fire to Wei's absence, making a sequence out of what they remembered. I stood in the smoke and heard where their account went wrong.

Wei had pointed south. Others went toward the fire. He ran when he heard civilians screaming. After he came back, he saw someone wounded beyond the gate. He ran again, out into the open.

Into the trap.

He had left a gap in the line by doing what he had always done: moving toward someone who needed help.

"I only heard it afterward. And there is nothing anyone could have done."

Nothing anyone could do. I looked at your hand resting on the notebook. So many deaths in my life had ended with someone saying words like those. Here there had been a running child. Wei had seen that child from the gap in the line, then moved.

Smoke scraped the back of my throat. Your account no longer reached me from the distance that had held everything since the hut. Each fact arrived plainly.

Wei died while I was distracted.

Wei died because he ran toward someone he thought was hurt.

Wei's core had been weakened long before it broke. The weakness was old. Years old. Years near me.

I had known all of it already. Hearing you say it brought the scene close enough to hurt. The smell of wet wood clung to me.

My qi jolted.

Leaves curled on the nearest tree, five paces away, their edges folding inward as the discharge passed through them. I had made no movement toward the trunk.

The hair on your arms lifted. You glanced up across the space between us, then opened your notebook. Your pen moved over the page.

I stood at your fire and remembered the one in the mountains where Wei had sat with me. That fire had long since gone cold. Here the damp wood went on smoking while you wrote.

You had given me your account. Now you let me stand with it and made notes at your careful distance. Your pen scratched steadily, a small sound that demanded nothing of me.

The distance inside me had narrowed. I could still look at the fire without moving toward its warmth, and I could still leave your food untouched. But what you had said about Wei pressed close, too painful to put aside.

He fought at the front. He ran toward the figure on the ground, into the space his enemies had prepared. His core broke there. You turned a page while I stared at the curled leaves, unable to get past that moment.

He deserved to live more than I did. Whatever kept repairing me should have been his. I could kill the grass three paces around my feet simply by standing there. I could survive wounds he never could. I had been left with all of it.

The smoke shifted. The fire dimmed. You closed your notebook.

The smoke brought someone else to mind, farther back than Wei. I followed that recollection because his last moments hurt too much.

A woman named Mei, eight thousand years ago, or close to it. Dates grow less certain after the first millennium. She lived near a river in a province whose name had since changed. She had taken me into her house with the practical hospitality of a woman used to travelers. All she expected in return was that I would eventually leave.

She cooked rice with vegetables from her garden. The seasons limited what she could put in a pot, but decades of practice made those meals worth sitting down for. She knew how long the rice needed over the heat. She salted the greens carefully. Her left index finger bent at the second joint; she held her knife around it as though the crooked finger were part of the handle.

I stayed three months, longer than anywhere else in that century. Looking at your small fire, I remembered how willingly I had sat down at hers. I had almost found somewhere I could call home.

By my twenty-first day there, she had developed a deep, wet cough. It worsened at night. I could have treated it easily, guiding warmth through the inflamed tissue. I had performed that small act hundreds of times.

I listened to her cough and did not treat her. Fourteen more days passed, then another fourteen, and the cough grew worse. I knew what was happening. Even now, with smoke in my throat, I found no reason for having let it continue.

She died in her house at the end of the third month. Her lungs failed slowly. She was conscious through it, with an immortal guest close enough to hear. I could have prevented it. I stayed while she died.

Mei, with her crooked finger, her patient cooking, and the cough I had left untreated.

The flame shrank under the wet wood. After all those years the sound from her room was still there. Whatever explanation I might once have offered myself was gone. The coughing remained, along with the knowledge that I had heard it.

Eight thousand years separated Mei from Wei. Other people lay between them in my memory. I had stayed near each of them, grown attached, and failed to keep them alive. At your fire I wondered how often I had repeated the same failure without learning to recognize it.

The fire settled to embers. You slept next to it, your breathing as even as ever. Each breath was clear now that the wood had stopped hissing.

I stood. The memories pressed close enough to hurt: the crooked finger around a knife, Wei's bare feet, and the figure beyond the gate. I could not set them out of reach.

Morning would come. You would write. I would stand.

The pattern would continue.
