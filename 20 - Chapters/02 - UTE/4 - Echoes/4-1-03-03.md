I had been walking for three days since the hut, perhaps five, when a beetle crossed the ground at my feet. Paths had carried me through some stretches of forest. Elsewhere I had walked between trees or across clearings without finding a track.

The beetle was small and black, one of the leaf-litter kind that broke down what fell from the trees. It carried something pale between its mandibles. It passed from green grass into the circle around me.

It stopped. Its legs folded under it, twitching against its body. The pale scrap stayed in its mouth. The legs went still.

I stared at the place where it had stopped, close enough to see its folded legs against the black shell.

The beetle was dead and I was not.

A branch overhung the path ahead. Its leaves were green when I stepped under them. A few heartbeats later, when I emerged, they had browned. The branch would recover once I was gone. I would not be there to see it.

Grass, insects, and leaves withered or died wherever I stopped. The smallest things went first. The damage spread from where I stood, the same distance in every direction.

Everything except me.

My chest tightened. I closed my hands. The skin across my knuckles was unmarked. The beetle lay at my feet.

Everything dies near me. Except me.

I could barely draw a full breath.

What protects me?

What closes my scratches? What repairs me after a fall? Something has maintained this body across millennia, through wounds that would have killed anyone else. Now a beetle dies at my feet while I stand here whole.

I looked at the dead branch overhead. Another question followed, one I had circled since the hut.

Why didn't it protect him?

Whatever keeps me alive has never needed my permission. Small wounds close before I notice them. Greater injuries heal while I watch. I have carried this body through whole ages without finding a way to make its survival stop. Even here, with dead grass against my ankles, its quiet work went on inside me.

Wei had stood next to me. He had lived in my house, prepared food, and slept close enough to touch. For all that time he had been closer than these dying leaves. If whatever guarded me could reach past my skin, surely he had been near enough. It only had to reach that little distance.

It had let him die.

I opened my hands. The nails had left no marks in my palms. Whatever happened to those around me, this body always emerged intact.

Why not him?

My qi jolted outward in a single involuntary pulse.

The radius expanded from three paces to five. Grass browned past the old perimeter. The green disappeared in a ring around my feet, faster than it had at the oak.

The trees at the edge shuddered. Leaves fell from their branches, drying before they reached the ground.

The air pressed against my chest. Each breath tasted metallic, too thick in my throat.

Then it passed. The pulse receded and the radius contracted to three paces. The dead grass stayed dead, but the trees at the edges recovered.

I stood in the center of the wider circle. Two more paces of grass had died because I had stood here asking.

Why not him?

For the first time in many days, something held my attention beyond the next step.

It sat under my ribs with each shallow breath. Hunger had faded. Sleep had become another thing I failed to consider. Yet I stood over a dead beetle with my fists closed, unable to leave the question alone. I had waited in the hut for something to reach me. Here it was.

I searched what I knew. I had seen so many wounds heal, watched so many bodies fail. None of it told me why his had been allowed to stop.

I walked.

Dead grass brushed my ankles as I went. Fallen leaves lay in the path ahead. Each small body I passed brought me back to the one answer I still lacked.

Why not him?

Water sounded ahead of me, running over stone. I had crossed a country full of little creeks since leaving the hut. Rainwater cut channels through the ground, yet I had passed them all without seeking a drink. This stream crossed my path.

I stopped on the bank. For days the forest had fallen quiet wherever I went. Here the water continued splashing over its shallow bed.

I knelt. After ten thousand years of drinking from streams, the movement needed no thought. My knees settled into the mud.

It was clear enough to show every stone on the bottom. The current ran quickly over them, breaking into small ripples. Once I would have found this place pleasant. I could almost hear what Wei would have said about it.

Not now.

I looked into the current. I began to picture him here, but the remembered voice never quite arrived. Water slipped between the stones until the attempt to remember subsided.

I cupped some water and drank. Somewhere between the hut and this bank I had stopped noticing thirst. My hands met under the surface, filled, and rose to my mouth anyway.

The water was cold in my throat. I registered its temperature and the sensation of it, but did not connect those two with preference.

At the bank, where my knees pressed into the mud, the grass was dying.

The damage spread slowly this time. Three paces around my knees, the blades began to lose their color. I had sent no fresh pulse into the ground. I was simply kneeling there, long enough for whatever escaped me to settle into the bank.

Green faded to yellow, then brown. The tips curled inward before the rest of each blade followed. In drought it would have taken days. Here it happened while I drank, little by little, until the grass nearest my knees lay dry against the mud.

Under my right knee an ant colony was moving. Its small vibrations spread through the soil, away from where I knelt. More insects moved outward under the dying grass. The ground was full of them, all retreating from the same place.

Everything was always leaving. Until only I was left.

Alone.

When I stood, two knee-shaped depressions marked the brown grass. Mud showed through the flattened blades. Anyone coming afterward could have seen precisely where I had rested.

I left the bank. More grass faded along the forest path as I walked.

A bird called from a low branch overhead. I knew the kind; their cries passed between the trees each morning. It stopped when I walked under it. A predator might have brought an alarm call or a sudden flight through the leaves. I heard neither. The branch above me was still.

I passed. Thirty paces behind me, the bird resumed.

A spider web crossed the path between two trees, level with my face. The spider sat in its center. I saw the threads and walked on instead of stepping aside. They broke against my skin. The spider landed on my sleeve, scrambled toward the cuff, and stopped, legs curled under it.

I flicked the dead spider off my sleeve. Debris.

I was the desert. I carried it through the trees, and the dry ground followed under my feet.

I had watched the circle since that first night by the oak. The ants had already shown me its edge. Now, with the dead spider behind me, it was no longer something merely worth noticing. Wei had lived nearer than those ants. He had spent days inside the distance that was killing everything around my feet.

Did I kill him?

Had it happened slowly, through all that time at my side? I had seen the beetle stop. I had seen the grass brown around my knees. Perhaps damage had accumulated in him too, too gradually for me to see before he failed.

His core had been cracked before the sect found us, before the fight. I had been with him through that as well. Perhaps my presence had widened the damage until his core could no longer hold.

No way to test. No way to confirm or deny.

The stream ran on behind me. Unaffected. The bank where I'd knelt was dead.

Grief pressed under all of it, and I could do nothing useful with that either. Another patch of grass browned under my feet.

Ahead, green undergrowth crowded the path. I walked toward it with the dead bank behind me.
