We saw the smoke in the late morning. A thin column, gray-white, rising above the canopy to the south. Too steady for a forest fire. Too concentrated. A chimney. A settlement.

The last time I had walked this path, there had been nothing south. Forest, river, more forest. No chimney. No laundry lines. No smoke. A few decades, and the land rearranges itself around people who were not there before. We would have needed two more days at least.

You stopped and leaned against a birch. The ribs. You didn't say it, but the lean said it. We had been walking for a watch and a half already.

"There," you said, pointing south with your chin. "Half a day. Maybe less."

We walked south. I kept looking back.

The settlement was smaller than the smoke had suggested: a handful of houses along a river, a bridge, a woman hanging laundry who watched us approach without alarm. I found a room. A bed. Paid with a coin from the pouch you carried. The bed was narrow, the blanket rough, the walls thin enough to hear the river. It was still the first bed in weeks.

You sat down on it. The mattress gave, straw rather than cotton, and the give made you close your eyes. The body's relief at a horizontal surface that was not earth.

"Stay," I said. "Rest. Eat something that isn't just rice."

You opened your eyes.

"And what will you do?"

"I'll be back before nightfall."

You waited. The question had not been answered and we both knew it.

I took the pen from your bag. You watched me do it. I held up the second notebook, the summary you had prepared for me.

"In case something comes back," I said.

You nodded once and drew your legs up onto the bed, carefully, ribs governing the geometry. Lay back. The ceiling was low, stained, unremarkable. You studied it as though it were the sky.

"Be back before nightfall?" you asked, but I was already moving.

Qi-speed is the removal of friction: air, gravity, the body's preference for slowness. The forest blurred. The trees became lines. The path we had walked in three days unspooled beneath me in moments, the landmarks arriving and departing in reverse: the stream crossing, the granite outcrop, the slope with the bracket fungi. At qi-speed, the world passes through you.

The clearing announced itself before I saw it. An absence of canopy. Light where there should have been shadow. The forest stopped, and what lay ahead was a circle of devastation.

I had remembered it wrong.

That was the first thought, standing at the edge, which was not where I had placed it. I had remembered thirty paces of shattered trunks, another forty of felled trees. A circle. Regrettable but bounded.

The felled zone ran closer to seventy paces. Past it lay another fifty paces of trees cracked but standing, held up by neighbors that were themselves cracked, listing at angles, canopies torn open. A hundred and twenty paces in every direction. A crater in the canopy.

The fissures in the ground were deeper than I remembered. Some had widened, from rain, maybe, or the settling of loosened soil. The earth itself had not recovered. The exposed roots of the downed trees were pale, dry, already dead. Nothing had begun to grow. The clearing smelled of resin, split wood, and, under both, ash.

I walked to the center, to the exact spot where I had stood when I drank the poison. Where my body had produced the thing that did this. The ground under my feet was compressed, packed hard by the downward force of the shockwave, the vertical component that had driven into the earth while the horizontal component had driven into the trees, into the man who now sat against a birch tree half a day's walk south, breathing carefully around broken ribs.

I sat down cross-legged with the pen in my lap and the notebook closed beside me. I shut my eyes.

The meditation was deliberate. I went inward, past the qi, past the meridians, past the channels I had been cleaning in your body for days. Into the deeper architecture. The place where the shockwave originated. The substrate. The thing-below-qi that I had no name for and that the erasures had taken my understanding of.

I reached for the watch and a half.

The understanding that the poison had unlocked: the clarity, the seeing, the brief window where everything had made sense, the connections had been visible, and the architecture had been readable. A watch and a half of knowing. Erased by the thing that protected me. Taken back.

I reached for the structure, the framework, deliberately. What had I understood? What had I seen? The channels, the pathways, the reason for the shockwave, the origin of the protection: all of it had been clear for a watch and a half, then taken, and the taking had left me with nothing.

I reached.

Static.

The same static as when I attempted to remember Wei, but louder. Denser. Like pressing your ear against a wall and hearing machinery on the other side, present, operational, inaccessible. The understanding was there. Its weight, its shape, the outline of something enormous behind the noise. But I could not see it or retrieve it.

I pushed harder. The static intensified. A pressure behind my sternum, the shockwave's echo. A warning. The mechanism that had erased the understanding was still active, ready to take whatever I recovered.

I stopped and opened my eyes. The clearing was unchanged. The downed trees, the fissures, the exposed roots. My breathing had accelerated, and I slowed it. The pen was still in my lap. The notebook was still closed. I had nothing to write.

The watch and a half was still erased. Whatever I had known and seen was behind the static, a wall I could not tear down.

I sat with that for a while. The wind moved through the downed trees, the low sound, the intimate one, air threading through wreckage. Somewhere a woodpecker tested a dead trunk. Three taps. Pause. Three taps.

The ash-smell had been there since I arrived. Now, sitting still, breathing evenly, I smelled it deepen: wood-ash and charred earth. The cooking fire we had built at the center, the night before the poison. The domestic context overlaid on the destructive one.

The smell opened a door.

Wei had said it once, on a morning that smelled like this, like ash, like the aftermath of something that had burned. A cooking fire. The domestic context that Wei inhabited when he was not dying.

"If you don't know where to go, just go."

The memory was short. One sentence. Wei was walking ahead of me. The morning was gray. He did not look back when he said it, because he was stating a principle and wanted no approval. A Wei directive that governed ambiguity: if you don't know, go. If you're uncertain, move. If the destination is unclear, the motion is still valid.

If you don't know where to go, just go.

I opened the notebook. Wrote one line: *Static. Wall. Active. Understanding present but inaccessible.* Everything the meditation had produced.

I stood up and looked at the clearing one more time. A hundred and twenty paces. Three rings. Worse than I had believed, more comprehensive than I had remembered. The thing inside me protected me at any cost, and this was the cost. This was what it looked like from the outside.

I turned away. I had seen what I had done. I had stood at the center of my own destructive radius, and I had not remembered.

Just go.

The return at qi-speed was even faster. The forest blurred, unblurred, resolved into fields, a river, rooftops. The settlement. The narrow room.

You were lying on the bed as I had left you, your notebook open on your chest. Reading. Your own observations, your own handwriting, the investigation continuing in my absence. The room smelled of straw, river damp, and something cooking downstairs.

You looked up and measured my face. Whatever you saw there, you did not ask.

I handed you the pen and opened my notebook to the line I had written.

You read it. Sat up, slowly, ribs, and took the pen. Wrote under my line, in your careful hand: *Confirmed. Active resistance. Not passive decay.*

You held onto the pen, closed the notebook, and turned to the window, where the light was amber and slanting.

"Before nightfall," you said.

Half a watch to spare.
