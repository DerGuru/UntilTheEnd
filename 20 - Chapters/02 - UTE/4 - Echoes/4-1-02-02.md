Branches shook east of the path. A spirit-beast pushed through them, horse-sized or larger, its body swollen with decades of qi. Plates of hardened skin covered its shoulders and spine.

It crossed the brown grass without slowing. The ants had turned here; the bird had fled. Either its territorial drive was stronger, or its qi sheltered it from whatever had turned the smaller creatures.

It charged.

Its weight shook the ground under my feet.

I moved.

I had repeated this movement so often that it took scarcely more attention than breathing. The beast bore down on me. I raised my open hand to the shoulder joint, where the plate was thinnest.

My palm met hardened skin. I sent a single pulse of qi through it.

The plate cracked with the sound of splitting stone. The beast twisted sideways under the blow, sliding past me for three paces. Four. Its claws scored the dirt.

It turned with a snarl.

I had taken two measured steps to reach it. There was time enough to place my hand between the plates of its skull while it was still turning.

Under my palm, its qi-channels converged at the junction that sustained them all. Each channel fed into it.

I pressed. The junction collapsed.

The beast dropped, its snarl cut short. Loose earth shifted under its shoulder. Then it lay still.

It lay on the ground. Large and dead.

I looked at my hand. Blood from the cracked plate smeared my fingers and the gaps between my knuckles. It was darker than human blood, with iridescence where the light caught it.

I wiped it on my thigh, as casually as I might once have wiped away garden soil.

The cloth dragged over my knuckles. I turned back toward the path.

The brown circle moved with me as I passed the beast.

I walked. More forest. More path.

The blood dried.

An abandoned hut stood off the forest path. Whoever had built it had wanted distance from other people: one room, four walls, and a leaking roof, far from the roads.

The door hung from one hinge. A hole in the frame marked where the other had pulled free. The lower edge dragged against the threshold, leaving a gap just wide enough for me to pass through.

I went in.

The doorway gave me somewhere to go.

Dust covered the floor, the single surface that served as a table, and the window frame where a shutter had once hung. Air still moved through the room. Nobody had brought a broom here in months.

The stone fireplace held old ash. Its cold reached me from across the room; damp had settled into the hearth as thoroughly as it had into the walls.

I sat on the floor with my back against the wall. It was close, and leaning needed no further arrangement.

Wei would have fixed the door. Or tried to.

I could almost see him crouched under it. He would have examined the hole, found a piece of wire or a strip of bark, and tried to fasten the door back into place. Broken things bothered him. He rarely waited to be told they were beyond repair.

The door hung where its last occupant had left it. I stared at the gap under the missing hinge.

Wei would have made fire. I didn't.

The cold from the floor reached through my clothes. He would have gathered kindling, probably complaining about how wet it was. Fourteen-year-olds expected considerable cooperation from the world. Then he would have arranged the sticks, coaxed a flame out of them, and sat opposite the hearth until warmth reached his face. Smoke would have drifted through the room. We would have had something ordinary to do with the night.

The fireplace was cold. Nobody made fire.

For a breath or two I waited for the scrape of kindling against stone. I could picture his hands arranging it. The room was as distant as before.

The walls smelled of damp wood. Rot had spread through them since the hearth went cold. I knew that smell from shelters left empty long enough for rain to soak into the timbers.

It brought back a rocky overhang in the mountains. Damp wood had smoked on our fire there when Wei first talked about fear. That night seemed impossibly far from this room, though I had lived it in this same body. My own question came back clearly.

"Are you afraid of me?"

He had denied it. "I'm not."

He should have been.

I wanted his voice to hurt. My fingers pressed into my knees. My breath grew shallow as I waited for the sound of him to reach somewhere I could feel it. Even pain would have given me something to hold on to.

It didn't cut.

The words came back perfectly. They faded while I sat there, leaving my fingers pressed into my knees.

Another smell. Older. Before Wei. Before this century.

A plains-runner, the last of its kind. It had six legs and hollow bones, shaped by a continent that had since changed beyond recognition. I had lived long enough to recognize what its disappearance would mean. I remembered how beautiful it had been.

I had killed it because I was bored. After so long, something unfamiliar could still make me stop. That creature had been unfamiliar enough.

Hollow bones broke differently. The marrow was thin, its scent lighter than I had expected. For three heartbeats I had been satisfied. Then I had stood over its body with nothing more to do.

Dried blood from the spirit-beast still marked my hand. I couldn't have said whether the fight was this morning or yesterday. I had killed it, wiped my fingers, and walked on. Millennia lay between those two bodies. Against the wall I saw how little had changed in what I did afterward. Once I had killed to interrupt the silence. Now I could barely interrupt it even by remembering him.

I should have recognized the pattern sooner. But then, I never did.

Wei would have hated seeing me like this.

I sat with that thought until it, too, receded.

Night settled over the hut. Starlight entered through gaps in the rotting ceiling, drawing thin lines across the floor. I sat against the wall with my eyes open. Even now I remember exactly where the light lay.

I waited for another memory of him. For something sharper, something that would reach me as his voice almost had.

Nothing came.

Silence. The hut. The cold fireplace. The broken door.

The ceiling with its scars of light.
