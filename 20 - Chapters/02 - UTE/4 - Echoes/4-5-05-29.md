We were on the road again. You wrote faster now, with an urgency that had turned the notebook from your project into my lifeline.

You wrote before I spoke, while I spoke, and afterward, filling gaps: my posture, my expression, the behavioral markers my words did not carry. You were building a backup of me, a Yun-on-paper who would exist even if the Yun-in-person forgot.

"The next site should be east. Past the ridge, where the soil changes," you said, at the fire, on a morning that followed a night that I mostly remembered.

"When did I say that?"

You showed me the notebook. Third entry from the top. The date. The time. My words.

I had given a direction, with specifics: past the ridge, where the soil changes. It was the instruction of someone navigating from knowledge she couldn't account for. The investigation instinct was still operational, still producing output.

I did not remember saying that.

This was the pattern: selective erasure. The memories that vanished all connected to the investigation, to the substrate, to the cognitive territory the self-protection targeted.

Other memories survived. Your meditation from last night was intact, loud and irritating, the acoustic assault that constituted your evening procedure. So was the playful argument about ginger.

But the investigation-adjacent memories were all gone.

You were my memory now. Your notebook held the record my mind could no longer maintain, the thoughts I had thought, forgotten, and would think again.

Millennia of self-sufficiency and my memory was being maintained by a man whose evening breathing exercises sounded like a water buffalo with swollen nose.

You adapted.

The notebook entries changed format. Previous entries had been observational. Your perspective. Your analysis. "Subject touched basalt. Withdrew hand." "Subject spoke eleven words." "Subject shows behavioral markers consistent with—"

New entries became instructional: my perspective, my words quoted exactly. You were writing no longer for your own reference but for a reader who would need the content to reconstruct herself.

"Read this every morning," you had written at the top of a new section, in handwriting deliberately larger than your usual miniature. It was readable at a glance.

The section held everything from what we were doing to what we had found and what we concluded.

You had turned your notebook into my morning briefing.

You were also paranoid.

Over the days it showed. You looked behind us, intermittently, undramatically, at the frequency of passive surveillance for a threat you were not discussing.

Your handwriting steadied when you noticed me watching. The shake was there when you were unobserved and gone when you were observed. Control.

Something had happened.

I did not know what. It must have happened inside the memory gap, during my investigation, and it had changed your behavior. You showed fear of something external to me and to the substrate.

"Are you alright?" I asked.

"I'm fine," you said. I detected the lie. It had the same shape as mine. Small, necessary, protective. I let it slide.

We continued. You wrote. I forgot. You reminded. I continued.

You compensated. I failed.

The dependency was uncomfortable. Millennia of self-sufficiency, now conditional on a broken-core researcher with a notebook, a pen, and a fear you wouldn't name.

But the dependency was functional.

And functional was more than I had managed alone.
