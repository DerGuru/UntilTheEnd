The path ran east through thinning forest, worn by hooves, rain, and feet. It descended toward lower ground, where the ridges dwindled into open country.

We had been moving for three days since the departure. Or four. The number was your department, the responsibility your notebook assumed and I had silently ceded, because counting required a care I did not have.

The dynamic had stabilized. We were learning each other's edges in motion, without ever naming them. I moved. You followed. The ten paces held.

You cooked at nightfall. I sat six paces from the fire.

The ginger reached me anyway, carried on smoke and steam. I did not eat. You did not offer.

It was restraint, an arrangement, the respect between someone who had stopped performing and someone who had stopped expecting.

"You're staring again," you said on the second evening.

"I'm evaluating."

"You said that last time."

"I haven't finished my evaluation yet. It is still ongoing."

You looked up from the pot. You processed the quantity of words as much as their content. Twelve. The longest voluntary response since your arrival.

You returned to the pot. You did not smile, or not in the conventional sense. Your face performed a different operation, a compression of features that looked like concentration but sat, quietly, in the same functional category as amusement. The compression happened. You stirred.

"Your technique is wasteful," I said.

I had advanced from evaluation to judgment. From the perspective of someone who had watched meals be made across civilizations, your cooking was wasteful. You used too much water. The ginger was cut too thick. The heat was distributed unevenly because the stone arrangement maximized stability rather than efficiency.

"My technique," you said, with the compression-face, "keeps me alive."

"For now."

You stopped stirring. The compression-face intensified. You studied me across six paces of gradient, over the grass that browned near me. You were recalibrating. The subject had offered criticism. The subject had engaged.

You wrote something. One line. Then you resumed stirring.

We moved through the foothills, through the transition between mountain forest and lowland vegetation, the shift altitude produces. Gradual, unremarkable, the same one I had seen in this region about twelve times over the centuries.

Except this time, it was the pair of us. Two shapes where there had been one and should have been three.

Alone, I had been a point: a location, a source of death with no one keeping a distance from its edge. Together we were a line. We pointed somewhere even if the somewhere was still undetermined.

The path narrowed on the fourth morning. Forest pressed in, denser, older, growth that marked a region humans rarely crossed. The canopy closed. The light dimmed.

You shortened the distance when a bend threatened to hide me from view. The narrowing path brought you closer. Eight paces. Seven.

At seven paces, the grass between us yellowed. The transition zone had compressed. You were inside the outer gradient.

"Give me space," I said.

"The path doesn't let me."

"Then slow down."

"Then I lose you."

The logic was accurate. The narrow path hid me from you at each bend. To keep me in sight, you had to choose between slowing us both and coming too close.

You did not back up. You adjusted, angling to the side to find the extra handspan at the path's margin. Seven and a half paces. The grass: pale yellow. Stressed but surviving.

Your left knee popped when you shifted. The broken core's cost: joints wearing faster than they should, the compensation visible as you favored one side.

I let my pace bleed a fraction slower, enough that you didn't have to steal distance to keep me in sight.

We emerged from the dense growth into a clearing, the kind ridgelines produce: a break in the canopy, a view.

The lowlands spread below. Rolling. Green. A patchwork of cultivated land and wild land, stitched together by rivers I couldn't see yet but smelled in the damp air.

Days of travel, a month at the outside. It was the first hint of a direction, and it felt like returning.

"If you're coming," I said, "keep up."

The sentence went further than telling you to give me space. It assumed you would continue with me, however casually I phrased it. Keep up. Or fall behind. The verb was yours.

"Where are we going?" you asked.

I moved.

You followed.

The clearing shrank behind us. The path descended into the lowlands, into terrain that would bring encounters, distances, and the time the next stretch of existence required.

We left the mountain behind: the days of standing, the long silence, the slow, measurable thaw.

The air carried wet earth, grass, the damp of rivers, and the faint smell that meant other people. Somewhere ahead. Not yet.
