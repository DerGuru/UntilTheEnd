You wrote as the light changed over the ridge. Our conversation had ended with my question unspoken. You understood that I needed time before I could explain it.

You were used to waiting for explanations. When something resisted you, you left space and came back to it. Watching your pen, I realized how much of that patience I had begun to borrow.

By evening you had built a fire. Ginger and rice went into the scratched pot on its usual stones. Your hands knew the work.

You filled two bowls. You set mine at the usual distance, looked at the green grass between us, and moved the bowl closer, to two paces. Then you watched the patch where you had set it.

We ate quietly. When you finished, you set down your bowl, picked it up, and put it down again. Your hands were still moving when you spoke.

"I would like," you said, and paused. "I would like to reconstruct the event. If you are willing."

The notebook lay open before you. Your fingers held the pen ready, though they weren't steady.

"Why a pen?" I asked.

Your head came up. The question had interrupted the procedure. "What?"

"A pen. Not a brush."

You glanced at the pen in your hand as if noticing it for the first time. "Smaller script. More per page. A brush requires ink preparation, drying time, and horizontal surfaces." You turned the pen between your fingers. "A pen requires a pen."

I had expected a practical answer. Asking was the surprise. The last genuine question I had put to you concerned your shattered core: how it felt, when it had broken. That had been about pain. Now I could take an interest in a small thing you held while we ate.

"From the ridge," you continued, "I saw you step forward. No running start, no push. You walked off the edge as if you were simply taking one step farther."

"Yes."

"You fell. The first heartbeats—" You consulted your notes. "The first heartbeats were slow. Relative to what I expected. You were close to the wall. I could see your robes. The wind caught them."

"Before I counted two," I said. "That was when the wind began."

You wrote. "At what point did the robes flatten against the body?"

I considered. The memory was precise: the wind pressing the fabric tight, clinging like wet cloth. "Four heartbeats. Perhaps late in the third."

"Consistent," you said, settling the pen against the page. "After about fifteen heartbeats I could no longer make out much detail. You were already small. A dark shape against gray stone. Falling."

The fire cracked. A log shifted.

"I counted," you said. "Twelve heartbeats to somewhere near the midpoint. After that I could no longer judge how fast you were moving past the wall."

"I counted the first nine," I said. "Then I stopped counting."

You looked up from the notebook. "Why?"

"Because the fall stopped changing. The same wind. The same sound. Counting told me nothing more."

You began to write, then stopped. "What did you do instead?"

"I turned inward."

Wood burned between us while you waited, pen hovering above the open notebook, giving me time to find the words.

"I felt something," I said. "During the fall. A pressure." I put my hand flat against my chest, over the place beneath the skin. "Here. Like a hand. From the inside."

You wrote. "The core?"

"Near the core. Around it. But not—" I shook my head. "Not qi. The qi was still circulating as usual. This was something else. Something underneath."

"Underneath the qi?"

"Underneath everything."

You looked up. The pen hovered. "What was it?"

I kept my hand against my chest while I considered the answer. I knew how little it would tell you.

"Something else."

The fire lit your face from below. You watched my hand resting against my chest, your pen still.

"Something below the qi," I said. "Below the channels. Below everything that your cultivation theory describes. A substrate. Something at the foundation."

You wrote the word, then underlined it. "Substrate. That is an unusual term for—"

"I know what it means. The thing beneath. The thing everything else rests on." I looked at the fire. "It is the closest word I have."

You let the word stand. Your pen moved again as I tried to say what I meant.

"The substrate stirred." I paused, remembering the faint movement. "Like something shifting before waking."

"How far had you fallen?"

"I don't know. Between nine and fifteen heartbeats, closer to fifteen. The ground should have been close by then. Instead there was this."

You went back over your notes. "Then you had fallen five or six hundred paces. Perhaps two hundred and fifty remained. At the pace you were moving, another five heartbeats or so."

"Yes."

"It stirred when you were only a few heartbeats from the ground."

"Warmth," I said. "From below the core. It expanded outward through the channels. Then through other paths. Older ones." I held my palms toward the fire. "It used what was there, as water uses a streambed. I had never felt those paths so clearly."

"When did you slow?"

"The warmth became pressure. A hand pressing on my chest from inside. It grew stronger. Then the wind changed. Less pressure against my face. The roar dropped. When I opened my eyes, I was moving much more slowly."

You tapped the pen against the page. "Air qi. Did you feel anything around you? Strong wind cultivators can gather air beneath themselves to break a fall. I've seen it done."

"No. There was no qi outside. No pull from the air. No compression beneath me. The force came from inside. From the chest outward." I paused. "The air did nothing. The air was just air."

You wrote a line, crossed it out, and tried again.

"From the ridge," you said, "I expected you to reach the base after about twenty heartbeats. Seven beats later, you were still descending. I could see that. But I couldn't tell exactly when you slowed. From so far away, it was difficult to distinguish from—" You swallowed. "From the alternative."

"From impact."

"I watched for movement," you said. Your voice was level, but I heard what it cost you. "At the base. For a very long time, I watched for movement."

"A thousand and twenty heartbeats," I said.

"What?"

"I counted that many at the base. Shivering."

The pen had stopped.

"The landing," I said. "I was already slower well above the ground. Nearer the rock, the warmth in my chest grew intense. Then I drifted down like a leaf and hung still just above the stone. Two or three heartbeats." I paused. "After that I descended gently until my feet touched stone."

You didn't write.

"Then cold came," I said. "The warmth withdrew. Ice in my joints. My fingers. As though whatever caught me had taken the heat with it. I shivered through all those heartbeats."

The fire burned low between us. You set the pen down.

"The substrate," you said. "It acted autonomously."

"Yes."

"Without your direction."

"Without my knowledge. Without my direction."

"The warmth, the pressure, and the slowing came together. Something held your body against the fall. From within."

"Yes."

You were quiet for a while. The fire cracked, its smoke rising straight through the still air.

"I have documented seventeen anomalous events since the beginning of this study," you said. "I stayed composed for each one. Otherwise the record would have been unreliable." You turned a stick in your hands. "This is different."

"Different how?"

"The other events were—" You searched. "Contained. A toxicity radius. An organism avoiding proximity." You broke the stick. The snap was loud in the quiet. You looked at the two halves. "The other events suggested anomaly," you said. "This suggests something with judgment."

I said nothing. You had arrived at the same conclusion I had reached at the bottom.

"Something decided," you said. Quietly. To the fire.

"Yes."

"Something that is not you."

"I don't know. It lives where I live. It uses what I use. But it decides without asking."

You placed the stick halves in the fire to watch them catch.

"I didn't die," I said.

"You didn't know that."

You were right. Standing at the rim, I had thought only of finding out. I watched the broken stick burn.

"No," I said. "I didn't."

You set the pen beside the open notebook and left both there. "Then why did you do it?" you said.

I paused. You wanted an answer.

"Because I am old," I said. "Very old. Too old."

You waited. For the rest of the sentence. For the elaboration.

There was none. The reason was complete.

Unsteady light moved over your face as you watched me across the fire. For once you had forgotten the open notebook beside your knee.

"Don't do it again," you said.

I looked at the pen you had laid aside. I could not promise for the woman I would be in a thousand years. Even sitting across from you, with the broken stick burning between us, I could not make that promise.

The silence was the only honest response.
