I made a light with qi to carry on searching after dark. Sealed cylinders lay in wall recesses, under collapsed shelving, and in low corners where they had rolled. I checked each one before breaking its wax. Then I unrolled the contents far enough to read a few lines. Most held sect records, administrative trivia preserved with more care than it deserved. I set those aside and moved on.

By the time the dark outside had shifted from black to the deep gray that precedes dawn, I'd opened more than a hundred cylinders and found two more scrolls that mattered.

The second was about fifteen hundred years old. It concerned a woman; the gendered pronouns were clear in a grammatical structure that predated the gender-neutral reforms of the Middle Period.

The margins were dense with dates, locations, weather conditions, and notes on the subject's physical state before each observation. Further notes described her afterward. The handwriting was neat even where the writer had almost run out of room.

*I tried to die,* she wrote. *My body refused. As if it knew something I did not.*

Different words. Same meaning. The coincidence was too precise to be coincidence.

The third was older again, about four thousand years. Its script was so archaic that I had to read the characters several times, following changes in their meaning across the intervening eras. I couldn't determine the cultivator's gender. The account documented qi directed by will, anomalies in the surrounding world, and continued life despite a wish to die.

*The world around me obeys,* this one wrote. *I have never learned this. I have never asked for it. It obeys as if I am the law and the world merely follows. I do not know what I have done to deserve subjection so total that even the earth bends.*

Three cultivators. Three epochs. More than two thousand years of distance between the first and the last.

I laid the scrolls side by side on the cleared floor of the library. Parallel beams of morning light crossed the paper. You sat opposite with your notebook open. Each time I moved from one scroll to another, you leaned forward to see which passages I was comparing.

Together, the three accounts revealed something I'd missed while reading them separately. All used the same arrangement: an observer's account surrounding passages in the cultivator's own words. Each cultivator had someone at their side, watching closely enough to record what happened before a statement as well as afterward.

"Parallels?" you asked.

"Different language. Identical experience. Three people across three millennia, all describing the same thing. Their qi responds to will, not training. Their bodies sustain themselves against their wishes. And the world around them..." I paused with a finger on the word *obeys*. I couldn't bring myself to use it. "Changes."

"Changing what is real."

"Adjusting it. Gently enough that the changes look as though they've always been there."

You wrote that down. I saw you underline *always been there*.

"And all three are dead."

"All three stopped documenting. The scrolls end, just like the inscriptions at the ruins. Mid-sentence. Mid-thought. There's no conclusion, farewell, or sign of violence. They simply stop."

"Forgetting."

I looked at you.

"That's your theory." You glanced at the earlier notes. "From the cave inscriptions. You said they forgot."

"Yes."

"Something made them forget what they were investigating. Erased the thought. The question. The connection."

"Yes."

"And you think whatever did that protects you too. It keeps you alive. It erases anyone who gets too close to understanding it."

I said nothing.

"Because if they understood it," you continued quietly, "they might try to stop it. Whatever it is could be protecting itself. It could make them forget instead of killing them."

You had reached the conclusion I'd been avoiding. I looked down at the scrolls between us. You had needed only the records to follow the connection; you hadn't needed to feel the qi under those ruins. Either the evidence was becoming clearer, or I was doing a poorer job of concealing what I saw in it.

"Eat something," you said.

There was fish. You'd caught it somewhere, probably in the nearby stream while I'd been occupied with the scrolls. You'd cleaned it, gutted it, and cooked it over the fire I still hadn't seen. The smell was good. You had managed all that while I was still reading the same three accounts.

I hadn't fished in a long time. Perhaps I never had. The smell brought up an uncertain memory of water, a long wait, and a line held between fingers. I couldn't place the shore. I might have been remembering someone I'd watched.

I took the fish and ate. Its flesh was firm, seasoned with something peppery you must have found at the stream bank. I worked around the small bones, setting each one at the edge of the plate. My eyes returned to the scrolls.

"So I'm not the only one," I said between bites.

You looked up from your notebook. "No."

"Others had this. The... whatever it is that keeps me alive. This qi obedience. This reality... adjustment."

"And they're all dead. Or they would still be present."

"Yes."

"Which means whatever keeps you alive didn't keep them alive."

"Or it did. For a while. And then stopped."

"Why would it stop?"

I set down the fish. That was the question I'd been turning over since the first scroll. I looked at the passage about forty days of meditation. Whatever had sustained that cultivator through his attempt to die had eventually let him end. I had no account of what changed.

"I don't know," I said.

You picked up your chopsticks, set them down, and picked them up again. I'd seen you do that before. Each time, another question followed.

"Your body does it," you said. "Not your technique. Not your training. Your body sustains itself. Against your will."

"Yes."

"Then it's not external. It's you."

"It's something in me. That's not the same as being me."

"Isn't it?"

I looked at you across the fish bones. Centuries of evidence lay open on the dusty floor between us.

"No," I said. "Whatever refuses to let me die also changes things around me. Perhaps it erased those researchers. None of us chose it. It's something done to me. Through me. As if I'm the instrument, not the musician."

"And the others were instruments too."

"Apparently."

"Instruments that broke."

The fire outside crackled. A bird sang, the right species for this region at this season. Even the time of day was right for its call. I noted all of that while you watched me.

"Maybe you're not different from them." You spoke carefully, leaving a pause before continuing. "Maybe you're just... newer. Maybe whatever wears out instruments hasn't worn you out yet."

I finished the fish, set the plate down, and wiped my hands on my robe.

"Or maybe," I said, "I'm the one who broke all the others."

You stopped writing.

I stood, went to the shelf, and pulled down the next cylinder.

I spent the afternoon comparing the three scrolls with the seven sites. I checked the dates first, then the locations. You turned pages in your notebook while I traced passages on the floor. Across thousands of years, the accounts described the same symptoms, followed the same course, and stopped just as abruptly.

Ten total. Seven from the ruins. Three from the scrolls. Ten cultivators across millennia. Ten beginnings. Ten erasures.

I was still here, reading their words. Chalk grated between my teeth. Incense lingered in the air, and the metal next to my knee was cold. Had they felt that cold too, in the places they'd searched?

A shelf settled deeper into its collapse. Wood groaned. Dust sifted down.

I waited for the dust to settle before reaching for the scrolls. Ten others had stopped. I was alive, still able to search, although I understood no more than they had.

I began to suspect the same thing had sustained me and stopped them. Perhaps I was still useful to it. I disliked the thought enough to roll the paper more tightly than I should.

I eased my grip before the paper creased, then stacked the scrolls neatly. I'd handled ancient documents for longer than most libraries had existed. I could at least put these away properly. I said nothing of my suspicions.

You wrote in your notebook. The fire died to embers.

The light in the ruin shifted from gold to gray and finally to the blue that precedes total dark.
