We walked back to camp in silence. I shuffled along next to you in the outer robe, too large, too warm in some places and not warm enough in others, the world pressing against me from every direction with its new, exhausting detail.

The same trail felt different. The same stones, the same switchbacks, and the same pines leaning at the same angles as this morning. But I was walking it in a different body. My soles registered every pebble, every root, and every transition from packed earth to loose scree. A sharp stone caught the arch of my left foot and I flinched, actually flinched, pulling away from ground I would scarcely have noticed before.

The air had weight. It entered my lungs cool and dry, expanding the tissue, pressing against the inside of my ribs. Each breath carried the taste of the mountain: pine resin, stone dust, and the sweetness of something flowering uphill.

It was exhilarating. It was exhausting.

A spider had built a web between two branches at head height. I stopped. The silk caught the light in individual threads, each carrying a bead of dew that split the sun into visible colors. The spider sat motionless at the center, legs folded.

I'd walked past ten thousand webs in my life. This was the first one I'd experienced.

You waited without asking why I'd stopped, carrying both packs, giving me time to look at a spider. For a while, there was something besides your guilt to hold us there.

We walked on. The pine needles underfoot were soft, each yielding with its own small give, a surface made of separate things. The bark was rough when I brushed against a tree, textured with the ridged architecture of years. An ant climbed one of the ridges, crossing the trunk one obstacle at a time, turning around a splinter, finding purchase above it. We had been navigating the mountain in much the same fashion.

We reached camp. The fire had gone out, so you knelt and rebuilt it. The wood caught. The warmth spread. I sat close enough for the heat to reach my face and hands, and the cold in my bones eased a notch.

The fire was different too. Until now warmth had been a condition my body maintained from inside. This heat was directional. The side of my face toward the flames was warm. The side away from them was cold. The boundary ran down the center of my body like a line drawn by the fire's reach, precise enough to trace along my skin.

I held my hands out. The heat reached my palms first, then the backs of my fingers, then the wrists. The progression had always been real. I just hadn't noticed before.

A bird landed on the pack you'd set down, pecked at the strap, and left without finding anything edible. Its weight had been on the pack for three heartbeats. Claws scratched the fabric. A beak tapped the buckle with a small percussive click. Wings whispered as it launched. Three heartbeats of bird, rendered in full detail.

You sat across from me with your notebook open. Your fast, urgent handwriting at the clearing had softened into something steadier. You wrote, paused, glanced up at me, and returned to the page.

"I had to," you said. Not to me. To the page. To yourself. To whatever version of the morning you were trying to fix in ink before it became something you could live with.

"I know," I said.

You looked up. The words had landed wrong. Or right. My "I know" implied understanding of an event I couldn't remember, knowledge I shouldn't have had.

"Do you—"

"No. I don't know what you did." I looked at my hands. At the robe. At the camp, exactly as it had been this morning, as if nothing had changed, as if the world hadn't cracked open and sealed itself shut while I wasn't watching. "But I know you did something. And I know it was necessary. And I think it's eating you."

You said nothing. Your pen stopped.

"And I also know that it worked. At least in part," I said. "Because I can still feel it."

You set the notebook down between us. Open. The page facing up, the fresh ink still damp. An offering, or a confession, or both.

I didn't read it. The content was dangerous. What was safe was the feeling, and the feeling didn't require reading.

Your guilt would stay a while, settling into you like sediment, part of what you carried forward. But now you let your gaze rest on mine longer. Cautious, fragile hope showed in it, a terrible thing done and the result you'd wanted beginning to appear. At least in part.

The first crack that might lead somewhere.

We sat. The fire burned. The morning was still early and everything was precisely, painfully, overwhelmingly real.
