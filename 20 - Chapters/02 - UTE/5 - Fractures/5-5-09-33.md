For two days the trail ran east and we followed it. The small wrongnesses accumulated. A root caught my foot where no root should have been. A branch gave way under your hand when you reached for balance. A ledge crumbled at the exact point where we'd planned to rest. We had to check each foothold, each place we might set down a pack.

The flask leaked. I emptied it, dried it, and refilled it. It leaked again. Same seam. Same thin film of moisture. I checked the stitching. It was intact, tight, and without a visible flaw. I found no cause. It joined everything else going wrong, one small thing at a time, in the steady rhythm of something being repaid.

The first evening, you sat cross-legged by the fire with the flask in your lap, a needle between your teeth and heavy thread from your repair kit laid out on a flat stone. You turned the flask over, running your thumb along the seam. You held it up to the firelight and squinted with the focused displeasure of a man who could not find what he was looking for.

"The stitching is fine," I said.

"The stitching is fine," you agreed, around the needle.

You re-stitched it anyway and tied it off. You filled the flask from the stream and set it upright on the stone.

We both watched it.

For a hundred and twenty heartbeats, the flask sat there, sealed, re-stitched, repaired. Then the thin film appeared. Same seam. Same slow weep through the leather.

"I'll try wax tomorrow," you said.

I doubted wax would matter. The leak was beyond wax, thread, or competence. But saying "I'll try wax tomorrow" was easier than admitting we had run out of things to try. Easier was what we had, with the water still seeping down the leather.

On the second evening, you built the fire. You were good at fires. You had built a hundred in front of me and never failed. You struck the steel. Sparks landed on tinder. And the tinder didn't catch.

You blew gently, then reconfigured the kindling. Drier pieces, smaller pieces, positioned to catch the spark from a better angle. You struck again.

Nothing.

Your jaw tightened. You handled frustration as you handled everything, by acknowledging it, categorizing it, and proceeding with the methodical patience of a man who didn't let obstacles become excuses.

Third try. New tinder. Fresh sparks. Careful breath.

Nothing.

Fourth try. You rearranged everything, stripped the bark thinner, and built a nest of the driest fibers you could find. Fibers that should have caught from a hard look, let alone a steel spark. You struck. The sparks landed true. The fibers glowed for half a heartbeat, a faint orange thread of heat, then went out.

I let you go on until you sat back. You looked at the cold tinder, at your hands, and at the fire steel as if it had personally betrayed you.

Then you turned to me and waited.

"Let me," I said.

You moved. I knelt where you'd been kneeling. The tinder nest was good; it should have caught three tries ago. I reached for the smallest thread of qi I could manage, the barest whisper of intent, and fed it into the kindling. A thread of heat, barely enough to kindle a glow where the sparks had failed.

The fire caught, a hesitant flicker spreading quickly through the dry nest. All that careful preparation worked with my heat.

You watched it burn. Your face was as neutral as faces get when the person behind them is working hard to keep them that way.

The flames grew from that first flicker into a steady burn that would last. The wood crackled. The warmth spread. Normal. Ordinary. The most basic human technology: controlled combustion, heat, light, the domestication of a force older than every other tool.

"It's happening to you too," I said.

You looked up from the fire. Orange light shifted across your pupils as the kindling burned.

"The stone yesterday. The branch today. The fire."

"I noticed."

"I'm sorry."

You considered the apology.

"Don't apologize for things you can't control," you said. "Save the apologies for things you choose."

You cooked. I stared into the fire and thought about the mountain.

I'd sat long enough with the missing three hundred paces of geography. The slopes above that impossible grass were still there. Still standing. Still being a mountain, as mountains are supposed to be.

The remaining rock stood solid above the gap, without a crack or a slide of scree. It had simply continued. I looked for something to learn from that.

There was a lesson there. Probably. But I wasn't in the mood for lessons.

You handed me a bowl. Rice with dried mushrooms, a small variation. You introduced those now and then, because you understood that monotony was its own form of erosion. The chewy mushrooms tasted of earth, smoke, and a deep savory richness.

I ate mechanically. Fuel. Maintenance.

You ate too. The scab on your injured finger was dark, small, and completely ordinary, a wound a person collects, heals from, and forgets. You hadn't forgotten it. Your eyes went to it again and again, checking that the scab was real, that the world was playing by its normal rules.

"I could fix that," I said.

You glanced from me to your finger.

"No."

"It would take a heartbeat."

"I know." You turned the scab toward the firelight and studied the small dark ridge of dried blood as if it held information worth extracting. "I'd rather keep it."

I waited.

"It reminds me," you said, "I can be hurt. I can heal. The scab is proof of both." You flexed the finger. The scab stretched but held. "I'd like to keep the proof."

As if your broken core wasn't already a monument to the fact that you could be hurt. I thought it instantly and said nothing. You'd made your point. The scab would stay.

After dinner, we would sit for your daily round of channel repair. For now, the fire burned. Darkness settled around us at altitude, where the sky seems close and the stars are sharp. Even by the fire I felt small and exposed.

I should have been thinking about what I was. That question had been driving everything: the investigations, the ruins, the scrolls, and the cultivation techniques that defied categorization. What am I? A question that assumed an answer existed, that it was knowable, and that knowing would help.

But sitting there, staring into the fire, eating rice, with the headache pulsing and a section of mountain missing because of me, the question changed.

What am I? was still there.

Next to it came another question: how much can I destroy?

I measured the range of what I could do as coldly as a person measuring a weapon's reach. I had split trees, made weather, and locked reality. I had erased a mountain flank so completely that only our memories placed it there.

Each event was larger than the last. The trajectory was clear: upward. Where it ended was not a philosophical question. It was a geographical one.

How many mountains. How many valleys. How many villages with red circles on a sect map.

Under that question came another, quieter, more personal, more terrifying in its simplicity.

Can I stop?

Whether I wanted to stop had become academic. I needed to know if I could. Did whatever I was becoming have a limit? Would I reach a point where the escalation ended and the power stabilized, where the world stopped being a thing I accidentally altered?

I didn't know.

Not now. The thought came and I let it come. It settled into the growing archive of things I wasn't ready to examine, the shelf for questions without answers.

"I'm eating," I said, to nobody. To you, who were there. To the mountain, which was partially there.

Your mouth did the thin thing, almost a smile. You could still recognize a joke, however far off laughing at one had gotten.

"You're eating," you confirmed.

"Yes. I am eating."

"Good."

I finished the rice and set down the bowl. The fire spat a spark that landed on the ground and died.

Tomorrow we would walk. East, because every trail turned that way and I'd stopped arguing. We would walk as the small wrongnesses accumulated like rain in a basin, rising toward a level I couldn't see yet.

But for now, right now, I had eaten. I was warm. The fire was lit, even if it had taken multiple tries and some qi. The stars were out, unchanged by what I'd done to a mountain or what a mountain had done to me.

I could look at them without having to explain myself. A useful quality in stars.

My head hurt.
