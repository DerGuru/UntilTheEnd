I woke to the sound of your controlled, deliberate breathing. You lay in your bedroll with your eyes open, waiting for dawn to make it worth getting up.

You had been awake for a while, lying in the dark, breathing carefully, thinking whatever thoughts a mortal thinks after a night three paces from something that can rewrite reality.

I didn't announce my waking. I lay still as the sky turned from black to the deep blue that precedes dawn. I liked that color between definitions. For a while I could lie there without deciding whether the night was over.

The world looked wrong.

A subtle wrongness, as in dreams: furniture in the right place, light falling from the wrong side. You know, without being able to explain, that you're not where you should be.

Colors were too much. The pre-dawn blue was too blue. The gray of the ashes in the fire pit was too precisely gray. Against the brightening sky, every pine needle stood out in the same vivid green.

Shadows were wrong too, though their positions were correct. I checked, tracking the angle of the emerging light against the objects that cast them. But the shadows moved strangely. When I shifted my gaze, a shadow adjusted a fraction of a heartbeat after the light changed, as if following a copy of it.

The world was self-correcting. Still absorbing what I'd done to it. Still fitting itself back into the shape of normal reality after being held in the shape of mine.

I sat up. The headache had been continuous since the field, a hot wire behind my eyes that pulsed with my heartbeat. I pressed my temples, breathed, and stood up.

You were ahead of me. Already packed, while I was contemplating the wrongness of the world around us. Already standing at the edge of our campsite with something in your hand that caught the early light in a thin metallic gleam.

A knife. The small utility blade you carried for cutting food, for trimming rope, for the mundane tasks a mortal performs with tools because he has no qi to do without them.

You were studying your left hand, specifically the index finger. Your right hand held the knife against its pad, the edge pressing just hard enough to dimple the skin without breaking it.

I didn't speak.

You pressed. A small cut, precise and controlled, the deliberate incision of someone conducting an experiment. Blood appeared immediately, a thin red line, bright against the pale skin of your finger.

You stared at the blood and waited for it to reverse, for the skin to rebuild itself under it. The cut went on bleeding. Normal. Expected. The ordinary response of skin meeting a blade.

You let out a long, shaking breath. Your shoulders dropped and your jaw unclenched, but your hands still trembled.

You put the knife away, found the cloth you used for cleaning utensils, and wrapped your finger. The blood soaked through the fabric in a small, spreading stain. It darkened as it dried, looking exactly like what it was.

"It stays," you said, nodded, and tucked the wrapped finger against your palm. "Good."

We ate in the colors-too-bright morning. Rice, cold from last night, eaten with fingers because neither of us had bothered to heat water to clean the bowls or perform any of the small rituals of preparation that made meals feel like meals instead of fuel. You ate with your right hand. Your left hand, the injured finger, rested on your knee.

The cut would scar, barely. It was small, shallow, a wound that heals in a few days and leaves a thin white line that fades until it is invisible to everyone who isn't looking for it. But you would look for it. You would look at that scar as soldiers look at theirs, with the recognition of someone who survived something and needs the proof.

"The shadows are wrong," I said.

You studied the ground, then a tree and the angular shadow it cast across the brightening earth.

"Delayed," you said. You'd noticed. Of course you'd noticed. "As if they're being generated rather than cast."

Assessment. The scholar's reflex, converting observation into analysis, filing the impossible under *documented* and moving on.

"Will it stop?"

"I don't know."

The world continued adjusting around us, small corrections, tiny shifts, a settling after exertion. The colors were still too vivid, but less so than half a watch ago. The shadows were still delayed, but the delay was shortening. By afternoon, perhaps, the world would look normal. By tomorrow, maybe it would be normal. It was settling as a lake does after a stone, ripple by ripple, the disturbance spreading to the edges and dissipating. Except I was still in the lake, a stone still moving, sending out fresh ripples before the old ones could fade.

I packed and we started walking.

The trail was there, still going east, pulled by whatever internal compass had decided that isolation was the appropriate direction. I'd stopped fighting it. Forcing south meant wrestling with terrain that rearranged itself against my passage, burning energy on a war with geography. I could disagree with the direction and still refuse another day of that exhaustion.

So east we went. Into the mountains. Away from people who might be standing next to trees when I got angry. We walked in a loaded silence, too much to say and no words adequate to say it.

I began to describe what the field had felt like from the inside.

"It was like holding—" I stopped. Holding wasn't right. Holding implies hands, something outside yourself that you grip. "More like being— No. Like becoming the—" Wrong again. The field hadn't been something I'd become. It had been something I'd done, except done implies action and this had been reflexive, automatic, as unconscious as blinking. "The weight," I said. "There was weight. The pressure of keeping everything—"

I stopped, then started again.

"Imagine you're—" No. There was no analogy. Every comparison I reached for collapsed under the distance between the experience and the language available to describe it.

"The world was—" I pressed my palms against my temples. "It was like—"

Silence.

"You don't need to explain it."

"I want to."

"I know. But wanting to explain and being able to explain are different things. And forcing the explanation might—" You glanced at the nearest tree, quick, the new reflex of a man who had learned to check whether the trees near you were intact.

"Might break something. Might cost something. Might translate the frustration of inadequate language into the force of splitting wood."

I shut my mouth.

By midday, the colors had settled. The shadows were tracking correctly. The disturbance seemed to have passed, leaving the earth as quiet as it becomes after an earthquake. Whatever damage was left lay under that ordinary surface.

But it didn't feel right. The wind touched my skin and I flinched at its closeness. The air itself was nearer. The ground under my boots transmitted vibrations it shouldn't have. Your footsteps, yes, but also deeper things. The slow grinding of rock against rock somewhere below. The movement of water in soil. The pulse of roots drawing moisture upward through channels too fine to see. Movement ran through the whole mountain.

A small brown sparrow crossed our path, its heartbeat fluttering against my awareness like a fingertip tapping glass. It landed on a branch six paces away. The branch bent under its weight, and the tiny impact traveled into the tree.

I stopped walking.

"What?" you said.

"Nothing." I started walking again. But it wasn't nothing. The boundary between me and the world had thinned. Before the field, I could let my attention retreat to my own skin. Now everything nearby registered: texture, weight, temperature, and movement. The world pressed against me from every direction, feeding me information I hadn't asked for and couldn't turn off. The relationship between what I was and what the world was had shifted. So had the unspoken contract that governed which of my emotions became force and which stayed private.

The contract had been rewritten, and the terms felt worse.
