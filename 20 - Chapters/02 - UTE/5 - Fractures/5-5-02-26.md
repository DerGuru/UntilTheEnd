Your breath showed in the morning cold as we hurried along the trail. We left prints, broken stems, and all the usual evidence of passage, but they had an easier means of finding us. Every emotion that broke through the wall I kept trying to build announced itself in trees, weather, and the trembling of stone. Their talismans, the resonance collectors tuned to my qi signature, caught every burst. Each one gave them a bearing. I'd been handing them a map all morning.

We'd been moving fast. Faster than comfortable, which for me meant a pace you set your jaw against and held out of sheer refusal to be left behind, even when being left behind was the obvious tactical choice. You didn't complain. You didn't suggest slowing down. You walked, your pack tightened to reduce movement, your notebook secured in the waterproof case that had become as much a part of your kit as the water flask.

The sensible thing would have been to separate. I was the target. I was the one they were tracking. Without me, you were a mortal scholar on a mountain trail. Invisible, maybe not irrelevant, but safe. Or at least safer. With me, you were standing next to the epicenter of something that had already destroyed a hundred and eighty years of tree growth in a moment of poorly managed irritation.

You knew this. I knew you knew this. Neither of us mentioned it.

We crossed the river at a ford that had shrunk since the last mapped survey, either seasonal variation or one more rearrangement around me. I'd stopped being able to tell the difference. The water was cold. The smooth stones underfoot were slick with the algae that grows where water runs slow and sunlight reaches bottom. I crossed without trouble. You crossed without trouble. The world did not react to the crossing.

Small mercies. The small mercies were the ones that kept adding up.

The sect cultivators appeared on the far bank before I'd finished drying our clothes.

Fifteen of them, equipped for more than the cautious observation I'd interrupted with the tree-splitting. Gray robes, yes, the same anonymous fabric that the Pencil's delegation had worn, but these wore them differently. Their belts were tight, their sleeves tied back. Talismans rested against their forearms, a finger's reach from activation. They could trigger them on reflex, after years of practice that had made defense instinct.

They stood in a crescent, open side facing us, its geometry saying *we are not surrounding you* with the military precision of people who could surround us in three heartbeats if the conversation went wrong.

The one in front was a woman. Compact. Fieldwork had weathered her with the lines that come from years of watching, evaluating, and deciding who is a threat. Alertness had done more than the sun. She was maybe Foundation Establishment. Solid, competent cultivation, built on discipline rather than talent.

She held nothing. Her hands were at her sides.

"Yun," she said, with the flat ease of someone briefed extensively enough to need no notes. "We've been asked to make contact."

You stood next to me, tense in the rigid stillness of a mortal body that wanted to run and was choosing not to. Your breathing was too controlled, your hands too still. You were performing calm with the desperate concentration of a man who knew any sudden movement could tip the balance.

"You're the Pencil's people," I said.

A blink. The nickname hadn't traveled to her level. "Inspector Lian Zhuo has requested a formal meeting. These mountains are not safe for—"

"You," I interrupted. "They're not safe for you. Because of me."

The woman paused. Her prepared speech had been interrupted, and her training kept it from derailing her. "We're aware of the anomalies. The sect's position is that—"

"You don't want to fight."

"No."

The single word hung in the air. Behind her, fourteen cultivators maintained their crescent with the stillness of people who really, genuinely, with complete sincerity, did not want to fight.

I should have found that reassuring. Instead, it made the fist in my chest clench tighter, because people who don't want to fight are people who have evaluated the fight and determined they would lose. People who knew they would lose and came anyway are people who want something badly enough to risk losing.

"What do you want then?" you asked.

You shouldn't have spoken. You were mortal. Unarmed. Irrelevant by every metric cultivation society used to measure relevance. Speaking here was like a house cat addressing a kennel of war dogs: so categorically outside the power dynamic that the appropriate response was no response at all.

The woman studied you properly this time, taking in capability, threat level, and purpose in a single glance before her eyes came back to me.

"We want to understand," she said to me.

"The sect has recorded seventeen disturbances in this region over the past six days," she continued. "Weather confined to small areas. Tremors. Trees breaking without anything touching them. And three instances of what our instruments suggest might be—" She hesitated.

"As what?"

"Changes reaching backward. Things that appear to have always been as they are now, despite evidence that they weren't." She delivered this with the detachment of someone reading a report, but her eyes were wrong. They had the brightness of someone afraid for a long time. Professionalism, discipline, and duty held her there.

I understood then. Every moment they spent here gave them something to take home, even if I refused them. They were harvesting information with every glance.

Observation, understanding. I was the most significant cultivation anomaly any of them would meet in their lifetimes, and the Nine Rivers Sect, the Pencil's sect, had decided that understanding me was worth more than confronting me.

I was research.

"No," I said.

The woman blinked. "We haven't—"

"No, I won't submit to examination. No, I won't demonstrate. No, I won't let your people take measurements." It was building, the thing inside me that reacted to pressure, that turned emotion into force. "And no, I don't know what's happening to me."

That last part was the truth, and it cost something to say. I rubbed my temples. The headache had been there since morning, the low persistent ache behind my eyes that tightened when I tried to think about things that had been taken.

"We're not asking for—"

The ground trembled.

A small tremor, barely enough to rattle the pebbles between us. Anyone who had seen the trees would have needed little more warning.

The crescent didn't break. It shifted. Three cultivators on the left side took a half-step back. Two on the right adjusted their footing. The woman in front didn't move. Her composure was better than theirs. Or her fear was controlled differently. Or she had simply decided that if death came it would find her in proper formation.

Your hand was on my arm.

I didn't remember when you put it there. Your grip was light. Mortal-light, the pressure of fingers that couldn't compete with qi-enhanced anything. It held.

The trembling stopped.

"We'll leave," the woman said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "But the Inspector has asked me to relay a message."

She reached into her robes slowly, deliberately, with the careful movements of someone who understood that reaching into clothing near a person who made earthquakes was a gesture that required advance notice. She produced a folded sheet of plain paper. Simple, practical, something scholars used and bureaucrats filed. Nobody in the cultivation world considered it impressive.

She set it on a rock between us.

"We don't want to fight, Yun," she said again. The repetition was deliberate. A phrase designed to be remembered, to echo, to become the summary of this encounter when it was recorded in whatever reports she would file.

They left. Fifteen of them, moving with the coordinated withdrawal of people who had rehearsed their exit as carefully as their entrance. No backs turned. No sudden movements. The crescent dissolved into the tree line like smoke into fog.

The paper sat on the rock.

I didn't pick it up. You did.

You read it. Your face did something I couldn't parse. Your gaze paused on a line, then returned to it.

"What does it say?"

You met my eyes. "It's a gesture of goodwill. Kind of. It says they've been recording your qi emissions from a distance. You emit bursts at irregular intervals, day and night. No fixed pattern. Sometimes every sixty heartbeats, but never longer than a quarter watch apart. Constantly." You paused. "It says that two nights ago, the night you forgot what I tried to tell you, the bursts stopped entirely for two watches. Complete silence. No emissions at all."

Two watches. The gap. The thing I couldn't remember, the hole that pulsed behind my eyes.

Two watches of silence in a pattern that never went silent. Whatever was inside me had turned inward to do something. To take something.

I rubbed my temples.

"Burn it," I said.

You folded the paper and put it in your notebook.

Close enough.
