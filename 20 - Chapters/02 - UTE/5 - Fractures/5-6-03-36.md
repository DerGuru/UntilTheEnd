I hadn't slept. Every sound had been amplified through the long night, every shift in the dark registering, my thoughts circling back to where they'd started. The same questions, the same uncertainty about what morning would bring. At some point I'd stopped trying and just lain there while the stars moved with imperceptible slowness.

The fire was already going when I gave up pretending. Water heated in the pot. The routine of a camp where someone competent had decided the day would begin with order.

I lay in my bedroll while you worked. Early gray light flattened the colors around us. You moved between the fire and the packs with the quiet efficiency that was your natural state, measuring water, portioning rice. But the rhythm was off. You checked the fire twice, adjusted a log that didn't need adjusting, and walked to the edge of camp for no reason. You came back to retie a knot on your pack, straighten the bedroll you'd already straightened, and brush dirt from a surface that wasn't dirty. The efficiency was there. The stillness underneath it wasn't.

I sat up into the pressure of the headache. I pressed my thumbs into my temples and breathed until the pain settled at a level I could bear.

"Morning," I said through the pain.

"Morning." You handed me tea without turning around. The cup was warm. The tea was adequate, brewed by a man whose attention was elsewhere, who had measured the leaves by muscle memory while his mind ran calculations I couldn't see.

I held the cup. Steam curled in the still air. The valley was quiet with early morning stillness. Before the first wind or birdsong, the fire murmured low and, faintly under it, water moved somewhere below us.

You sat down across from me to eat rice from the pot, mechanically, with the chopsticks held in the precise grip you never varied. You chewed, swallowed, and stared at a point to the left of my shoulder.

"You slept badly," I said.

"I slept fine."

You hadn't slept fine. The skin under your eyes was darker than yesterday, the jawline harder, the posture of a man who had spent the night not sleeping and was now performing the morning as if sleep had happened. I didn't push. We all have our performances.

I ate my rice. No mushrooms today, so it was plain and white, the taste of nothing in particular and the texture of sustenance. I scraped the bowl clean anyway, because that was what you did when food was finite and the next market unknown.

A bird landed on a boulder to the north. Small, brown, the anonymous mountain kind I had never learned to identify. It hopped twice, cocked its head, and flew away.

"There's a clearing," you said. "A little to the east. I found it yesterday."

"The one you were exploring?"

"Yes." You set down your bowl. "I'd like for us to go there this morning."

Your delivery was careful and weighted, a rehearsed sentence now being performed. The performance was almost good enough to hide the rehearsal.

Almost.

"Alright," I said.

You nodded, stood, and cleaned the pot with the methodical attention of a man who needed to clean a pot more than the pot needed cleaning. You scrubbed the inside with sand from the streambed, rinsed it, checked it, and scrubbed again. The pot was clean after the first pass. The second and third were for you.

We packed. You pulled on your old boots, the pair with the cracked sole you'd sealed with pine resin and stubbornness.

The morning had warmed. Blue showed through the gray. The valley below was still in shadow, the sunlight reaching only the upper ridges and painting them gold above the darker slopes.

You led me to the clearing, open, wide, and treeless in the center. Such spaces form where a flat shelf of rock lies too close to the surface for soil to accumulate. Short, wind-flattened grass grew between small stones. The horizon showed in every direction, the sky pressing down with the leaden weight of high altitude.

The moment we stepped into the open, my chest tightened. The edges of my vision sharpened across a space you had deliberately chosen for us. We had come here for a reason I couldn't examine. My pulse picked up, and the headache throbbed faster with it.

The grass under my feet was individual. Each blade pressed against the sole with its own pressure, its own angle. The air tasted of stone, altitude, and something metallic that might have been fear or the morning. A mouse darted across the ground two paces ahead, a gray streak low against the grass. I tracked it all the way to cover, unable to let a movement pass unnoticed.

There were no big trees, only openness. The clearing was a stage. I measured the distance to the nearest significant structure, a boulder field forty paces north, and found it adequate.

Adequate for what, I didn't ask myself. I rubbed my cold hands against my sleeves.

I breathed deliberately as the cultivation texts taught: in through the nose, slow, measured, letting the air fill the lower lungs first, then the upper. Out through the mouth. Again. My pulse slowed. The sharpness at the edges of my vision softened. The mouse was just a mouse again.

It wasn't enough to calm me, but I regained some control. The difference between a fire burning and a fire being watched.

I stopped in the center. The grass was damp under our feet, the dew soaking through to skin. Morning light, low and angular, cast shadows too long for the objects that made them.

You put your pack down at the edge of the clearing. You removed the things you normally carried close: the water flask, the small knife, and the fire steel. You set them on the ground against the pack, with the deliberate placement of someone keeping a workspace clear.

Then you approached me, stopping five paces away.

The distance was odd. We usually walked a companionable three paces apart. Now it was five. Two additional paces, measured out between a man and something that might react.

"I need to ask you a question," you said.

Your voice was steady. Your hands were not. They hung at your sides with a fine tremor, held there by an effort that showed in your rigid fingers. You had chosen to stand five paces away, and even there, keeping still cost you.

"Ask away."

I said it simply, openly, as one speaks to someone one trusts. I trusted you entirely.

You knew this. Its weight showed on your face, the strain of a man about to use someone's trust as a weapon because the target was behind walls nothing else could breach.

"It's going to hurt," you said. An apology, offered in advance, for a wound you were about to inflict deliberately.

"That's how you know it works," I said, feigning bravado. I had no idea how you were going to do this. No idea what you would do to me, how much it would hurt, how long the pain would last, or what the consequences would be. I just knew that whatever it was, it had to be done.

You closed your eyes. Your jaw worked like a man biting down on his last chance to stop, chewing it, swallowing it. You opened your eyes again.

"Yun."

The name was not even my real name. But it still worked like a tether, anchoring me to the moment when everything else was about to change. You stood like a man holding a stone over water, knowing where the ripples would reach once he let it fall.

"Have you killed Wei?"

The world stopped. The wind fell still. The grass no longer bent. My heartbeat hung suspended with everything around me: the clearing, the sky, the man five paces away, the mouse somewhere in the grass, and the mountains on every horizon.

My hearing narrowed. One moment there was wind, grass, and the small ambient sounds of the clearing. Then only my own breathing, loud, close, ragged through a tight throat.

The light went wrong. Too bright. The morning sun, gentle a moment ago, burned through my vision, bleaching the grass white and turning the sky into a flat sheet of glare. Darkness gathered at the edges, narrowing all that brightness.

I shivered with cold and my face burned. My hands were ice. Goosebumps rose on my arms. Sweat gathered at the back of my neck. Every sensation crowded in at once. The cold wouldn't ease under the heat, nor would the heat yield to it. I stood caught in both.

Have you.

The words hung in the air, each one separate, each one without meaning. Four words. A question. A subject, a verb, and an object. A sentence a child could understand. I struggled to put it together.

Killed.

I reached for the third word, tried to attach it to the first two, and almost understood what they meant together.

Wei?

The fourth word completed the sentence. The meaning was almost there.

The wall struck a fraction of a heartbeat before comprehension. Each word had approached the others, almost joined them. The meaning assembling itself from four simple words was taken mid-construction, like a page torn from a book before the last line could be read.

But the feeling was there.

Below the wall, below the defense, the feeling persisted without a cause I could name. My chest hurt. My hands were cold. Whatever had taken the words had left me this, as it had left the grief when you spoke his name by the fire. I couldn't retrieve the question, yet its weight pressed on me as if you were still asking it.

It had a shape. The shape was the answer to the question, almost in reach.

A crack.

Inside me, the wall split along a single hair-fine crack. It ran from the ache in my chest toward the place I could never reach, spreading through my defenses with the quiet inexorability of ice in stone. I stood on the same damp grass, facing you across the same distance, while something deep inside me gave.

The crack was small, but it was enough.

And through the crack, for one moment, I saw everything.
