You walked ahead of me on the narrow trail, your notebook put away. I was still thinking of two words I had glimpsed when you opened it in the tea house. A page before the map had lifted, then settled against its neighbor.

Half a heartbeat. Long enough to read the words and the date above them.

The handwriting was yours, but the strokes were shorter than usual. You had pressed harder. The cramped spacing left scarcely any room between the two words. I remembered how evenly you'd set down our measurements, leaving space for additions. Here, you had compressed an entire encounter into less room than you gave a change in the wind.

Two words. Written on a page dated when we first visited the crater.

*Approached. Refused.*

Someone had approached you at the crater. You had refused. The entry gave me the outcome and left everything else unsaid.

I had said nothing as you turned to the map. Your finger traced our route. We spoke about the narrowing course, the uninhabited mountains, and whatever was drawing me south. I kept the brief entry to myself.

I carried them through the tea and the headache. Afterward, we packed for the night march. For the first watch or so I concentrated on finding a route south through the broken terrain. Now I had room to think about what I'd seen.

We followed animal tracks between thick roots. A sliver of moon gave little light. I checked the star I'd been watching; it sat farther from its neighbors than I remembered from yesterday.

Usually you walked behind me, where you could see what I stopped to examine. Tonight you'd moved ahead without comment.

I suspected you'd seen my eyes move when the notebook opened. You knew how much I could read in half a heartbeat. Perhaps you were walking ahead because you thought I'd seen that page. From here I saw your shoulders but not your face.

The delegation had followed the disturbances with talismans. Those could locate us. To learn who traveled with me, the Pencil would need people. Informants could watch a camp or speak to someone at its edge. His scholars had evidently done more than gather qi.

The crater would have given them an opportunity.

I had been alone on the basalt with my attention directed downward. Someone nearby could have felt the energy. You had waited at the rim, out of my sight.

Someone had approached you while I pushed deeper. I remembered the basalt cracking under me. I had paid so little attention to the rim that a scout could have spoken to you there without my noticing.

You had refused. I could imagine practical reasons: perhaps the sect had offered less than you expected to gain by staying with me. That would have suited the man I kept telling myself you were.

You stepped over a root. Perhaps you had simply refused to betray me.

You had said nothing at the crater. In the many days afterward, you'd held the notebook closer to your chest. Even the gray-robed woman's offer of cooperation had passed without you mentioning the earlier approach. In the tea house, I'd learned of it only because a page had lifted.

You had made that choice without expecting me to acknowledge it. If you'd wanted credit, there had been plenty of opportunities to show me the page. You'd let them pass. As your shoulders moved under the pack, I imagined refusing an offer on someone's behalf, then carrying the knowledge quietly for so long.

I could have asked. But I said nothing.

If I asked, you'd have to explain a decision you'd chosen to keep private. Perhaps you had spared me the knowledge of how exposed I'd been at the crater. I could at least spare you an interrogation about why you'd helped me. Your name was easy enough to say from this distance. I let the moment in which I might have called it pass.

You walked ahead. I followed where the trail narrowed between roots and widened over bare ground. For once, I was content to leave you the lead.

The stars moved. The wrong one sat where it sat. We walked south.
