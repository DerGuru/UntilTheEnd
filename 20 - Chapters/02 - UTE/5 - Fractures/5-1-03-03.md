I practiced the technique again the next morning. You were still asleep, or pretending to be, which amounted to the same thing. The air held that gray, pre-dawn stillness that makes the world feel like a held breath.

I stood in the clearing behind the shrine and raised my arms.

The channels opened before I'd finished the stance, as suddenly as floodgates raised against a full reservoir. Qi poured through them. I shifted my feet through unfamiliar positions with an ease I couldn't account for. Each placement was exact. I knew when a heel should lift, how far a wrist should turn, and where the next movement began.

The technique was complex: seventeen distinct forms, each building on the last, each opening meridians I hadn't known existed. By the fifth form, my breathing had synchronized with patterns I couldn't have described if asked. By the ninth, the qi in my surroundings had begun to respond. Grass bent inward. The air thickened, and morning dew lifted from the leaves into a fine mist that hung at my waist.

I stopped at the twelfth form.

Continuing felt like stepping off an edge. My weight had begun to shift forward, toward a movement I might be unable to reverse.

I dropped my arms. The mist settled. The grass straightened. The morning continued as though nothing had happened.

My pulse was steady. My breathing was even. My right hand trembled. I pressed it against my leg, troubled by how little effort those twelve forms had required.

"Twelve," you said.

I turned. You were sitting in the doorframe of the shrine, legs crossed, notebook open. Your hair was flattened on one side from sleep. The pen in your hand was already moving.

"You stopped at twelve. The panel shows seventeen."

"I'm aware."

"You could do all seventeen."

"Probably."

"Why stop?"

The thirteenth form gathered in the channels I'd opened. It would draw more qi than the twelve before it. How much more, I couldn't tell. I wanted to understand what I was doing before I let that movement carry me further.

"Warming up," I said.

Your eyes narrowed and your mouth tightened. You wrote something in your notebook anyway. The scratch of your pen on paper was the loudest sound in the morning.

We left the shrine that afternoon. There was nothing more to find. I'd dug up every panel, mapped the qi anomalies, and measured their reach as best I could. I stood in different spots to feel the currents, comparing their pull against the slope of the ground. The shrine was one marker. I needed several.

The road south was narrow and laced with roots that made walking an exercise in attention. You walked behind me, quickening your steps until you almost caught up, then falling back again. I'd heard that rhythm before. Usually a question followed.

"The technique," you said eventually. "It's not in any manual I've seen."

"It's Pre-Consolidated. Most techniques from that era were lost."

"Lost or destroyed?"

"Both usually mean the same thing."

"Not always." You caught up to me where the path widened. "Some things are lost because nobody cared to preserve them. Some are lost because someone specifically wanted them gone."

I said nothing. You were right, and the distinction mattered. I didn't want to think about which category this technique fell into.

"You've never seen it before," you said. It wasn't a question.

"No."

"But you can do it."

"Apparently."

"Apparently." You let the word sit between us for a few steps. "You did twelve forms of a technique you've never seen, on your first attempt, at dawn, in a ruin you found three days ago." A pause. "And your explanation is 'apparently.'"

"It's the most accurate word I have."

You let it go. You were right, though. I had no explanation. The pull of the technique still ran through my shoulders, like knowing a song from its first few notes without remembering where you'd heard it.

We walked for the rest of the day. Forest thinned into scrubland as the mountains gave way to foothills and flatter ground. The qi here was spread across the earth in a layer so dilute it barely registered. Normal. Boring. I almost enjoyed it.

Almost, because the path was wrong.

I'd walked this route before, years or decades ago. The date was uncertain, the geography clear. This path should have curved east after the third ridge. Instead it went straight, cutting through land that I remembered as dense forest. I looked for stumps. Had the forest been cleared, or had I remembered it incorrectly?

My memory was not wrong.

I stopped to look back. The ridge behind us sat where it should, its shape and treeline unchanged. Only the path differed, by a gentle deviation. If I hadn't known its old course, I would never have noticed. Anyone walking here for the first time would find it natural.

Loose stones lay along the altered verge. Grass grew between them. Every small detail supported the path I was looking at. Only my memory contradicted it.

You walked past me, stopped, and looked back.

"What?"

"Nothing." I started walking again.

Two hundred paces further, you stopped by a pine that grew at an angle against the path's edge. You crouched to run your thumb along the bark where it compressed against the trail's stone border.

"This tree is sixty years old at least," you said. "But the roots grew around the path. As if the path was here before the tree."

You turned to me.

I kept walking.

Three anomalies: the qi at the shrine, the morning wind, and the path. Three in five days. Too many for coincidence. Too few for certainty.

I needed more.

The next ruin was two days south. A library, according to the old maps, or what was left of one. I'd been there once, centuries ago, when it still had a roof. Its custodian brewed terrible tea and told worse stories. He was long dead. The roof had probably collapsed too.

The chalk grit at the back of my tongue brought back the taste of that tea. It had been genuinely terrible: leaves picked too late in the season, dried too fast, and then over-steeped. Of everything I might have retained from that visit, I had retained the tea. The cup almost touched my lip again.

I wondered if the library was where I remembered it.

I wondered if I'd find it changed too, shifted by a finger's breadth or whole li, its foundations as settled as the stones beside this path.

I wondered who was doing the rearranging.

At that thought, pain spiked behind my left eye. It came and went within a breath. A bright patch lingered across my vision, like the mark of lightning after the sky goes dark.

I rubbed my temple as I walked.

Your pen scratched in your notebook.
