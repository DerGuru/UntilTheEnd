From the valley floor, the dark cave entrance sat where I remembered it. A trail zigzagged up the cliff toward two stone pillars carved as hands pressed together in meditation. The smallest finger on the left had broken off in the three centuries since my last visit. The right hand was intact. Its carving had always been finer, as though a right-handed sculptor had given that one more care.

I studied the surviving fingers from below. I remembered small things with a precision that unsettled me more than the large discrepancies. A shifted path or a valley could be argued away. After centuries, I might have confused one stretch of ground with another.

But I remembered the sculptor's unequal care. I remembered the finger that had since broken. Eleven switchbacks crossed the cliff, each turning at the same sharp angle I could still trace in memory.

That kind of memory isn't normal. I'd known it for a long time. I just hadn't had a reason to care until the things I remembered started disagreeing with the things that existed.

You stood at the base of the cliff and looked up.

"How far?" you asked.

"The entrance is about sixty paces up. The trail is manageable."

"Manageable." You studied the trail. The switchbacks were narrow, barely a person's width, carved into the cliff face by someone who'd valued efficiency over comfort. There were no railings. The drop was sheer.

"You could wait here," I said.

"I could." You shouldered your pack. "I won't."

We climbed.

You tested each foothold before settling your weight. Your eyes were fixed on the trail ahead. When it narrowed, you drew closer to the cliff. Your knuckles were white around the pack straps. You breathed in a careful rhythm that faltered whenever loose stone shifted under your boots.

You never said a word about the climb. Your knuckles stayed white around the straps, and you took the next step whenever I did.

Within ten steps of the entrance, the air chilled my face. The chalk taste arrived immediately, sharper here, metallic. I touched the wall fittings: torch brackets, equipment hooks, and iron rings set at regular intervals. They were so cold that my skin ached against them.

And the incense. Stronger here. Actual. As if someone had lit a stick somewhere deep inside and the smoke was drifting toward us through tunnels we couldn't see.

"Someone's here?" you whispered.

"No."

"But the smell—"

"There's no one."

The complex was extensive. I'd been here multiple times over the millennia. Once was centuries ago, when a cultivation sect had used these caves for meditation retreats. They'd carved chambers into the rock. Round rooms of various sizes, connected by narrow passages, designed to isolate practitioners from each other and from the world. Good acoustics for qi meditation. Good darkness for introspection.

The sect was long gone. The caves remained.

I moved methodically from room to room, palms flat against the walls. With fingers spread, I found where one trace thinned under another. The qi was layered, as it had been in the library. Cultivators had pressed energy into the rock over centuries of practice. Each layer was distinct, worn smooth by repeated use.

Under all of it lay that foundation again. Impossibly old qi permeated the bedrock. I followed a seam with my fingers, searching for where the energy ended. It ran through the mineral grain as though it had been there when the stone was still molten.

My qi. My signature.

The differences were small, like those between your handwriting at twenty and at forty. The essential shapes persisted. I could recognize the same hand, changed by age, practice, and forgetting.

I sat in the largest chamber. Its size and elaborately carved ceiling suggested the sect leader's meditation room. The designs were ambitious, pretentious. I pressed both palms flat against the floor.

Warmth crept from the foundation qi into my palms, then up my arms. I recognized its course. My fingers stiffened against the floor.

I pulled away.

The warmth stayed. My palms tingled. The headache pulsed, a single beat, like a drum struck once and left to resonate.

"Yun." Your voice, from the passage. "You should see this."

I found you in one of the smaller chambers. You were standing in front of a wall that had been carved more recently than the others. The stone was cleaner, the edges sharper, the technique different. Someone had been here after the sect had left. Someone had used this room as a study.

The wall was covered in inscriptions.

Different script from the library. More recent, about five hundred years old, based on the character forms. But the content matched. Anomaly reports. Qi flow deviations. Spatial inconsistencies. Terrain that didn't match records. Phenomena that defied established understanding.

Another researcher. Another catalog of wrongness.

"Same thing as the library," you said. You couldn't read this script either, though it was less ancient. You pointed to the arrangement of the entries, then to the matching layout in your notes. "Isn't it?"

"Similar."

"Similar content? Or similar—"

"Both."

I read. The writing was dense, with careful distinctions between what the researcher had measured and what they suspected. They checked each observation against earlier records. Seventeen anomalies over four years: qi surges, terrain shifts, and changes that left things looking as though they had always been that way. The researcher had given that last phenomenon its own name.

Seventeen anomalies. Four years.

I had seven in twelve days.

The pace was different. But the pattern held.

At the end, the writing stopped abruptly. The last character was half-formed, its final chisel mark still visible where the stroke had ended.

Two researchers. Two catalogues. Two identical endings.

I ran my thumb over the unfinished character. The stone was smooth where the chisel had rested. The mark ended cleanly, without a scrape across the surrounding surface. The writer might simply have opened their hand and dropped the tool.

"They stopped," you said.

"They forgot."

You were quiet for a long moment. The incense smell drifted through the passage, sourceless, sweet and wrong.

"You know that," you said. "You don't guess it. You know it."

"I know it."

"How?"

My thumb rested on the final stroke. In the qi under it, so close to my own, was the same abrupt loss I'd known while trying to follow a thought. The researcher had been forming a connection. Then it was gone, utterly, and the unfinished character sat before them. I knew that blankness.

I thought of my own list. Seven anomalies. I put my hands in my empty pockets. For now, every entry lived in my memory alone.

Not yet.

"We keep going," I said. "There will be more sites. More researchers. More endings."

"And you think they all end the same way."

I studied you. Your face was steady, your eyes clear. I searched for alarm. Either you were braver than I had allowed for, or you had yet to grasp what we were looking at.

"I think," I said, "that the question isn't how they end. It's why they started. What drew them here. What made them look for the exact thing I'm looking for."

"And?"

I turned back to the wall. My thumb had cleared the dust from the unfinished character. Its writer had been close enough to something true to begin putting it into words.

"And whether what stopped them will stop me."

You didn't ask the obvious question.
