Evening settled over our camp by the river, two days past the library. You'd built a small, adequate fire without fussing over the kindling. Scrolls lay around you in order of age, with your copies next to the originals. You'd added translations of the key passages. I had stopped trying to convince you that chronology wasn't always the most useful arrangement.

"You think you're like them," you said.

"I know I'm like them," I said.

You set down your brush. You'd been reproducing a passage from the third scroll, the one about the world obeying, in cleaner characters, as archivists preserve texts by rewriting them.

"You know." Not a question. A confirmation.

"My qi obeys my will, not my technique. My body refuses to die. The world around me adjusts. I experience everything they documented. The difference is time."

"And the fact that you're still here."

"And that."

A log shifted in the fire. Sparks rose. Beyond its light, water broke against stones I could no longer see. The river was louder now.

"You said the others were erased," you said. "Their memories. Their thoughts. The connections they were building."

"Their documentation ends. Mid-sentence. Mid-thought."

"You think the same force that keeps you alive reached in and—"

"Folded the page. Yes."

You picked up your pen, then set it down again.

"Then why hasn't it happened to you?"

I looked down at the oldest scroll. Since finding the first account, I'd returned to that question whenever there was a pause in the work. I had always found another cylinder to open or a measurement to check. Here you were waiting for an answer.

"I don't know," I said.

"Guess."

"I don't guess."

"Then hypothesize. You're a better scholar than you pretend to be."

That almost made me smile. I looked away before you could see it. You'd become good at getting past a refusal by offering me a more respectable name for what you wanted.

"Hypothesis one," I said. "I'm stronger. The protection is identical in all of us, but mine is more robust. The erasure that stopped the others can't stop me."

"Problems?"

"The erasure appears to be part of the protection. One source. One signature. You don't outrun your own shadow."

"Hypothesis two."

"I'm different. Fundamentally. Whatever was in them is something else in me. Similar symptoms, different condition."

"Problems?"

"The qi signature is identical. Not similar. Identical."

"Hypothesis three."

The fire consumed a thin edge of bark. It curled, glowed, and broke away as ash. Each change followed from the last, and nothing needed questioning. For a few breaths, that helped.

"The erasure hasn't happened to me yet," I said. "The others had years. Decades. Perhaps centuries or millennia. Then they stopped. I've had millennia. But maybe that's not immunity. Maybe it's a longer wick."

I fell quiet before offering another possibility.

"Or it already happened to me. And these are my own records." I looked from one scroll to the next. At first, this one was easier to examine than the others.

"Problems?"

"The scrolls describe practitioners with techniques, formal training, and traditions they could name. I don't remember learning as they describe it. Nothing like you did. Nothing an observer would recognize as that kind of cultivation. And each account was written by someone else. An observer. A companion who followed them and kept records." I shook my head. "I've never had that. No one has sat beside me with a notebook, cataloguing what I do. Except for you."

You looked down at your notebook. Your pen rested on a half-finished line, and you left it there. Firelight shifted across your face as you looked from the notebook to me.

"You're afraid," you said. Not unkindly.

"I'm aware."

"Of what? The erasure?"

"Of the possibility that I'm the one who causes it."

Silence.

The fire cracked. Beyond it, the river rushed over the stones. A moth circled the flames three times, then veered into the dark.

"The qi under the ruins," you said carefully. "Your signature."

"Yes."

"Laid down centuries before the buildings existed."

"Millennia, in most cases."

"The researchers who documented those anomalies and then forgot were studying *your* qi. Without knowing it."

"Yes."

"And you think something in that qi, in *your* qi, detected that they were getting close to understanding it. And erased the thread."

"I think," I said, "that whatever keeps me alive is very good at ensuring it isn't understood. And the method it uses is forgetting. Other people's forgetting."

You were quiet for a long time. The fire shrank between us. Wood lay at hand, but neither of us added it.

"Whatever is in your qi does it," you said. "Not you. You said that. The instrument, not the musician."

"Yes."

"Maybe that's the wrong distinction."

I looked at you.

"Maybe it is you. And you don't remember because the same force that makes others forget is making you forget too. Not what you're investigating. What you're doing."

Pain began at the base of my skull. It spread slowly forward until a dull ache pressed behind both eyes. I tried to follow what you'd said, but the effort sharpened it. Looking at the fire was easier.

I pressed my palms against my temples and held them there.

The ache held. The river came through clearly, and the titles on the scrolls were still legible. Yet whenever I tried to consider your suggestion, I lost my place.

"Yun?"

"I'm fine."

"You're pressing your head."

"I have a headache."

"Same kind as before?"

I lowered my hands and opened my eyes. The fire had shrunk further. Its dying light reached only half your face.

"Same kind as always," I said.

You held my gaze. Then you added wood to the fire. Flames caught along its underside. In the renewed light, a moth began circling. I couldn't tell whether it was the same one.

"I'm going to write something in my notebook," you said. "And tomorrow you're going to let me keep the page."

The folded summary pressed against my skin. I held my hand clear of the pocket. You knew about the missing page.

"Write what you want," I said.

You wrote. I watched the fire. The moth circled.

"I heard something else at the river," you said. "A trader mentioned Resonance Talismans. Old technique. Someone's been buying rare components for them, the kind that haven't been in demand for centuries." You paused. "He said the buyer was meticulous. He took notes on everything."

I filed that away without answering. Talismans were tools. Someone collecting tools didn't necessarily know what they were building.

Tomorrow we would leave for more ruins. The library had given us enough to know which questions to ask next. Those dead cultivators had followed similar questions until their records stopped, and their different hands lay side by side in the firelight.

Ten dead. One alive.

"Maybe," you said, not looking up from your writing, "you're not different from them at all. Maybe all of them thought they were the exception. All of them kept searching. All of them were still asking questions right up until the moment they stopped."

I said nothing.

"Maybe the difference between you and them is just time," you said. "You haven't stopped yet. But maybe you will."

"Is that supposed to be comforting?"

"No." Your pen scratched. "It's supposed to be a warning."

I lay back on the ground by the fire. Above the river's sound, the stars turned in their correct positions. Those cultivators had looked up at much the same sky from these hills. I traced a constellation and wondered how long each of them had been allowed to keep searching.

For now, I could still remember every account we'd read. I repeated their dates to myself under the stars.

I closed my eyes.
