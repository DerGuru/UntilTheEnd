At the river crossing three days south of the library, merchants crowded a tea house with their packs still at their knees. A few lower-ranked cultivators sat among the passing travelers. The trading town stood where two roads met. We should have heard prices or complaints about the journey. Instead, people kept asking one another which roads were safe.

A tea seller told us about a village to the east where the qi had reversed direction overnight. Foundation-level cultivators, after decades of quietly building their channels, had woken with their cultivation destabilized. Meridians had collapsed. Stored qi had scattered. Some had lost everything they'd built. Others had died.

A traveling merchant described lights above the northern mountains. Normally invisible qi currents had shone through the clouds in branching threads. They had lasted a watch or more. He'd watched from a hilltop and said it looked like the sky was bleeding.

A sect courier passing through with dispatches said that two regional sects had sealed their gates. No entry or exit. They were calling it a disturbance of the spiritual climate. I recognized the phrase: reassuringly formal, broad enough to cover anything.

You sat at the tea house table and listened to all of it, composed, cataloguing evidence.

"The whole region," you said, after the courier had left. "Can you determine how far?"

I'd been feeling disturbances for days, spreading beyond the places we'd visited. I compared each reported location with our route. There was a delay after our passing, but the timing matched often enough that I stopped treating it as coincidence.

"Not just the region. But weaker the farther you go," I said.

Your eyes went from me to the merchants at the next table. I waited for the question. You swallowed it. Discussing our part in their troubles here would have been a poor idea even by our standards.

You drank your tea instead. I drank mine. The tea seller came by twice to see if we wanted more and twice I waved him away, because whatever was happening in the world outside this window was not going to be improved by putting more tea into our cups.

That evening, the sunset lingered far too long. Colors shifted along the western horizon: purple shading into green above a thin line of gold. They held for as long as a short incense stick takes to burn. When stars appeared, I checked their positions. Those, at least, were right.

You stood at the window of our room and watched it.

"Beautiful," you said.

"Dangerous."

"Can't it be both?"

I didn't answer. I guess it could.

The next morning brought more reports. A road that had been straight for a century now curved around a hill travelers swore hadn't been there seven days earlier. Someone had seen a waterfall running upward. A grove had doubled in size overnight, its new trees as old and established as the originals.

My signature ran through the disturbances beneath the town. With each site I visited, the reports came from farther away. I was asking more questions, getting closer to an answer. Meanwhile, people were losing roads they had walked all their lives.

I was making it worse.

Outside the town, dust lifted from the road ahead. The hills past it looked ordinary.

I walked on, putting the reports in order. The first shrine, then the changed path. The stone that had hummed under my hands. Each discovery had led us onward. Now I had reports of deaths to set against them.

My signature was in them. Each attempt to understand it had been followed by changes reaching farther into inhabited land.

People were dying because I was looking for answers.

I said nothing to you. When you opened the maps, you traced our route alongside the reported anomalies. Your finger paused at each place we'd visited. You looked up at me before moving to the next. I suspected you had reached the same conclusion.

I wasn't ready to say it.
