I woke irritated. The irritation had no name, no target, no identifiable cause. It sat in my chest like a fist, clenched around something I couldn't see. Residue. The dregs of whatever had happened to me last night, whatever you had tried three times to explain, whatever my own mind had erased with the surgical precision of something that knew exactly what it was doing.

The gap was still there. Pulsing. A hole in the shape of a truth I couldn't remember.

I stood and stretched. The cool morning air smelled of pine resin, dew, and damp earth. Around us lay the clean stillness that comes before the world fills up with heat, noise, and the business of existing.

Ten paces away, a branch snapped. The sound was sharp, loud, the crack of wood being broken by force. The air was still; nothing moved among the branches. A pine bough that had been intact a moment ago now hung at an angle, exposing pale, wet wood. The break was as clean as if someone had gripped it and pulled.

You looked at the branch, then at me. Your hand moved to your notebook.

Something in the undergrowth stirred. A small, fox-like creature, a minor spirit beast of the sort that lives between wildlife and cultivation. It had little intelligence, but enough sensitivity to follow qi as a dog follows scent. It had been sleeping in the brush. Now it was awake, head tilted, ears forward, staring at me.

It was listening to something other than the branch. Something below the audible range, below the physical range, in the qi-spectrum where only spirit beasts and very old cultivators could listen.

It heard me. Whatever I was leaking, whatever emotion was translating into force, breaking branches and catching the attention of creatures that shouldn't have noticed me, it heard that.

You wrote. I packed. We walked.

The day should have been unremarkable. The trail was well-maintained, cutting through mixed forest toward a river crossing we'd been aiming for since the mountain pass. The weather was clear. Terrain was manageable. The normal business of putting one foot in front of another and letting the distance accumulate like a debt being paid in installments.

But each disturbance followed something I felt. The pattern went back to the shrine, to the first wrong wind, to the first anomaly. Now it was different. Until now, the world had adjusted quietly behind my back, leaving changes that seemed to have always been there. Now it was responding as I watched. To me. To what I felt.

The sect scouts appeared around mid-morning.

Two of them, on the ridge above the trail, positioned with the calculated casualness of people who want you to know they're there. Gray robes. Talismans at their belts. They watched and waited, the same patient observation that the Pencil's delegation had promised and the Pencil's organization had clearly delivered on.

My hands clenched.

Three pines, old, sixty years of growth at least, split along their trunks. The breaks ran straight down the center, through sound wood, as if invisible hands had gripped each side and pulled. The wood screamed. Each long tear carried through the forest, fiber after fiber parting as the trunks were forced into halves.

The scouts disappeared. Alive, unhurt. They simply chose to be elsewhere, very quickly, on the refined survival instinct of people trained to recognize when a situation has outgrown their authority.

"Yun," you said. Quiet. The quiet of someone standing next to a drawn blade.

I stared at the trees. Three of them, split. The fresh wood inside was pale, wet, and glistening. Sap ran down the torn surfaces like blood from exposed wounds.

"That was not—" I started.

It was. I knew it was. The lie died before it formed.

I unclenched my hands. The forest settled. The sound of tearing stopped. Birds, which had gone silent, began again, tentatively, one call at a time.

"Anger," you said. Not a question.

"The scouts."

"I saw them. They're gone now." You looked at the trees. "Along with about a hundred and eighty years of combined growth."

I said nothing. We walked.

The rain started half a watch later.

The rain came from a clear sky, without a cloud or a change in the wind to account for it. It fell in a circle twelve paces across, centered on me. Beyond the circle, dry ground. Inside the circle, a downpour heavy enough to flatten the grass and soak through your clothes in heartbeats.

You were standing at my side when it started. You didn't move.

I hadn't been thinking about Wei. Not consciously. But he was killed by a sect, even if indirectly. The thought had arrived as Wei-thoughts always arrived now: uninvited, unannounced, triggered by something I couldn't identify. I had pushed it away. Not now. The grief had gone somewhere all the same; it had found an outlet in the sky.

Twelve paces of private rain.

You stood in it. Water ran down your face, plastered your hair to your skull and darkened your robes from gray to black. You made no move to step out of the circle.

You stood there getting wet, then wetter, and said nothing.

That was your answer to whatever I was, to whatever I was doing to the trees, the weather, the ground under our feet. You stood in it.

The rain lasted a few hundred heartbeats. The surge of grief burned hot and brief, then left something slower, cooler, and harder to dislodge. It settled rather than erupted.

When it stopped, the sun was still shining. The ground inside the circle was dark and wet. The ground outside was dry.

"That was—"

"You," you said.

I nodded.

We walked. We dripped. I extended my qi consciously this time, pushing warmth outward in a small, deliberate act. The moisture lifted from our robes in thin wisps of steam. Within sixty heartbeats our clothes were dry, both sets, as if the rain had never happened.

The ground still bore the mark. The wet circle behind us, twelve paces of darkened earth under a clear sky, would take some time to dry on its own.

The frustration hit that afternoon.

I'd tried to read the scroll copies again. Your transcriptions, your careful hand reproducing ancient texts in modern characters. I'd unrolled them, my eyes tracking the lines. The characters had been there, legible and clear.

And my mind had refused.

Where the night before had offered slow, gentle erasure, this was a wall. Solid. Instant. I might have been trying to read a language I'd never learned. Except I had read these. I had understood them, discussed them with you and drawn conclusions from them.

None of it was left.

The ground trembled, barely. A tremor you felt through your soles, the low bass vibration of stone adjusting under stress. Dust rose from the trail in thin curtains. A rock the size of a man's head rolled three paces downhill without being touched.

I set the scrolls down.

The trembling stopped.

A branch. Three trees. Rain. A tremor.

Each one bigger. Each one less controllable. Each one more directly tied to whatever was happening inside me. The emotions I couldn't manage, the grief I couldn't process, the frustration I couldn't resolve, the gap behind my eyes where understanding used to live.

I was a string wound too tight. And the world was the instrument.

"Our question," I said. "Why it doesn't destroy the evidence."

You looked up from your notebook. Your hair had dried in tangles.

"The scrolls survive because I want them to. The notebook survives because I want it to." I gestured back along the trail, toward the split trees, the wet ground we'd left behind, and the dust still settling from the tremor. "It follows emotion. All of it. Anger breaks things. Grief makes rain. And wanting to keep something keeps it."

You went still. Then the pen moved.

"The warping can't destroy what you want to preserve," you said. Testing it. Tasting the logic.

"It obeys the same will it serves."

"Then the erasure—" You stopped. "The forgetting. The three times. That's also—"

"Also emotional. Also mine. Something in me that's terrified of the answer."

You wrote carefully this time, not fast. This insight, unlike the last three, might actually stay. We were one step from the truth I kept losing, feeling around the lock without opening the door.

The headache pulsed. Once. Hard. But distant, like thunder with a long stretch of dry sky between us.

I let the thought settle and held it carefully, as you hold something fragile that you intend to pick up again.
