Xiǎohú circled us openly now, darting from one side of the trail to the other after so many days of concealment. She nosed into a bush, sniffed a rock, and trotted ahead. Every few paces she paused to look back at us. We kept our slower pace while she crossed the distance twice, rustling through the undergrowth on either side.

You watched her with more interest than wariness now, following each movement as if it added something to your notes.

Xiǎohú disappeared into the undergrowth, reappeared ten paces ahead, and vanished again. Dry leaves rustled as she passed through them. Occasionally her amber eyes showed between branches as she looked back toward us.

A bird called. Xiǎohú's ears swiveled. She froze with the full-body stillness of a predator following sound. Two heartbeats. Three. The bird called again. Xiǎohú rushed ahead into the underbrush.

Xiǎohú's fur rustled against the dry brush. Your footsteps fell alongside it, the left one heavier, as it always was when the trail inclined. None of it drowned out the rest. In the market I had strained to keep the sounds separate. Now the same volume came with less effort. Somewhere since the village, filtering had become habit. The world was still loud. I was just better at listening.

By midday the trail dropped toward water. We heard the river before we saw it, a low, continuous rush through stone. Xiǎohú's ears had turned that way sixty heartbeats before the sound reached us. She was already at the bank when we arrived, drinking in quick, precise laps.

The river was narrow, cold, and clear. A mountain stream that had grown into something wider, fed by tributaries and snowmelt, running over smooth stone with the cold clarity of water that has never been near agriculture or settlement. The bottom showed as a mosaic of river-tumbled rock, each piece worn smooth by the current.

You set up camp on the bank. Xiǎohú finished drinking and stretched out in a sunny patch, her amber eyes half-closed, her legs loose against the ground.

I went to the water and knelt. The stones at the river's edge were genuinely cold against my knees, the new, insistent sensitivity making every physical contact an experience. I leaned forward, cupped water, and brought it to my face.

Cold. Achingly, beautifully cold. I held the water against my face, its temperature shifting against my skin. I knew this river by that cold now, as distinctly as by its shape.

I washed my face. Water over forehead, over cheeks, over closed eyes. I opened them on my reflection in the water.

The shallow pool near the bank was sheltered from the current by stone. Its still surface held my face like a mirror, soft at the edges, the details distorted. Recognizable. Unmistakably me.

Tired. That came first. I looked like a woman who had slept too little for too long, whose rest no longer restored her. Lines had appeared since last month. Shadows under my eyes showed the cost of headaches, heightened sensitivity, and taking in too much through channels unaccustomed to it.

Thin. Thinner than I'd been. My cheekbones were more prominent. The jaw sharper. The face paring down to essentials, losing its softness.

Real. That came last. The face in the water was seen from outside, with flaws I could examine instead of smoothing away in my imagination. I usually carried an idea of my face without needing to look at it. Here were the lines that idea left out, the particular shadows under each eye. Human.

I smiled.

The smile arrived as the rain had arrived, without intention, without cause, from some internal process I couldn't name. Maybe the child. Maybe the rice. Maybe the cold water on my skin. Maybe just the simple, animal satisfaction of being alive near a river on an afternoon when nothing terrible was happening yet.

But the reflection had smiled first.

The smile on the face in the water preceded mine by a fraction of a heartbeat, a blink's worth of time, the irreducible minimum of *before.* My lips began to move. The muscles of my face engaged, the contraction humans call smiling. And the face in the water was already doing it.

Already smiling.

Already there. Ahead of me, as if it had known.

I froze. My face went still, the muscles locked with the gesture unfinished.

The reflection kept smiling.

The same face, the same features. The reflection smiled while I did not. I stared at that impossible difference, waiting for it to frighten me.

I expected fear. For many days the pattern had been reliable: something impossible happened, fear followed, and the wall took what I understood. That was how things went.

But the fear didn't come.

The reflection smiled at me, and warmth spread through my chest, deeper than the temperature changes I'd felt since the breakthrough, through my whole body. The warmth of being looked at by someone who wants you to be okay.

I tilted my head. The reflection's smile was gentle. Relieved. That was the part that undid me, the part I couldn't process and didn't try to.

The crack pulsed. The feeling hummed. Something moved through me, too large to be a thought, too warm to be a memory.

The headache came. Sharp, precise, the warning I knew by now. The wall had noticed. Whatever I'd been about to understand was already dissolving, the shape of it collapsing even as I reached for it. The edges of something enormous retreated behind the architecture that kept me from knowing what I was.

But the warmth held. The wall could take the thought. It couldn't take the feeling.

The reflection's smile faded. Slowly. The corners of the mouth turned down. The eyes changed. The face in the water wore sadness, deep and old, the kind that has lived in a body so long it has become structural.

Then that faded too. The expression relaxed, returning to my own. Whatever had looked through my eyes had said what it needed to say and stepped back.

My face in the water was only my face again. Tired. Thin. Real. No longer smiling, on either side.

I sat back. My knees were wet. My hands were wet. The water in my cupped palms showed me only a distorted reflection of sky.

I breathed.

The feeling in my chest, the nameless frequency, the vibration that the deletion couldn't reach, was different. Warmer. As if something that had been running cold had, for the first time, been acknowledged.

I sat by the river for a long time. The water ran. The stones were cold. Sunlight moved slowly across the shallows.

When I stood, my legs were stiff and my face was dry. The crack in the wall hadn't closed or widened. It was there. Stable.

I wiped my hands on my robe and walked back to camp.
