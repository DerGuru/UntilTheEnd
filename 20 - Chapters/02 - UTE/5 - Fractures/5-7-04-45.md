On the third day of walking, the trail opened onto a crossroads town, a place built where travelers need to eat, sleep, and buy things. Its market square was, for reasons no one had bothered to explain, a near-perfect rhombus. Thirty buildings, maybe forty, clustered around the intersection with the pragmatic density of structures built for function rather than aesthetics.

People.

For the first time in many days, we were among farmers, traders, and ordinary townsfolk. These people knew nothing about me. Their belts carried tools or coin pouches instead of the talismans of those watching for disturbances. A woman sold vegetables from a stall set out for the morning. An old man sharpened tools at the corner of the intersection. Children moved between adults with the purposeful aimlessness of small humans performing the important work of being young.

I walked among them and felt everything.

The new sensitivity made the village overwhelming with ordinary detail. Normal life at full volume felt like a first encounter. Cooking fires smelled distinct, each carrying the flavor of its particular pot, food, and fuel. Voices overlapped: laughter, argument, the bass murmur of commerce, and the treble clarity of children. Underfoot, the packed earth of the market square had been worn smooth by generations of feet. Each step transmitted its density and moisture, the texture of a surface walked on ten thousand times.

It was almost too much. On the trail I'd learned to attend to one bird, one scent, and one texture at a time. Here they came all at once. But the skill was there now, built over three days of walking. It strained. It held.

You were in your element. Markets were your natural ground. You'd sold the herbs I'd pointed out on the trail, roots and stems that grew only above the qi line, farther past the last settlement than most gatherers traveled. I knew them by sight, smell, and the subtle hum they carried. The apothecary at the edge of the market had gone still when you opened the pouch, then paid without haggling. We split the money and separated. You moved through the stalls efficiently, your pouch heavy for the first time in many days. Rice. Dried vegetables. A spool of heavy thread for mending. Salt.

I bought fruit. A basket of small, hard pears from a woman whose stall had the military precision of someone who took produce seriously. She counted my coins, copper, the small denominations of rural marketplaces, and frowned.

"You're three short," she said.

I counted again. She was right. The sum was wrong, three coins fewer than it should have been.

"Sorry." I found the additional coins and paid.

The pears were beautiful. Smooth-skinned, green-gold, the variety that grows in mountain valleys and carries the altitude in its flesh. I expected firm, dense flesh and the sweetness those trees produce despite thin soil and cold nights.

I bit into one. The flesh inside was brown. Mealy. Rotted from the center outward, the exterior perfect and the interior past salvage.

I threw it away and tried another. Also brown. A third was brown too.

Three pears. Three perfect exteriors. Three rotten interiors. Finding three bad fruits in a basket of thirty was unlikely enough to be suspicious and ordinary enough to be deniable.

The words from the crack came back. "The debt. The cost. Every scar in the fabric of what should have been." They gave a name to what I'd been feeling. Debt. Now its shape showed in these persistent, nagging failures, each ordinary enough on its own, accumulating against me.

I found you at the fabric stall, holding your outer robe, my outer robe now, technically, the one I'd been wearing since the clearing. You were discussing repairs with the vendor. A tear in the shoulder seam. It also needed shortening. Simple work for someone with the right tools.

The vendor took the robe, threaded a needle and made three precise stitches.

The thread snapped.

New thread. New stitches. Snap.

Third attempt. Heavier thread. Slower stitches. The needle went in, the thread pulled through, the seam began to close.

The fabric tore along a different seam. A new tear opened a fingerbreadth from the repair, with the quiet sound of threads parting.

The vendor stared at the robe. The expression of a professional encountering a failure that couldn't be explained by skill, materials, or any of the variables that professionals control.

"I'll try—"

"Don't," you said. You took the robe back and folded it. The tear was visible, a thin line of separation in the gray fabric, another entry in the growing catalog of small things going wrong.

You glanced my way. I looked toward the waste pile where I'd left the rotten pears.

"We'll buy a new one," you said. The tone of someone solving a problem by refusing to argue with it.

The woman three stalls down sold traveling clothes in sturdy fabric, simply cut for people who wore things out and replaced them without sentiment. You held up a gray robe against your shoulders, then chose another and sized it against mine. One for you, one for me, so we'd both have an outer layer again.

You paid, unfolded yours, and put it on.

The seam at the left shoulder split on the first movement. Thread pulled quietly free of fabric, with the small, definitive sound of something that had been made correctly and had failed anyway.

You stood still. The new robe hung open at the shoulder, the tear identical in location to the one on the old robe, as if the failure had transferred rather than been repaired. As if the problem had never been the garment.

You took it off. I put mine on. It held. The fabric didn't tear.

You looked from the robe in your hands to the one on me. You ran a finger along your torn seam, then glanced at my intact shoulder.

You went back and bought another robe. You put it on carefully, handling it as if you expected it to fail. It held. The seams stayed.

Three robes. You'd meant to repair one.

The connection was clear. The same debt, the same slow extraction. Each loss was trivial in isolation; together, they were beginning to empty the pouch.

We ate lunch at a stall. Rice and braised greens, simple food prepared in a small space with little time. The food was adequate. Warm. Real as only market food is real, made by a person, for a person, in the exchange of coins for nourishment that had been going on as long as there had been markets.

The greens had the too-bitter edge of vegetables stored a day too long. The rice was fine. The rice was always fine. I was beginning to count on it, which seemed unwise given the morning we'd had.

I ate. You ate. The fox-spirit waited at the market's edge, outside the human space, amber eyes in the shadow of a storage building.

After lunch, we walked through the market again. You still had to get some salt. I bought nothing. The rotten pears had exhausted my interest in shopping. We passed between stalls, people, and conversations that meant nothing to us, everything to those having them. The anonymity of public spaces where nobody looks at you because nobody has reason to.

There were two traders at the salt stall. One lean, one wide, the complementary geometry of a business partnership that had survived on the road longer than it had survived in any fixed location. The lean one was talking. The wide one listened with professional patience, having heard this story many times and knowing his role was to listen anyway.

"...froze them where they stood. Both armies. A hundred men on each side. She looked at them and they couldn't move. Not a muscle. That's how she ended it."

"Which war?" the wide one asked.

"The village war near the southern pass. Two villages fighting over a boundary stone."

"Two villages don't have armies."

"They had enough."

I stopped walking. The lean trader made a grand, practiced gesture, holding his listener's attention with a wide sweep of his hand.

"There was no freezing," I said.

Both traders turned. I knew how inappropriate the abrupt correction was. A woman they'd never seen had interrupted a story she had no business knowing, correcting details she had no reason to possess.

"She told them the rock wasn't a boundary marker," I said. "It was a glacial erratic. It had been sitting in that field since before either village existed. Since before anyone had *fields*. And their fields didn't even overlap. So she told them to stop fighting about it. And they felt foolish enough to stop."

The lean trader stared. The wide one let out a breath that was almost a laugh.

"That's not how I heard it," the lean one said.

"That's how it happened."

"And you know this because—"

"I've been to both villages. They think the freezing version is hilarious."

I walked on with the small irritation of hearing my own history told wrong by someone who wasn't there. I'd been the only witness to a correction nobody wanted a hundred times before. Most of the world's stories were wrong, and there was little to be done about it.

A child appeared at the market's edge.

She was six, maybe seven, old enough to navigate a crowd independently and young enough not to understand why adults don't say the things that are obviously true.

The girl was staring at me with the unblinking, uncomfortably direct gaze of a child who has noticed something and hasn't yet learned that this is supposed to be discreet. She kept staring until she walked over. She stopped two paces from me and looked up.

"Why are you so sad?" she asked.

"I'm not sad," I said.

"Yes you are."

Was I sad?

The crack pulsed. The feeling hummed. Under the wall, the guardian, and the new gentle warning that had replaced the old brutal erasure, something ached with loss.

I was sad. I had lost something. Or someone? I didn't know what. The content was gone, the memory with it. The wall had seen to that. But the sadness was still there.

"I don't know." I shrugged.

She tilted her head. The gesture of a child turning over an answer that doesn't fit the expected format.

Your hand reached my shoulder. Gentle. "Come on," you said.

I let you guide me away. Away from the child, away from the mirror, away from the simple question that had cut deeper than any cultivation technique.

The child waved. The small, casual wave of someone who has said what they wanted to say and is done.

I didn't wave back. I should have. But my hands were at my sides and my chest was full of something that might have been sadness.

You walked closer after that.

"Why are you so sad?"

I didn't have an answer.
