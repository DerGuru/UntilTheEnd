You fell asleep first. Your breathing evened. Your hand, which had been resting on your notebook, relaxed.

I stayed awake.

There was a fourth scroll. I hadn't mentioned it.

I'd found it during the night search, wedged behind a collapsed shelf in the deepest alcove. The cylinder was smaller than the others, its seal harder and darker than wax. I'd broken it open, read the first three lines by qi-light, and recognized something too familiar to show you.

I'd slipped it into my robe before you woke. Now I unrolled it by firelight. The paper was so thin I could see the characters from either side. Its ink had aged to a deep bronze that caught the light with a metallic sheen.

It predated written history as it was now taught. I knew of no other surviving record from the civilization that had produced it. I could name no standing building. Yet someone had kept this scroll intact, carrying it far enough through time to reach a library older than most present nations.

The first lines were observational. Precise, austere.

*Forty-three deviations in two lunar cycles. The qi responds before I act. The world adjusts to accommodate my presence. I have documented each instance. They are consistent.*

Forty-three deviations in two months. I had found fourteen in twenty-one days. Their pace was close enough to mine that I checked the numbers again.

I read on. This was the oldest account we'd found, the earliest record of someone noticing what I was noticing. The writer had been unusually thorough. Each observation had a place next to earlier measurements, with careful notes on what had changed. They tested their own conclusions.

*I have concluded that the source is internal. The deviations follow changes in my emotions, my position, and most critically, my intentions. When I intend to go somewhere, the path adjusts. When I wish for rain, the clouds shift. When I expect safety, threats diminish. Cause follows where I expected consequence. What I experience seems to shape the world.*

My hands tightened on the scroll.

*I have attempted to control this effect. I cannot. The adjustments occur without a conscious choice, at a depth I can scarcely reach. They are as automatic as breathing, as involuntary as a heartbeat, and as difficult to stop.*

I set the scroll on my knee and held it flat. The fire was dying. You breathed.

I could have written those sentences in that order. The writer moved from observation to possibility, then tested the possibility as I would have done. Several times I anticipated the next line. Each time the paper agreed, and the reasoning was uncomfortably close to my own.

I read the final section.

*What will happen when I understand what I am? I have observed a pattern. The closer I come to comprehension, the harder it becomes to continue. My thoughts scatter. My focus breaks. I reach for a conclusion. A moment later, my mind is blank. I can no longer read my own notes.*

*Something does not want me to know.*

*I am not certain it is something external. I am increasingly certain, I*

The scroll ended there. I searched the blank paper past the last word for a faded stroke. As in the other accounts, the sentence ended unfinished. Its final word was *I*.

*I*

I rolled the scroll slowly, supporting the thin paper with both hands. It had survived longer than any of the other accounts, and I kept its edges level, unwilling to let my haste damage what so many centuries had spared.

I placed it in the cylinder, sealed it, and put it back into my pack.

I was the source. Something in me kept turning me away from that thought.

Rain struck the ground all at once. Within heartbeats, water fell so densely that the fire hissed out. I had scarcely put my pack under cover before the earth around it darkened.

You woke, rolled over, and pulled your blanket over your head. Then you went back to sleep. Apparently rain was a problem for whoever was still awake.

I stood.

Rain struck the qi barrier around my face. Cultivators at my level seldom get wet involuntarily; that barrier forms as readily as a blink. I let it drop. The first drops hit my forehead hard enough to make me close my eyes.

The rain soaked my robe through to the skin. Cold spread across my shoulders, then down my back. It was the cold of mountain water, sharper wherever a fresh stream found its way under the cloth. The fabric should have repelled it. I held the barrier down while the robe grew heavy.

I stood in it.

Water ran down my face. My hair plastered to my skull. The robe clung to my shoulders and arms, its weight pulling against me whenever I moved. The wet seam along each sleeve rubbed at my arms.

I tilted my face into the rain and kept my eyes open.

For twenty-one days I had measured ruins, read scrolls, and followed evidence I could barely bring myself to consider. Standing here required less of me. I loosened my shoulders under the weight of the robe.

I watched where the water went. It gathered at my cuffs, ran from my fingertips, and dropped to the ground. It fell without any direction from me.

At least, I thought it did. The paths had bent around me. Forests had filled places where I remembered clearings. Beneath each ruin, I had found qi too close to mine. Here water struck bare rock next to my feet. I wanted to trust that it would have fallen exactly so if I had never come.

Black cloud hid the sky. I lifted my face again, getting water in my eyes. For a moment, being thoroughly wet occupied all my attention.

A shallow puddle formed in a depression by my feet. My face appeared in it between the falling drops. Each ripple broke the reflection apart. It reformed, first an eye, then the line of my cheek, before another drop scattered it. I couldn't hold a clear view of myself for more than a heartbeat.

A raindrop hit the center of my reflection. My face dissolved and I looked away.

I stayed until the cold reached deep into my limbs. It had been a long time since I'd let weather affect me this much. When I bent my fingers, the chill ran along each joint.

Then I sat back down. Water dripped from my sleeves into a pool next to me. The scroll cylinder was dry in my pack.

You were still asleep. Your blanket had slipped and I pulled it back over your head without thinking about it.

The rain fell.

I sat there in my wet robe, thinking of the cultivators who'd asked these questions across so many thousands of years. They had stood in places like this one. They had kept records until they forgot.

Rain ran from my hair onto my hands. I returned to the question I'd been asking since the first scroll: if others had what I have and they all died, why was I still here?

I had the same qi, the same experiences. Strength alone was a poor explanation.

Luck?

I didn't believe in luck.

Then why?

I sat with the question, listening to your breathing under the rain. The question held. I repeated it once more, waiting for the headache or the blankness to follow.

Those writers had stopped halfway through their thoughts. I had reached the end of mine.

Not yet.
