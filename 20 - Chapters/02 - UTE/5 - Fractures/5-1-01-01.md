The shrine lay deep in a fold of the mountain, its broken roof barely visible above the slope. Vines choked whatever was left, thick as rope, cracking through stone that had once been carved with care. A strip of thinner mud between the trees was all that remained of the path.

You stopped at the base and looked at your shoes.

"These were new," you said.

I didn't answer. I was already climbing.

The air changed the moment I crossed the threshold. At the same temperature, the same height, a pressure gathered behind my ears. The qi here moved at an angle to the currents outside. It ran uphill under the still air.

I knelt by the entrance wall and pressed my palm flat against the stone. The surface was colder than autumn could account for, like bare metal in deep winter. Old fittings lined the doorframe, brackets for a gate long gone. Every one of them was cold as midwinter.

Chalk dust. I tasted it before I saw it: a grit between my teeth, fine and persistent, from walls that had been crumbling for centuries. I spat. It didn't help.

Under it all ran a smell of incense, musty and sweet with distant rot. I turned my head, looking for a source. The stone held no burner, ash, or smoke stain. The smell stayed.

I wiped dust from the nearest relief panel with the heel of my hand. Slow, methodical strokes. Moss came away in clumps, revealing carved lines. Geometric and precise, every shape repeated on purpose.

"You could use a brush," you said from behind me. You'd made it up, too. Your breathing was uneven, which you covered by adjusting your collar.

"Could." I kept scraping, my fingernails in the grooves. Some edges were worn smooth; others were still sharp. Whoever had carved this had gone over each line until its depth was exact, even in the awkward recesses. They had believed in what they were doing.

You wandered the perimeter while I worked. I heard your boots on broken tile, each careful step testing the surface before you committed your weight. You found a beam that had fallen at an angle and rested against the far wall. You must have leaned on it, because the wood groaned.

"Careful," I said. "This place is old."

"I'm being careful."

"The ceiling over you is held up by optimism."

You moved.

I worked the wall for most of half a watch. Layer by layer: dust, lichen, moss, and more dust. I recognized the symbols underneath, but too many were missing to make sense of them. At the edges, entire lines had worn away.

But the qi. The qi was interesting.

It flowed toward me. The natural channels in the stone ran east, following the mountain's spine, as water follows gravity. This undercurrent pulled south. Toward me. When I moved to the next panel, it adjusted. When I stepped outside to check the foundation, it followed.

That wasn't normal. Qi settles, pools, and flows according to geography. Spiritual density draws it, but I was keeping mine masked. It should have continued east.

It had an old feeling. Older than the shrine. The qi predated the stone it moved through, by centuries at least. Whatever had settled here had done so long before anyone thought to build walls around it.

I said nothing.

By noon, you had managed to cut your hand on a splintered beam. Not badly. A shallow slice across the back of your left hand, the kind that bleeds more than it should because the skin there is thin.

I eyed the cut. "You should wrap it."

I did. Clean cloth from your pack, wound tight, three loops and a tuck. I didn't look at your face while I did it. I'd been wrapping wounds for longer than this shrine had existed, longer than the mountain had borne its current name. Precise, practiced work. I checked the tension once more before letting go.

You studied the bandage, turning your hand over once.

"Thank you," you said.

I was already back at the wall.

The afternoon brought wind from the northwest instead of the usual east. It carried a smell of wet pine that didn't match the dry season. I added it to the list I'd begun three days ago, when a riverbed we'd crossed had been two paces wider than I remembered.

I wasn't keeping the list on paper. Not yet. Lists on paper can be found.

By evening, I'd cleared four panels. The carvings showed cultivation forms: body positions, qi flow indicators, and the visual shorthand that sects used before written manuals became standard. Old work. Pre-Consolidated Era, probably. The stone was the right age.

You built a fire in what might have been the shrine's inner courtyard. You set a pot over the flames and started cutting something that might have been a root vegetable into shapes that might have been intentional.

"Anything?" you asked.

"Nothing conclusive."

I was concealing plenty behind that one word. The qi flow was anomalous. The wind was wrong. I kept looking back at the carvings. Their arrangement was so reasonable that I almost knew where each line should go.

I sat across the fire from you while the flames settled into the wood. Chalk dust had collected in the lines of my palms, and the cold metal taste clung to the back of my throat.

You slid a bowl toward me. I ignored it for a while. Then I ate.

You watched me eat with a scholar's attention and said nothing. Your notebook sat by your knee, closed.

"You're different," you said.

"How."

"During the months in the crater. You were closed. Sealed. Like a door someone had nailed shut." You paused over the next words. "Now you're open. But it's worse."

I met your eyes.

"You're not grieving anymore," you said. "You're hunting."

I set down the bowl.

The fire popped. A spark rose and died. Somewhere in the dark outside the broken walls, an owl called, the two-note cry of a species that shouldn't have been this far south.

I added it to the list.
