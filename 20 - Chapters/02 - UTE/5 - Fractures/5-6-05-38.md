We stood in the clearing, five paces apart, the morning dew still cold under my feet. Something had happened. Your face held an expression I'd never seen you wear: defeat, guilt, and the careful blankness of a man reassembling himself in front of me. Your face told me everything I couldn't remember.

"What happened?" I asked.

You didn't answer immediately. You studied me as you studied damaged texts, searching for what survived, cataloguing what was missing.

"I asked you the question," you said. Your voice was level. Controlled. The control of a man describing a wound he'd inflicted. "I won't repeat it, because it might trigger the response again. Your face went still. Completely still. Like stone. The world—" You paused to search for the word. "The world went quiet. The wind stopped. The grass stopped moving. Everything just held."

A bird called somewhere past the clearing. The sound was sharp, distinct, each note landing with a clarity that was new, like a color seen for the first time.

"Then your eyes changed," you said. "Something looked through them. At me." You swallowed. "It saw me. All of me. And then, just as quickly—" You snapped your fingers. The sound was too loud. "Over. You blinked. You looked at me and asked me to go ahead with the question."

The wet grass under my feet was cold, each blade distinct against my skin. When had grass become something I felt so sharply?

"So you already asked it," I said.

"I did. You were asking me to do it again."

Silence.

"What do I remember?" I said, reaching for an answer before you could ask. Hope showed on your face, thin and fragile, the last thing that dies.

I reached inward. Past the headache, past the wall, past the smooth blank surface where experience should have been. There was nothing to grab. I searched for an image, a word, or a fragment of whatever had happened between your question and my forgetting.

But under the gap, deep and impossible to name, something hummed, like the warmth left in a chair after someone has stood up.

"Something happened," I said. "I can feel it. I can't tell you what it is. I can't even tell you what shape it has. But something—" I pressed my hand to my chest. The hum was there, under the ribs. Steady. "Something got through."

You stared at me with renewed attention, sharp enough to alter the defeated set of your face. You were too careful for hope, too disciplined to reach for something that might be taken. But you had expected total failure and were hearing something less final.

"Whatever you asked, whatever happened, it's gone. All of it. But there's a residue." I pressed harder. "It wasn't erased. This is still here. Whatever it is. It is humming in my chest. I don't know what it is, but it's something real. Something that survived."

The wind picked up gently, carrying the smell of pine, stone, damp earth, and grass. The light had softened since we'd arrived, the harsh glare settling into something warmer. At the clearing's edge, the mouse moved through the grass.

The world went on, detailed and present around me. I stood in it feeling things I hadn't felt before you asked your question.

"That's never happened before," I said. "The hurt stayed. The fear. But anything close to an answer was always gone. This time something survived."

You reached toward your pack for the notebook that wasn't there, still hidden in the crevice between the boulders. Your hands closed on nothing, then opened. You let them fall to your sides.

"Tea?" you asked. The word was quiet, practical, the single syllable of a man retreating into routine because routine was the only structure that held. You knelt by the small fire kit you'd set aside. Your hands found the motions of lighting a flame, warming water, and measuring leaves into the cup. The chipped ceramic cup, stained dark from years.

You handed it to me. The tea was warm. The ceramic heated my palms, the warmth specific and located, real as it had never been before.

I drank.

"I'm cold," I said.

I said it simply, absorbed in the cold against my skin.

You turned slowly, taking time to arrange a response to something that sounded impossible. Surprise crossed your face, then alarm, then something deeper I still have no word for, the expression of a man recalculating what he knew about a person on the strength of two words.

"You're cold," you repeated.

"Yes."

The breakthrough had loosened something. The wall still erased what I tried to remember with the same terrible efficiency, keeping the gap between me and whatever truth lay behind it. Yet warmth now reached me differently, as though that barrier had left something uncovered.

The wall did not protect sensation.

The feeling in my chest, the nameless frequency, the bass note the wall hadn't taken, came with a new sensory awareness I hadn't asked for and didn't know how to adjust to. The world had become more again. Louder. Sharper. More present.

The sensory sharpness from earlier, the clarity of fear when we entered the clearing, hadn't faded. It had deepened. On the way in, I'd felt grass underfoot and tracked a mouse. Now the dew on each blade was a different temperature from the air above it. Your breathing carried from five paces away, the rhythm too fast, too controlled. The tea in my hand smelled of its specific leaves, its specific water, and the minerals of the stream you'd drawn it from. My own skin smelled different. I smelled like a person: sweat, sleep, and the sourness of a body that had been running on fear.

Everything was more.

Every increase was uncomfortable. I had been shaped for less, my physical existence mediated by something that turned the world into abstraction before it reached me. Now the barrier was failing.

"The tea," I said. I drank more. The bitterness was vivid, each flavor distinct on my tongue, as if tasted for the first time. "It tastes—"

"Cold?"

"Not cold." I shook my head. "It tastes like everything."

Puzzlement crossed your face. You'd been a cultivator once; you knew how sharpened senses felt, the heightened awareness that came with qi flowing through open channels. But this was different. Something had stood between me and the world for longer than I could remember. Whatever erased my thoughts had also held sensation at a distance. Now everything reached me. For you, tea tasted like tea. For the me who had existed until three hundred heartbeats ago, tea had tasted like a category. Now it tasted like one event in one moment at one temperature, intimate enough to overwhelm me. The old knowledge of what tea was offered no shelter from this particular mouthful.

I sat down. The ground was cold, hard, and detailed. Every stone, root, and depression pressed into me with the piercing awareness of someone stripped of every barrier.

"I was—" I started.

The thought slipped, softly, like a word on the tip of the tongue sliding backward into the place where thoughts went when the wall decided they weren't allowed. The thought-echo, the residual vibration of whatever I'd been about to think, lingering in the space between intention and articulation.

"I was—"

Again. The same beginning. The same retreat. Whatever held the wall in place was quietly erasing the loose thoughts the breakthrough had scattered.

"I—"

Three echoes. Quieter than before. Aftershocks, fading.

I stopped trying to think. I held the cup. I let the cold in.

The cold was real. The cold was mine. It had reached me through the wall that still took my thoughts. Deliberately or accidentally, by design or by damage, the breakthrough had opened a channel between me and the physical world.

"Can I have your outer robe?" I asked.

You blinked, then removed it from your pack. The worn, practical, gray garment that served as both insulation and identity. You handed it to me without question.

I put it on. It held the warmth of your pack, unlike the distant heat my qi maintained inside me. Cloth lay against my skin, holding that small warmth close. Human warm. Real warm. The sort that comes from outside and has to be received rather than generated.

It smelled like you. Travel dirt and ink. The scent of a person who walked too far, slept too little, and documented everything.

I pulled the outer robe tighter.

The feeling in my chest pulsed. The nameless frequency. The thing that survived. It beat with the steady rhythm of something alive, something present, something that existed below the wall's jurisdiction and above the body's silent rhythms.

"Something changed," I said.

"I can see that."

"I don't know what."

"We'll figure it out."

Your face was still drawn with the effort of what you had done. I wondered whether you thought it right for wrong reasons, wrong for right reasons, or something that would take years to categorize. You watched me pull your robe tighter and offered no defense.

"Where's your notebook?" I asked.

You went still. The question had landed somewhere you hadn't prepared for.

"I put it somewhere safe," you said.

"Before we came here."

"Yes."

The implication settled between us. You had put the notebook somewhere safe *before* bringing me to the clearing. You had anticipated that whatever happened here might destroy things in reach. You had planned for the possibility that you wouldn't walk away, and made sure the record survived.

I said nothing. The feeling in my chest was evidence of something, the cold on my skin of something else. The hidden notebook told me more about your expectations than either.

"Go get it," I said. "Write everything down. What you saw, what I said, and what happened. Before you forget the details."

"I'm serious. You have the memory right now. I don't. If you don't write it down, nobody will know what happened here." I pulled the robe tighter. "Go. I'll wait."

You hesitated, reluctant to leave me alone in the clearing where the last few hundred heartbeats had changed so much. Then you nodded, turned, and walked toward the boulder field with the quick, purposeful stride of a man given permission to do the thing he wanted most.

I sat in the grass and waited. The dew soaked through the robe. The feeling hummed. The world was loud, sharp, and cold. I was alone with it.

You came back with the notebook. The waterproof case was dusty, the leather damp from the crevice. You sat down three paces from me, opened to a blank page, and wrote. Your hand moved fast, the handwriting urgent and pressing, a race against the softening of memory.

I sat in the cold and let you have the time.

I finished the tea and set down the cup. The ceramic was cold.

"Can we go back to camp?" I asked. The question was simple and real, with no subtext. I was cold. I wanted warmth. The fire at camp was warmth. The logic was mortal and basic, stripped of cultivation's elaborate relationship with comfort. I am cold, fire is warm, take me to the fire.

"Let's go," you said, and picked up your things. I picked up mine.

Something had changed. Something fundamental. Something irreversible.

The feeling in my chest hummed quietly, nameless, formless, and ancient as stone. It was steady beneath the cold.
