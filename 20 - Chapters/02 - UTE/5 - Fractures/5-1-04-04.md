The path leveled out under our feet where I remembered the beginning of a descent. We had reached a plateau. The elevation was similar, the vegetation matched, and we were still heading in the right direction. But I had expected a valley.

I remembered its walls, the shade under them, and the wind channeled between the slopes. Sound had carried differently there. On this open ground, your boots rang clearly next to mine.

I stood at the edge of what should have been a descent and looked at flat ground instead.

You stood next to me, breathing easily, doing nothing wrong. You had no memory of this place. For you, it was another stretch of land between where we'd been and where we were going. I kept looking toward the absent slopes. You looked straight ahead.

"Nice view," you said.

"Mm."

We crossed the plateau in silence. The afternoon sun was too warm for this altitude: another mark on the list. The air smelled of wild thyme crushed under our boots. A pleasant smell. A correct smell, actually. Thyme grew at this elevation. Some things were right.

I counted them because it helped. Right things, wrong things: two columns in a ledger in my head. The first was longer. I could name a dozen plants that belonged here, yet I still looked up between them for the walls of the valley.

Two days later, we reached the library.

It was exactly where I remembered it.

I stopped to check it against my memory again. After the shifted path and the missing valley, I had expected another discrepancy. Yet the library sat precisely where I'd seen it last. The walls enclosed the same courtyard. I could trace the main hall's old roofline against the sky.

The roof was gone, of course. Wooden beams rot long before stone walls fall. The gap was the right shape, with broken beam ends still lodged in the places I remembered. Centuries had passed here. I could account for what they'd done.

I stood at the entrance and tasted chalk, finer than the grit at the shrine. It settled powdery on my tongue, recalling the dust of pages too old to turn. The doorframe's metal fittings were cold: hinges, mostly, with a latch rusted past all use. Winter-cold.

And the incense. Fainter than at the shrine, but present. Musty. Sweet. Sourceless.

"Same smell," you said quietly. You'd noticed it at the shrine too. You opened your notebook to that entry, then looked around the doorway again. Finding it twice pleased you. Here was something you could compare, however little either of us understood it.

"Same smell," I agreed.

Inside, shelves had collapsed into heaps of gray wood. Cracked stone tablets lay scattered among them. Whatever texts had been stored here on perishable materials were gone. Paper doesn't survive centuries of weather. Neither do bamboo, silk, or skin.

Stone survives.

I spent two days there.

The first day was physical. I cleared debris, lifted fallen shelves, and catalogued what remained. I worked carefully. The wood was brittle, the stone under it uncertain. You helped. You were better at it than you would have been six months ago. Traveling had hardened your hands and softened your expectations of comfort. You lifted without complaining, sorted without asking why, and made careful notes before attempting to decipher anything.

We made camp in the courtyard. You cooked. Rice and dried vegetables, simple, competent. You'd picked up herbs along the road. I hadn't noticed, which meant you'd done it while I was walking ahead, lost in my own calculations. The food was adequate. Better than adequate. I didn't tell you that.

"The shrine had qi anomalies," you said over dinner. "This place?"

"Different."

"Different how?"

I considered the answer, my bowl balanced on my knees. The library's qi lay in strata of different ages, laid down over centuries like sediment. Most was ordinary residue from cultivators who'd studied or meditated in these rooms. I had felt their traces while lifting the fallen shelves.

Under them, pressed deep into the foundation stones, lay older qi. Much older. It predated the building, even the hill.

Similar to the shrine.

Similar to mine.

"The qi here predates the structure," I said. "Same as the shrine."

You set down your chopsticks. Carefully. "By how much?"

"Significantly."

"Can you be more exact?"

"At least twelve hundred years."

You picked up your chopsticks, then set them down again. "The qi was here before the library was built."

"Yes."

"As if the library was built on top of it."

"Or as if the qi was placed here and the library grew around it later. Like a pearl grows around grit."

"Placed." You tasted the word. "By whom?"

I ate a piece of radish. It was overcooked. "That's the question."

The second day was worse.

I found inscriptions on the foundation stones, etched permanently into the rock by qi. The characters belonged to a script that had been dead for longer than most civilizations had existed. I could read them.

One character stood apart from the rest, cut deeper. Its wider strokes had cracked the surrounding grain. A single word, written in the first person:

*Me.*

My qi surged in an involuntary pulse from my dantian. It spread through my palms into the stone, which hummed under them. The vibration was low and persistent. I knew that resonance.

I pulled my hands away and held them clear of the floor. The problem from the shrine had followed me here. I could read a dead language I had never studied, recognize patterns I had never been taught, and follow logic I had never encountered. I understood each line as soon as I uncovered it. I could remember none of the work that should have made that possible.

The inscriptions were fragments of observations. A researcher had recorded qi flow deviations, spatial inconsistencies, and things that failed to match their maps or memories.

The list I was building.

Someone had been here centuries ago, doing exactly what I was doing. They had measured the wrongness, catalogued the gaps, and tried to find a pattern.

They must have built this library to house their research. I pictured them standing where I stood, with chalk dust on their tongue. Had the fittings been cold under their palms too? Had they smelled incense? They had certainly asked my questions.

I sat on the floor of the main hall and pressed my palms against the stone. The inscriptions vibrated under my skin. Qi had settled so deeply into the rock that I couldn't tell one from the other.

Who were you?

The cut lines stayed under my fingers. Whatever I made of them, they would keep the same shape. I wanted that kind of certainty. I moved my hand to the next inscription.

The inscriptions continued down the walls, into the floor, and under the rubble. I couldn't have catalogued them in seven days. A lifetime's work, compressed into stone, left here by someone who had been searching, as I was.

Unlike me, they had stopped.

The inscriptions ended abruptly, halfway through an observation. Its final character was incomplete. Whatever had interrupted the writer, they had never returned to finish that stroke.

I stared at the final character. Half-formed. The brush-equivalent of a voice cut short.

"What happened to them?" you asked. You were reading over my shoulder. You couldn't parse the script, but you could read my face and whatever was on it had brought you close.

"I don't know."

"But you can guess."

I could. I didn't want to.

The chalk dust settled deeper between my teeth. The cold metal fittings caught the light from the doorframe, dull and still.

I stood up.

"We need to find more sites," I said. "As many as we can."
