Over our morning tea, you told me you wanted to explore the terrain. A practical, mundane task requiring solitude, clear sightlines, and the absence of a traveling companion who might accidentally rearrange the geography.

I didn't argue. The headache was bad enough that solitude sounded like medicine.

Your notes told me later what you found: an open clearing, about two hundred paces across. You had chosen it with the field's two-hundred-pace reach in mind. You thought in terms of destruction, like a man who had watched reality break several times and was now preparing to break it once more, on purpose.

No mountain faces rose nearby. You walked the perimeter, evaluated the surrounding terrain, and checked for any large geological feature in reach that might vanish if I was pushed past my limits. The missing flank was still visible on the horizon. You looked at that smooth absence where rock used to be as you planned. Your notes say it made you hate the plan even more, and made you more certain it was necessary.

The clearing had only a few trees, all of them small. Saplings, young growth, flexible wood that would bend without shattering. You were calculating tolerable cost. Tolerable cost. For a man who documented butterflies.

You wrapped your notebook in its waterproof case and wedged it deep into a crevice between two boulders, far enough in to survive whatever came. If it went wrong, which was a category you'd promoted from unlikely to probable, the documentation would survive. Someone would find it. Someone would know. Someone would read it and it wouldn't stop mid-word.

Then you came back with the casual stride of a man who had spent a morning walking, with nothing more significant to report than the quality of the terrain and the direction of the wind. You sat down. You boiled water and made tea.

"Wei would have liked this place," you said.

I flinched. The muscles of my torso contracted around the ache Wei's name made, the bruise that never healed. Healing it would mean accepting it, and acceptance meant something I wasn't ready for.

The pain was sharp. Clear. Present.

And then, thirty heartbeats later, I couldn't remember what you'd said.

You used his name as a key.

The sentence was gone: the words, the syntax, and the casual delivery. All erased. Whatever stood behind the wall had removed it with clean precision. I knew the aftermath all too well, but I couldn't learn to prevent it.

But the pain was still there.

I rubbed my chest, hand moving to the site of impact without my deciding it, pressing as if pressure could locate the source. The pain had no address. It existed without its cause, an echo without a sound.

"Something wrong?" you asked.

I looked at you. You watched me with a naturalist's attentive stillness, needing to see this, needing to confirm it, needing to know your theory was correct before you committed to the consequences.

"I don't know," I said. "Something— I feel—"

I couldn't finish the sentence. Pain in my chest, with no cause I could retrieve. The disconnection was maddening. I wanted to press my temples, breathe, and push against the wall until something gave.

"You created the field," you said next.

Fear. Immediate, visceral, spiking through my body like ice water. Fear of what? The words registered, began to mean something, and were gone. Faster this time. I barely registered them leaving.

But the fear didn't leave.

I couldn't retrieve the sentence, reconstruct it, or even remember that you'd spoken. But I was afraid. Suddenly, completely, groundlessly. The fear of a person who knows something terrible is true and can't remember what the terrible thing is.

"I—" My hands were shaking. "Why am I afraid?"

Your face showed how much it cost you to sit there and watch, though I couldn't tell why at the moment. You watched me feel things I couldn't understand, the disconnection between cause and effect playing out in real time on a person you had chosen to walk beside.

"The mountain," you said. "Turn around."

I turned.

On the horizon stood the mountain with the missing flank: the smooth absence where I remembered rock. From here I pictured the flowers at its edge, the geometry of nothing where something had been.

I stood, hands clenched. A deep rumble ran through the ground under me. I stared at the missing flank, a sick pressure under my ribs, trying to hold on to the connection between that empty space and myself. There had been something I knew about it.

The thought dissolved. The cause was taken.

What was left was the feeling. Guilt. Responsibility. The weight of having done something that cannot be undone, pressing on the inside of my chest like a hand.

I sat down. Slowly. The vibration stopped. The world settled.

You sat a few careful paces away, at the safety distance that had become our default. Your breathing was too controlled. You were running a deliberate test on someone who trusted you, your face drawn as you watched the results.

"Three for three," you said. Quiet. To yourself.

"What?"

"Nothing." You looked at the ground. "I think I understand now. How the forgetting works."

"Tell me."

"I could, but you'll forget the explanation."

The absurdity broke through. The circular trap, knowing that knowing was useless because knowing would be taken, made me laugh, a dark, exhausted, humorless laugh. It wasn't funny. It was the most terrifying thing you could have said. I laughed because the alternative was silence and fear.

"Then what?" I asked.

You turned to the mountain. To the absence, the evidence of what I could do when something cut deep enough.

"I have to make you feel something so deeply that the feeling alone, even without content, or any explanation, would be enough." You paused. "I have to reach the part of you that the forgetting can't touch."

I should have heard the warning in that, the timbre of a man describing a plan he despised, outlining an act of necessary cruelty with the detachment of someone already committed.

I didn't hear it. I heard a man with a plan. And plans were the one thing I didn't have.

"When?" I asked.

"Tomorrow."

You stood, walked to the fire, and cooked dinner. Your hands were steady through visible effort: the set of your jaw, the hardness of your shoulders. For the rest of the evening you didn't look at me.

I ate. The headache pulsed.

I didn't sleep.

Tomorrow came.
