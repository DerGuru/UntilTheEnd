Four days later, three visitors approached our campfire. Two men flanked a woman in muted gray cultivator robes. None wore a sect color. They slowed together at the edge of the light, keeping their hands visible. Their cultivation was middling, a notch higher at most. Most people would have taken them seriously.

I did not rise.

Their talismans gave them away before they spoke. Three polished jade discs hung at even intervals from each belt. Gathered qi pulsed behind their faint iridescence in the firelight. Resonance Talismans. The Pencil had sent them.

"We come on behalf of our master," the woman said. The other two left her room to speak. "We seek conversation, not conflict."

She was good. Her voice held a practiced warmth, with just enough formality to acknowledge who she was addressing. I could have trusted it easily, had I ignored the talismans.

I let her wait.

You set your pen down next to the open notebook. The page faced all three visitors. I suspected that was deliberate. Whatever they said next, they could see where it would be recorded.

"Your master," you said. "The Quiet One."

The woman's expression held steady. The man on the left shifted his weight toward the trees. The man on the right touched his talisman.

"Lian Zhuo," the woman said, placing careful emphasis on the name.

"He fused the factions of the Nine Rivers Sect," you said. "We've heard."

"Then you know he is a scholar. Like yourself." She nodded at the notebook. "He seeks understanding, not dominion."

"Everyone who seeks dominion calls it understanding first," I said.

Her brown eyes met mine with the same warmth. Her qi quivered under that composure. She held both hands still beside her belt.

"Our master proposes cooperation," she said. "The anomalies are affecting the entire region. Cultivators are losing control of their foundations. Villages are being evacuated. The spiritual balance is collapsing." She paused. "He believes you can help stabilize it."

"Because he believes I'm causing it."

Silence.

The fire crackled. Past its light, a night bird called. Another species outside its range. I was tired of noting them.

"Our master," the woman said carefully, "has observed a correlation between exact qi signatures and the anomaly patterns. He does not presume to assign causation."

"He presumes. He's just polite about it."

She had the grace not to deny it.

"The offer is genuine," she said. "Resources. Information. Access to research that our master—" She paused. "That Lian Zhuo has compiled over the past two years. The anomaly records are extensive. He has compared them thoroughly."

"And in exchange?"

"Cooperation. Your presence. Your... perspective."

Your perspective. The discs on her belt caught the firelight. With me nearby, their talismans could gather whatever each new disturbance released. The records might be real. So was the benefit of keeping me where the Pencil could place his collectors.

I stood.

The woman's hand drifted toward her talisman. Both men widened their stances. Their qi tightened as I rose.

I gathered my belongings, walked past the fire, and left the three cultivators standing there. Past the light, I took the path toward the stream where I'd washed dishes half a watch earlier.

They watched me go.

Your voice carried from the camp. "That was rude."

"That was honest," I said, without turning back.

After a pause, the woman spoke as evenly as before. "We'll be in the area. The offer stands."

Three sets of footsteps moved away together. As they faded, insects, wind, and water moving over the streambed returned.

You found me at the stream.

"They knew where we were," you said.

"Yes."

"How?"

"Their talismans. They are tuned to the frequency of the anomalies. They can track the disturbances. They can follow the trail to its source."

You sat on a rock by the stream. Water murmured over the stones below us, filling the pauses between our words.

"Other people can see it," you said. "What you are. What you do. Lian Zhuo has been watching. Studying. He has records that we don't. Two years of observations to compare." You paused. "That might be useful. Or it might be a trap."

"It's definitely a trap."

"That doesn't mean the records aren't real."

I glanced over. You were watching the water. The stream caught starlight and broke it into moving shapes on your face.

"They'll come back," I said.

"Yes."

"They'll bring more next time. Different arguments. Better bait."

"Probably."

"And eventually they'll stop asking."

You picked up a pebble, turned it in your fingers, and set it down.

"We'd better move," I said.

"You know, you say a lot of decisive things. Acting on the ones that cost you something is where you struggle."

I looked down at the pebble before you could see me almost smile.

We packed in darkness and moved south by moonlight. I chose gaps between trees or stretches of rock where we could pass without leaving tracks. The sect's maps would show no trails there. I'd walked those routes years or decades earlier, when avoiding attention had been a habit I could afford to neglect.

A thin column of smoke still rose from the abandoned fire. As we walked, the talismans' residual hum faded until it blended into the surrounding qi. The deep pulse in me went on.

I expected the Pencil to send more people with better offers and equally polite language. The demand under it would not change: let us use you.

Eventually he might come himself. I wanted to see the academic who spoke like a teacher and had united his factions at the cost of three elders' lives. His messengers had approached carefully. I expected their master to be more precise about what he wanted.

I was looking forward to it. That might have been the wrong response. But I could not help myself.

"When they come back," you said again, half a step behind me.

"We'll deal with them."

You followed without asking where I was leading. Your notebook was tucked under your arm. When I turned between the next two trees, your footsteps turned after mine.

The moon set. We walked on.
