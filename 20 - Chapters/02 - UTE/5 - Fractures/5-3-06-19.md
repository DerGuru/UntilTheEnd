We lengthened our stride after leaving the camp. Each morning we packed quickly; at each rest, I looked for the next stretch of open ground. Staying in one place gave the disturbances time to gather around us. It had already brought one delegation to our fire.

For three days we walked hard, south and west into hill country. Ridges cut off the view after each turn. More than once, a promising trail ended against a cliff face. I chose routes that kept us out of sight, accepting the slower crossings between them.

Good country for hiding. Bad country for research.

I hadn't touched a ruin in days. I missed the work, despite the unease that came with the warmth under a foundation.

The changes continued. I found a tree growing well above its usual range, then a stream bending away from the course I remembered. At night, I checked the same uncertain star. Its position might have shifted naturally over the years. I looked anyway, unable to settle the question.

The headaches had acquired a rhythm. They came in the mornings, then again before sleep, even when I was occupied with something ordinary. The low pressure behind my eyes persisted long enough that I began choosing when to read by the brief stretches of relief.

I rubbed my temples. You noticed. You always noticed.

"How long?" you asked.

"Since the library."

"Every day?"

"Most days."

"Have they changed?"

I considered it, fingers against my temples. The early pain had struck sharply when I approached certain thoughts. I could usually ease it by turning to something else. Now a dull weight spread behind both eyes. It lasted through the walk and shifted whenever I bent my head.

"They're broader," I said. "Less exact."

"That means they're either getting worse or you're getting used to them."

"Or both."

We reached a small town at the edge of the hills. North-south routes crossed the east-west road at a bridge over a narrow, forceful river. Buildings clustered around the crossing, plain enough to suit the practical people using them. The tea house had two rooms under a sagging roof. Its tea wasn't terrible.

I sat by the window while you ordered. Merchants packed up their stalls outside. Children ran through the diminishing light, and a sect courier hurried past with dispatches. I wondered whether he carried more of the frightened reports we'd been hearing for so many days.

The stalls still had to be packed away. People needed food, roofs, and places to sleep whether the qi currents ran properly or the Pencil had found some use for their disorder. A merchant finished closing his stall and turned back to the street.

I watched the children play.

"You're staring at the children," you said.

"Am I?"

"Long enough for your tea to stop steaming."

I hadn't noticed the time passing. The children kicked a ball between them with more enthusiasm than skill. They shouted over each other. When one fell, the child scrambled up to chase the ball again.

"They're loud," I said.

"They're children."

"Loud children."

You set a cup in front of me. A hairline crack ran halfway down from the rim. A good potter would probably have discarded it. Here, the tea stayed inside, which was apparently enough.

I traced the crack with my finger. It was barely deep enough to catch my nail, yet my eye returned to it every time. I turned the damaged side away from my mouth before drinking.

"The talismans tracked us once," you said. "They'll do it again. Moving faster doesn't help. It may have bought some time, but that is all it did."

I eyed you over the rim of the cracked cup.

"So, what do you suggest?"

"Stop running. Start planning." You opened your notebook to the anomaly map. I'd seen the numbered markers along our route before. Now I followed the lines between them: southeast, then southwest, each leg shorter than the last. The narrowing zigzag was visible even at arm's length.

"We're converging," you said. "Look."

I set the cup down. I had chosen each direction because the next site felt as though it lay south. On paper, those choices formed a narrowing pattern. My eyes followed the east-west swings.

I had checked paths, winds, and stars against my memory. Somehow I had failed to check my own route. Here it was, drawing closer to a destination I hadn't chosen by name.

The headache pulsed once.

"Where does it converge?" I asked.

You traced the lines with your finger, extending the pattern beyond the last marked point. Your finger moved south, the zigzag flattening, narrowing, until it came to rest on a spot you'd marked with a small X.

"Here," you said. "About four days from now, at the current pace."

"What's there?"

"According to the maps? Nothing. A mountain range. Uninhabited. There are no sects, villages, or recorded structures."

"That holds true according to my memory too."

You looked up.

I stared at the X. I could name no feature there, yet my chest tightened as I followed the marked ridge.

"But I've been there," I said.

"When?"

"I don't remember."

You waited.

"But I've been there and I don't remember what's there, which is—" Pressure surged behind my eyes. The light through the window hurt. Each shout from the children struck sharply inside my skull.

I pressed my hands against my temples and closed my eyes. Red spread behind my eyelids.

Enough.

The word came as readily as the others had. Not now. Keep going. Each time, I stopped following the thought I'd almost reached. I held the X in mind, eyes closed.

Enough.

The pressure eased. The red faded. The children's voices returned to normal.

I opened my eyes. You were watching me, one hand still next to the map. You left it there when I looked down.

"The headaches are connected," you said. Not a question.

"They're a symptom."

"Of what?"

I pictured the unfinished inscriptions. Those researchers had persisted through years of work, making careful records right up to the final stroke. Had they felt this same pressure? I rubbed my temple once more. I had almost lost the question while trying to answer you.

"Of whatever those cultivators had. Whatever I have," I said.

You closed the notebook, finished your tea, and stood.

"Four days," you said. "We have four days to decide whether we walk to whatever's at the end of that line or turn around."

I looked out the window. The children had stopped playing. The ball sat in the dust, abandoned, perfectly still. Past the town, the hills rose in layers of green and the darkening blue of approaching dusk.

"We don't turn around," I said.

You nodded without opening the map again, as if you'd expected that answer.

We left the tea house. The cracked cup sat on the table, empty, its fracture catching the last light.
