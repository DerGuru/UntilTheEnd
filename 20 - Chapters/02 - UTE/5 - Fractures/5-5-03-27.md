You handed me the rice bowl at dinner with a pause so slight I almost missed it. You caught yourself, completing the gesture. The bowl changed hands. But the pause had been there.

"You're afraid," I said.

You ate, chewing slowly, composing a response.

"Yes."

"Since when?"

"Since the trees." You set your bowl down. "The rain, I could stand in that. Standing in the rain is not comfortable, but it is endurable." You looked at the fire. "Splitting three trees from the inside with a thought is not."

I'd split three trees with anger I hadn't aimed. I'd made it rain from a clear sky, then made the earth move because I was frustrated about scrolls.

"If it helps, I'm afraid of me too," I said.

You looked up. Something shifted in your expression, as if my admission had changed a calculation from "doesn't know" to "knows and it doesn't help."

Neither of us slept well that night, so we broke camp before dawn. You made tea in the dark, working by feel, by routine, while I fed a thread of qi into the kettle to heat the water. Your core couldn't sustain a flame anymore. Mine could sustain a sun. The least it could do was boil water. No jasmine left. Just plain leaves from a pouch that was running low. The tea tasted like hot water. We drank it anyway, crouched by the dead fire in the pre-dawn cold.

Neither of us spoke. A bird started up somewhere in the canopy. Three tentative notes, a pause, then the same three notes again. Its small, uneven song carried clearly through the still air.

Moving was better than sitting.

The trail should have taken us south. We'd studied your map, annotated in your careful hand with the route changes the world kept making. South, toward the river valley where other people lived.

But the trail didn't go south.

It had, yesterday. South, clearly marked, cutting between two ridges toward the valley floor. This morning, the trail went east. Into the mountains. Toward higher ground, thinner air, less life, less buffer, more isolation.

"The path changed," you sighed. Flat. You were past surprise, in the territory where anomaly was simply the operating condition.

I stared at the trail. East, into the mountains, into the place where my emotions would have fewer things to break and more empty space to fill.

I hadn't wanted to go east. I hadn't consciously redirected anything. But the part of me that operated without consultation had decided that proximity to people was a variable to be minimized.

"We go south," I said. "Off-trail."

We went south, off-trail, through undergrowth nobody had walked, over terrain nobody had crossed, around obstacles nobody had bothered to clear because nobody had ever needed this route.

Half a watch later, we found a trail.

Going east.

You studied the trail, then me.

"You're doing this," you said.

"I'm not— I don't want to go east."

"What you want and what's happening appear to be different categories."

You were right. The gap between my conscious intention and the world's response was the thing that scared me most. I could live with being powerful. Being out of control, a force acting by rules I couldn't read, sat in my stomach like cold iron.

We tried south three more times. Each time, the terrain funneled us gradually east, with obstacles ordinary enough to make the detour reasonable. South grew increasingly difficult. A ravine here. A landslide there. A thicket of thorn-brush so dense that cutting through without qi would take a watch or more.

By afternoon, we were going east.

I hadn't decided to go east. Yet here we were. The part of me that was becoming the world had decided for both of us.

"The sect," I said. We'd stopped at a stream. The water was cold. It tasted of minerals and the metallic aftertaste mountain water carries out of old stone. "They've been tracking me. They'll know where we are."

"Yes."

"And they'll follow."

"Also yes."

You sat on a flat rock and pulled off your boots. Your feet were blistered from the rough terrain, your body running on endurance rather than cultivation. You examined the damage with the clinical detachment of a man cataloguing data. You took a small pouch from your pack: mineral powder that traveling merchants sold at crossroads for a few copper. You dusted it over the raw skin, hissing once through your teeth. Then you wrapped strips of cloth around the worst spots with the efficiency of someone who had done this before and expected to again.

"Let me," I said.

You looked up.

"The blisters. A thread of qi. Ten heartbeats."

You considered, longer than you should have, weighing what accepting would cost the self-sufficiency you'd built over months of walking behind someone who could sustain a sun while you couldn't light a candle.

Finally you nodded. "Okay. Thank you."

I knelt to place two fingers against the sole of your left foot. The qi moved in a narrow, precise thread, the smallest application I could manage. Heat without burning. The blistered skin smoothed as the fluid reabsorbed, the raw tissue closing over like water settling after a stone. Your right foot came next. Ten heartbeats each.

You flexed your toes, tested your weight on each foot, and put the boots back on.

"Thank you," you said quietly, the tone of someone who has accepted something and is already recalculating the balance.

I drank. The water was clean, yet its taste was off, distant. Things had tasted like that since the night I'd forgotten.

"We could separate," I said.

You refilled your flask, capped it, and secured it.

"No," you said.

"You'd be safer."

"Probably."

"You'd be invisible. Mortal. Nobody would—"

"No!" You didn't raise your voice, but the statement was absolute.

I rubbed my temples.

"Let's move on. We are going east, apparently." The corner of your mouth did something that might have been humor if it hadn't been so thin.

We walked east.

Later that afternoon, we passed a meadow. Small, sheltered, an accidental clearing where a stream had carved a bed wide enough to let sunlight reach the ground. Blue and white wildflowers grew close to the soil, blooming even at this altitude. A butterfly worked from blossom to blossom.

You stopped. You studied the meadow, the flowers, then me.

"What?" I said.

"Nothing." You adjusted your pack. "It's just flowers."

Flowers. My unconscious mind was reshaping this mountain range for purposes I couldn't identify. A sect of cultivators tracked my emotional outbursts through resonance talismans, and whatever kept me alive was actively preventing me from understanding it. In the middle of all that, flowers.

They were very blue.

We walked on.

Higher in the mountains, the air thinned. The vegetation thinned. Everything felt unreal, as if the landscape were a draft that hadn't been finalized.

I walked. You walked behind me.

Your footsteps sounded different. Lighter. Ready.
