I lifted a stone cylinder from a recess behind a fallen shelf. Its wax cap had held through centuries. Someone had placed it above the waterline that had ruined the neighboring scrolls, taking care to shelter it deep inside the wall. They had wanted this one to survive.

I broke the wax with my thumb. The cylinder opened with a dry click. Inside, stiff yellowed paper held ink faded almost to the same brown. The strokes were still legible. Whoever had chosen that ink had chosen well.

I held it to the light beam.

The character forms placed the script about three thousand years ago, before Consolidation. Later standardization had made cultivation scholarship accessible and, in the same stroke, boring. Here the writer had crossed out words and crowded additions between the lines. Their changes of mind were easy to trace.

The structure was unusual. Most of the text documented another person's condition through dates, locations, and behavioral notes. Indented passages used the first person, with a slightly different pressure of the brush. The observer had recorded the subject's own words.

Dictation, probably. A cultivator of that era might have had a scribe. A companion.

I read the indented sections first.

*My qi obeyed my will, not my technique.*

I read it twice. Three times. The words didn't change.

*I shaped the flow before I understood the form. My hands moved to positions I had never studied. I completed whole sequences without knowing how I had learned them. The qi flowed as soon as I intended it to move. I could direct it before taking a stance or beginning a breathing pattern.*

Three thousand years. A different cultivator, with another name and another life. Yet I recognized the experience. Holding that paper, I remembered how easily my arms had lifted into the first form at the shrine.

I set the scroll down. My hands were steady. My pulse was not.

In the other room, you were sorting rubble. Stones clicked into place, each scrape followed by a pause while you checked what lay underneath. You hummed, as you did when concentrating, with scarcely a tune in it. It was pleasant.

I picked up the scroll again.

The rest was fragmented. The writer described qi departing from known patterns or moving before the cultivator directed it. They had also recorded changes in the surrounding terrain. Distances failed to match earlier measurements. In different language, the account described what I'd been documenting for more than twenty-one days.

I checked those passages against the earlier entries.

I read until the light shifted and the beam moved off my hands. Then I carried the scroll to the entrance where the daylight was stronger and read more.

You appeared with rice. You'd gone outside, built a fire I hadn't noticed, and cooked a meal I hadn't smelled. You set the bowl next to me. I kept reading. You went back inside without speaking.

After a few more lines, I reached for the bowl.

The rice was good, salted correctly. You'd found wild garlic somewhere. Its sharp green taste made me pause over the first mouthful. Then I went back to reading, holding the scroll in one hand while I ate with the other. Against all odds, I was being fed competently in a ruined library.

I set the empty bowl down to pick up the scroll's second section.

Here the cultivator had documented consequences. Buildings had cracked along the lines of qi deviations. Small shifts in the landscape had added up until rivers changed course. I followed one account from the first measurement to the damage recorded below it.

And then: a passage that stopped my breathing.

*I attempted to end my cultivation. My body refused. I sat in meditation for forty days, willing my core to dissipate. On the forty-first day I woke as if from sleep, with my core not only intact but stronger. As if whatever sustains me does not care about my choices. As if it has its own will and that will is my continuance.*

My qi. My will. Not my technique.

He had tried to stop. Through core dissolution, he had attempted to unmake everything that separated a cultivator from a mortal. He should have come out of those forty days weakened, if not dying. Instead, his core had grown stronger. His decision had altered nothing.

I knew exactly what that felt like.

I set the scroll on the ground and stared at my hands. Chalk dust filled their creases. Stone grit packed the nails. The skin under the dirt was smooth; cultivators past a certain stage don't wrinkle. I turned my palms upward, trying to imagine them old enough to show how long I'd used them. They looked as they always had.

"Found something?" you said from the doorway.

"Several somethings."

You sat cross-legged across from me with your notebook ready. You brushed dust from your knee before resting the open book there.

"Three thousand years old," I said. "A cultivator who experienced what I experience. His qi obeyed his will, not his technique. He tried to die. His body refused."

Your pen stopped moving.

"And?" you said.

"Same fate. He's dead."

The pen resumed. Slower.

"So he eventually succeeded?"

"No." I picked up the scroll to turn it over. The reverse was blank. Like the inscriptions, this account simply stopped. "He didn't die by choice. He died because—" I looked at the last line again. "I don't know. The scroll ends."

"Same as the inscriptions."

"All of them."

You wrote. Fresh ink darkened under your pen. Months later, I would still be tracing the connections you recorded here, with a bowl between us on the dusty ground.

"How many is that?" you asked. "Total. Including the inscriptions at the ruins."

"Eight. Different periods. Different cultivators. Identical phenomenon. Identical ending."

"Same qi signature underneath?"

"Yes."

You wrote down my answer with the same steady pressure as the date. Then you drew a line back to an earlier entry. The pen moved. I waited for another question.

You let the silence sit. You had learned when to put down your pen.

"Eat some more," you said and pushed the pot toward me.

I didn't eat more. But I went in looking for more.
