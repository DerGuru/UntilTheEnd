Three days later, we began hearing the name Quiet One at roadside tea houses. Speakers lowered their voices when they reached it, glancing toward other tables before continuing. It took several conversations to assemble an account.

An academic had reunited the three fractured factions of the Nine Rivers Sect: the power-hungry, the researchers, and the traditionalists. For most of a decade they had competed for resources, weakening one another with each success. Now they served one purpose again. By every account, their new leader gave orders as quietly as a teacher correcting a pupil.

I called him the Pencil. A traveling physician who'd treated one of his followers for a qi-burn supplied the first description: "He's thin, angular, and always writing. He writes more than he speaks." It gave me a writing instrument to picture until I could put a face to him.

The Pencil's method was Resonance Talismans.

I knew the craft. Centuries ago, I'd seen a damaged, looted talisman tucked among supplies in a campsite cache. I hadn't paid it much attention. Such work predated Consolidation. Collectors sometimes traded surviving pieces from ruins without understanding what they held.

But the Pencil understood.

Resonance Talismans collect energy from their surroundings. Properly tuned, one will draw in a particular kind of qi wherever it is present. The maker has to know what the place contains. In ordinary terrain, a talisman prepared for rare, unstable currents would gather next to nothing.

The anomalies were producing those currents in abundance. Backward flows released raw qi, as did the shifts in terrain. Ordinary cultivators could make little use of it. The Pencil had recognized what his talismans could gather from the very places other people were fleeing.

He was harvesting my waste.

I looked down at my hands on the table. The disturbances I'd caused were supplying his faction. Every ruin I investigated brought more changes; his talismans gathered the energy released along their course. Until now, I hadn't even known to look for the collectors.

I was feeding them.

"The fusion had a cost," the physician told us. We were in a small town's only inn, sharing a table with someone who was too tired to be careful about what he said. "Three of the traditionalist elders didn't survive it. Some kind of ritual. I don't have details. But the Quiet One needed everyone committed. Permanently. The ones who couldn't commit..."

He trailed off, drank his wine, and looked down at the table.

"Permanently," you echoed.

"Permanently."

The next morning, you found the page.

I don't know how. I'd kept the folded paper against my skin, flat inside the inner fold of my robe. Someone would have had to search deliberately for it. Someone who knew a page was missing from a certain notebook.

You had already warned me about the missing page after we left the library. Now you had gone looking for proof you could hold in your hand.

I came back from washing at the stream to find my robe hanging on the low branch exactly as I'd left it, with no sign that anyone had touched it. My pack was in the same position. My boots were where I'd placed them.

Your shoulders were set differently when I returned. You met my eyes, chin level, and closed the notebook. You were more certain of yourself than before I went to the stream.

Your questions grew more precise. You returned to things I'd already told you, asking for a location or the order of two events. I began to suspect you were checking conclusions of your own.

I didn't answer.

You let the silence stand. Once, you would have filled it with another question. Now you watched where I looked, then turned back to your notes. You'd begun paying as much attention to what I left unanswered as to what I said.

"You're looking at me differently," I said on the next day.

"Am I?"

You adjusted your pack strap, then walked three steps. "Maybe you're standing differently."

I said nothing. We walked south between fields planted with the crops I expected for the season. They had reached just the right stage of growth. The air smelled of turned earth and drying grain.

I searched for an irregularity in those fields. Everything fitted so closely that I mistrusted it. The disturbances could straighten a path as readily as bend it. A place might emerge looking more orderly than it had before, with the roughness I remembered gone.

You wrote in your notebook that night, across the fire from me. You checked earlier pages, added a few lines, and returned to the new entry. You were connecting what you'd already observed.

You'd read the page. I was certain now. You knew what the fourteen anomalies added up to. You knew the correlation with our route. You knew what the qi signatures meant.

Yet you weren't running.

My fingers pressed against my palm. Eleven researchers had reached toward similar conclusions. Every record had stopped. I looked at your moving pen, remembering the final unfinished character in the cave.

You were approaching.

"You should stop writing," I said.

You looked up. The fire between us popped.

"Why?"

"Comprehensive records of anomaly patterns have a poor survival rate."

You set down your pen, held my gaze, and picked it up again.

"I'm not them," you said quietly.

I looked away first.

You went back to writing. You left the statement there.

I watched the fire burn low and thought of eleven unfinished records. Across from me, you continued yours. The folded page still rested against my skin. You had read it, then put it back exactly where I'd hidden it. Neither of us mentioned that. My hands stayed in plain view, clear of the pocket.

The fire died. The full moon was high. Your pen moved on.
