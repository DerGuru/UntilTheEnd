You were still writing when I stood up. My legs were stiff from sitting, my soles tender from the walk. The robe shifted around me, the hem dragging against the ground.

"I'll get some more firewood," I said.

You looked up. Your pen stopped mid-stroke. I thought you'd insist on coming, insist I stay, or find some reason to keep me in arm's reach where you could monitor what you'd done. But you just nodded and went back to writing.

The forest around camp I'd walked through for days without registering much. Now patches of dense undergrowth followed a logic of light and soil I'd never attended to. Small, pale mushrooms clustered on a fallen trunk. A beetle crossed a patch of moss. The damp moss yielded under my feet so differently from dirt, stone, or pine needles that it was a separate category of ground.

I picked up branches as I found them. Dry, light, the bark peeling in strips. Each one had a weight, a texture, a temperature, and a smell. Each of those properties arrived simultaneously, which was going to be a problem.

At first it was manageable. Trees. Ground. Air. Then a gust of wind brought resin, earth, water, and the sweetness from uphill. The sun moved behind a cloud, and the light changed. A bird called from the left. The branch in my hand was rough under my fingers. The ground was cold under my feet. The robe was slipping off my shoulder. All of it arrived in the same instant, each sensation as sharp as the others. I couldn't decide which to attend to. Everything was equally important, and that was too much.

I stopped, closed my eyes, and breathed. The sensations continued, but without the shifting light I could begin to separate them. I didn't have to take in the whole forest at once.

With my eyes closed, it was better. Fewer sensations. Only sound, touch, and smell, without the relentless flood of visual detail. I stood there with my arms full of branches and sorted.

The wind was constant. I could let it pass.

The ground was cold. I could stand on it anyway.

The smell had complexity. But it was not urgent. Background noise.

I held branches. Stable. Done.

I opened my eyes. The world was still there, still loud, still detailed. But I had chosen what to attend to. A small victory, the sort that doesn't feel like one until later.

I gathered more wood. Slowly. One branch at a time. One sensation at a time, as much as I could manage.

The stream was close, and now its sound separated into layers: water over stone, water over gravel, and water against the bank. Under them ran the deeper note of a pool downstream, with a higher pitch where the current narrowed between two rocks.

I set the wood down at the camp and took the pot to the stream.

The water was clear. Dark and light pebbles showed on the bottom, sediment drifting where the current disturbed it. I knelt at the bank and lowered the pot into the stream.

The cold hit my hands before the water reached the rim. The current pressed against the pot. Light fractured into moving patterns on the surface. Wet stone smelled sharp above the bank. Mud softened under my knees. The handle bit into my palms.

My fingers opened. The pot tilted, water rushing in lopsided, its weight shifting. I grabbed it with both hands as cold water surged over my wrists and up my forearms. So much arrived at once that my vision blurred and my hearing went flat. I lost all sense of where I was among the sensations coming from every direction.

I pulled the pot up, set it on the bank, and sat down in the grass next to it.

The grass was wet. Every blade registered, but sitting was better than kneeling, and breathing was better than not. The stream went on with its layered, detailed, relentless sound, and the sun lay on my face. Water dripped from my hands into the grass. I couldn't stop noticing it.

I sat there. The stream ran. The pot stood next to me, half full, water dripping down its side.

I don't know how long it took before your footsteps reached me. You moved more slowly in the forest than on the trail, placing each foot carefully, uncertain what you would find. The pauses between steps carried the difference, the small adjustments before you put your weight down.

You found me sitting in wet grass with a pot of water, looking like I'd just lost an argument with myself.

You didn't ask. You sat down at an ordinary distance, leaving room for the pot between us. You gave me space without making it a precaution. Questions would have been too much.

"Everything is too loud, too bright, too much," I said.

You nodded.

"The water was too cold. I almost couldn't hold the—" I stopped. The sentence was too complicated. I couldn't separate what had happened into things I could say one after another. "Everything arrived at once."

"It will get easier," you said.

Your certainty sounded borrowed from something you'd read or been told. You had never lost your senses and found them again like this. You were guessing.

But the guess was kind. And kindness, at that moment, was more useful than accuracy.

"How long?"

"I don't know."

That was better. That was honest. I could work with honest.

You picked up the pot, tested its weight, and carefully added some water from the stream. The cold hit your hands, and your fingers tightened against it, the small involuntary grip of someone who has learned to hold on.

We walked back. You carried the pot. I carried nothing, because I'd had enough sensation for the morning. The fire had burned low while we were away. You fed it, and flames rose around the fresh wood.

You cooked rice with what was left of the dried vegetables from two camps ago. The pot steamed. Each smell stood apart: starch, salt, the mineral note of mountain water, and the scorched edge where rice met the bottom of the pot.

After we ate, you cleaned the pot and stacked the wood. You straightened your bedroll, then mine, then yours again. The restless maintenance of a man running out of tasks.

"Sleep," I said.

"I'm fine."

"You look like you haven't slept in two days."

"One."

"Sleep."

You opened your mouth to argue, then rubbed your eyes. You lay down on your bedroll, pulled the blanket up, and closed your eyes with the reluctant compliance of a man surrendering to a superior force.

You fell asleep within a few hundred heartbeats, sinking straight into exhaustion. Your breathing deepened. Your jaw relaxed, then your forehead, shoulders, and hands. Each change took some of the morning out of your face. You were simply a person sleeping in the afternoon sun. For a while, guilt and vigilance were someone else's problem.
