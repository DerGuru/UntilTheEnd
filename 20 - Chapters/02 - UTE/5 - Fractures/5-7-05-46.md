You looked past me toward the salt stall. "Yun." Your voice was quiet. Three people stood beyond the traders, evenly spaced, their sightlines covering one another with trained precision. They wore civilian clothing without visible talismans or weapons. Their patient, cataloguing attention gave them away.

"I know," I said.

"They've been there since we came back from lunch."

The fox-spirit watched from the shadow of a storage building. The child was gone. The traders were still talking. The sun moved behind the ridge, and the market's light changed. We still stood among those thirty buildings under the diligent observation of three professionals.

We left the village. The fox-spirit emerged from shadow and fell into step alongside us. The trail was there. East, still, always east. The village disappeared behind us as we climbed into the mountains.

The sadness settled like sediment in still water, present though it no longer clouded everything. The trail helped. The rhythm of walking helped. The enormous slopes, unchanged by anything I felt, helped most of all.

The three from the market were still behind us. They held the same precise distance, too consistently for coincidence. Whatever instructions the Pencil had given after his people retreated from the field, vomiting and shaken to the roots of their cultivation, they clearly included distance. Observation only. No threats.

Your notebook was open in your hand. The hesitation of recent days had settled into a pattern: you recorded external observations and closed the book when they were about me.

"Lian Zhuo's people," you said. "The formation habits are consistent even in plain clothes."

"Formation habits?"

"They're equidistant. Their sightlines are complementary. Two watch us, one watches the environment." You paused. "Textbook observation pattern. Whoever trained them didn't account for the possibility of being observed by someone who reads tactical deployment as a hobby."

The observation was almost funny. The thin, dry humor of a man who had spent years studying exactly this kind of fieldwork and now found its execution lacking.

"Should we—" You closed the notebook.

"No," I said. "Let them watch. As long as they keep their distance."

The pragmatism was calculated. Three sect cultivators at observation distance were a known quantity. Driving them off would make them unpredictable. Cornered observers might escalate, interpret confrontation as aggression, or do something desperate as their options narrowed. I would have to watch for their return, wonder what they had reported, then face whoever the Pencil sent in their place. Keeping these three in sight cost less.

I didn't want things to escalate. Better to let them watch. Better to let them report. Information flowing toward the sect was information not building into the pressure that demands explosive release.

Besides.

Their sect had seen the field, seen what happened to the mountain. What could these three report? Two people walking east? With no destination? They would record where we stopped, what we ate, and which trail we took. After the field, those small observations might even reassure the people reading them. For now, they were witnesses I could tolerate.

We walked. The observers followed. They would probably reposition, find a new vantage, and resume their patient documentation from a different angle. The sect had shifted from confrontation to study. The shift seemed permanent, as changed doctrines are.

I was a phenomenon to be understood.

But that was a problem for later. For now, the observers kept their distance while the trail went east. The world was sharp, loud, and cold, with small things still going wrong around me as regularly as installments on a bill.

We made camp where the trail widened onto a shelf of flat rock. Generations of travelers had stopped here. Earth was packed hard across a broad circle, beaten smooth by years of boots and bedrolls. A fire ring of stacked stones sat at the center, blackened, its gaps filled with old ash. Someone had dragged a log to one side, seat height, its bark worn to a polish on top. The wind broke against the ridge above and passed over without touching the shelf.

While you built the fire, I sat on the log. The fox-spirit came openly into camp from the trail behind us. It padded past you, close enough that its fur brushed your leg, and sat by the fire.

You froze, your hand going to your belt on reflex. You had spent years in places where sudden appearances were rarely good news. Your weight shifted. Your eyes went wide.

The fox-spirit looked up at you with amber eyes, its ears loose, its tail resting by its paws. It didn't move while you stared.

You blinked. "There's a fox-spirit at the fire."

"Yes?"

"It's been—"

"Following us. For a while. And apparently it has deemed you worthy of noticing it."

You frowned at it. It stared back. The standoff lasted three breaths before the fox-spirit gave a long yawn and began cleaning its left paw.

Your hand dropped from your belt.

"It's not going to attack you," I said. "It decided to let you see it. That's the opposite of an attack."

"How long is 'a while'?"

"A couple of tendays. Since before the field."

"I've been—" You paused. "Something was following us. I could feel it. I thought it was the scouts."

"It wasn't the scouts. Well, not only them."

The fox-spirit finished its paw, stood, and padded three steps closer before sitting again. Close enough to touch, if you'd wanted to. You didn't. But you didn't step back either.

"What should I call you?" I said.

You turned toward me. The fox-spirit did too. You both held perfectly still, heads tilted at almost the same angle.

I turned to you. "Not you."

I turned back to the fox-spirit. "You have amber eyes. What about Hǔpò?"

The fox-spirit hissed, ears flat, teeth bared. The sound was brief and absolute.

"No to that one." I studied the rust-colored fur. "Hóngyè?"

Same reaction. A hiss, bared teeth, and narrowed amber eyes.

"Okay, okay." I chuckled. "What about Xiǎohú?"

The ears shifted from forward to sideways, loose again. The fox-spirit's mouth closed.

"Xiǎohú," I said again. "You like that?"

The fox-spirit stood and padded toward me, her tail swinging in loose, unhurried arcs, without the stiff movement I'd seen in excited foxes. She stopped in front of me and tipped her snout forward, pressing it against my hand. Brief. Warm. Then she sat and curled her tail around her paws.

"Xiǎohú, then," I said, briefly stroking her head.

You gave me the look of a man being told that the invisible animal he hadn't known about had a name he hadn't been consulted on.

"You just named a fox-spirit. Right now."

"She named herself. I just listed options."

"She?"

"She's smaller than a male would usually be. And she doesn't carry the scent. Male foxes mark. It's sharp, unmistakable. She doesn't."

I turned back to Xiǎohú. "Are you a vixen?"

Xiǎohú leaned forward and pressed her snout against my hand again. Her tail swept loosely through the dust.

I turned to you. "I think she's a vixen."

Xiǎohú looked at you. You looked at Xiǎohú. Your hand was clear of your belt. Her ears were loose. For now, that was enough.

The fire popped. A coal shifted. Outside the circle of light, the observers' campfire flickered like an echo. We were three now, visibly, where before we had been two and a secret.
