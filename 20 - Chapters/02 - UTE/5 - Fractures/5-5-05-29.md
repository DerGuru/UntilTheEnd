The grass inside the field hung still. A held breath. A pause between heartbeats. Everything inside the boundary was locked in place, as if something at the roots of cause and effect had been held fast.

At my side, you steadied your breathing. Deliberate. Even. Your lips moved, silently forming numbers as you counted breaths. One breath, two heartbeats. A method that didn't require qi, that couldn't be taken from you.

The cultivator who'd stepped toward you was frozen mid-stride. His eyes moved, his chest rose, and his fingers twitched at his sides. He could breathe. Good. I didn't want to kill him; he hadn't done anything yet. His foot hung a handspan above the ground, suspended, the moment interrupted before it could finish.

The Pencil moved first. He took a step toward the field's edge, testing, probing, with the curiosity of a man whose first instinct was to measure rather than retreat. He moved as carefully as he'd spoken when we met, as if the world were a text he was still reading.

He could move. Yet the other man's step still hung unfinished.

The pressure in my chest held constant. Every attempt to picture that foot reaching the ground met the same refusal. The field held us in a terrified insistence on preservation. I was certain that anything altered, damaged, or destroyed here would be forced back into place.

Nothing could die inside the field. Not for lack of trying.

One of the cultivators, young and nervous, a junior rank sent on dangerous assignments because expendability is a resource, drew a blade. He held it low, pointed toward nobody in particular. The movement was trained into him, a reflex too deep for the wrongness of the air to override. Steel cleared the sheath. The sound was flat, contained, swallowed by the field before it could properly resonate.

His hand shook. The blade touched his forearm. An accident, a minor cut that happens when shaking hands hold sharp objects. A thin line of red appeared on his skin.

Then the cut healed.

Between one blink and the next, skin grew over the wound as if a wrong line had been erased. It was the same skin, the original, complete down to its fine creases. I had watched the blade go in. Now no place showed where it had entered.

The blood that had emerged was absorbed back into the body.

Everyone watched it. The thin line of red that had welled from the cut reversed direction, retreating along his arm like a river running upstream, seeping back through skin that was no longer broken, returning to wherever blood came from, as if the entire concept of bleeding had been vetoed.

The cultivator stared at his arm. Clean. Uncut. The blade in his hand still carried a faint red trace, but his skin was unmarked. He turned his wrist over.

One hundred and eighty heartbeats had passed when he dropped the blade.

The Pencil was writing. Inside a field that violated everything his people understood about reality, he was writing. His hand moved with mechanical precision, the reflex of someone who documents what he can't comprehend. I recognized the impulse. You had the same one.

But his hand was shaking too.

A cultivator near the edge of the field, older, experienced, a weathered operative who looked like she'd survived enough dangerous situations to have opinions about them, tested the boundary. She struck a rock with her palm. It was a cultivation technique, a simple structural impact, the demonstration first-year students perform to prove they've mastered basic qi channeling.

The rock didn't break. Her hand did.

A sharp, nauseating crack of bone separating from bone, the wet sound fingers make when they fold in directions they aren't meant to. She gasped. Her broken hand hung at her wrist, bent at the wrong angle, its fingers splayed.

Then it reset.

The bones cracked again, the same sound but in reverse, the sound of things coming together instead of apart. The fingers straightened, the angles corrected, and the swelling that had begun to form subsided. Fluid retreated into tissue. Her hand restored itself in the reverse order of its breakage, detail by detail.

She screamed. I couldn't tell how much was pain; it sounded different from her gasp at the break. Her hand was fixing itself without a technique, without anything she had done to ask for it. The same force that had erased the cut and blood now corrected her fingers with absolute certainty.

One of the young cultivators vomited. The vomit hit the ground and stayed. Apparently the field did not consider bodily fluids a form of damage requiring correction.

Three hundred heartbeats gone.

You stood at my side, unmoved since the field had activated. Your notebook was in your hand, but you weren't writing. The pen hovered above the page, unable to begin.

"This is you," you said.

It needed no confirmation. You were looking directly at the evidence.

I opened my mouth to respond. In that moment the sixth cultivator, the one whose suspended step toward you had triggered the entire thing, finally completed his motion. The field released him, or he forced through it, or whatever hold it had on his trajectory simply expired. He stumbled forward. Toward you.

Intent is a strange thing. In the compressed chaos of those four hundred and eighty heartbeats, which felt as long as four watches, I couldn't tell if his movement was aggressive or accidental. He stumbled. He was off-balance. His training would have corrected him instantly under normal conditions, but here he moved with the uncertainty of someone who no longer trusted the ground to behave like ground.

His elbow caught you in the ribs.

Hard. The impact was loud, too loud, amplified by the field's acoustic strangeness: a qi-enhanced limb hitting a mortal body without the modulation qi-enhanced people usually apply around those who aren't. Your not yet fully healed ribs broke again. Three distinct cracks, like branches snapping, except branches don't live inside people's chests.

You went down. Your body hit the ground with the boneless limpness of someone who has lost structural integrity, whose torso has been compromised, whose breathing has just become a project rather than a process.

Then your body healed.

The ribs cracked back. The same horrible, grinding, organic crack, but in reverse. Bone found bone, edges met, and surfaces realigned with the precision of things being put back where they belonged. Your body jerked under the forced articulation of something repairing you without permission.

You were whole. Unhurt. Intact.

Your face was the worst thing I saw inside the field.

Your face should have shown relief. Three cracked ribs, healed in heartbeats, no lasting damage. A miracle by any measure. But your hands were shaking. Your jaw was clenched. Your eyes had the look of a man whose body had just been taken from him and returned without his permission.

You turned to me. Something shifted behind the eyes that had watched me make weather, break trees, and bend paths. You'd accepted all of that. You'd stood in my rain. But this was different. The rain had been around you. This had been inside you. Your ribs, your bones, your body: all corrected like a spelling error.

Looking into your face, I thought you had understood what I was. What I was.

The Pencil stopped writing.

He had been documenting everything: the cut, the blood, the bone reset, the ribs. His hand had been moving with the desperate industry of someone who knew that records were the only thing that survived. But now he stopped. His pencil hung above the page. His eyes were on me and in them was something I recognized.

"Mercy of a god," he whispered.

The words were barely breath. I caught them only because whatever lived inside me caught everything in that moment. None of his people reacted.

His hand trembled. This was the man who had unified the Nine Rivers Sect, who had sent gray-robed diplomats with resonance talismans and patient, measured offers of cooperation. All of that preparation. Now he looked like a child who had opened a door and found the room on the other side much larger than the building that contained it.

Four hundred and twenty heartbeats.

The field hummed. It hummed in my teeth, in my bones, and in the space behind my eyes where the headaches had been living for days. The hum held constant, the rhythm of a reality held in a shape I had given it without knowing how.

Something in my chest turned over. Hot. Dense. The field healed without asking. It corrected without consent, fixing everything in place until preservation began to feel like death.

My field. My doing.

The rage built from the marrow outward. The frustration that shook the ground had been a tremor. This was tectonic. And it was directed at the thing inside me.

I raged against the field.

It shattered. All at once. It broke like glass. The boundary cracked. The stillness fractured. Sound rushed back in: wind, birds, and insects. The world's ambient noise, suspended for four hundred and eighty heartbeats, flooded back like water through a breach.

Silence followed. Real silence. Nobody spoke over the ordinary sounds around us. They stood looking at one another, at their hands, at the place where the boundary had been.

The Pencil stared at his notes. Then at me. Then at the people he'd brought here.

One of the cultivators was on his knees, vomiting again. Or still. The field's cessation had not corrected the nausea. Nausea was the appropriate response.

They looked at me as villagers look at a receding flood, shaken, already calculating the damage.

Four hundred and eighty heartbeats. Then nothing. The field was gone: collapsed, used, and spent.

How had I done it? I couldn't explain. But it would not come back. Whatever wellspring had produced it had burned itself dry.

You sat on the ground. Shaking.

I sat down too. Three paces. The old distance. The distance that had meant safe, until the field proved it irrelevant.

You didn't move closer. I didn't expect you to.
