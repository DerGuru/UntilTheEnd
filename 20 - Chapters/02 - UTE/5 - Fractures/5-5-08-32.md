The sunrise came from the east, as sunrises do, as this one always had. You were already awake, already moving, already performing the routine of breaking camp that had become ritual. I was sitting with my back against a boulder, pressing my thumbs into my temples, waiting to learn whether today would bring burning or throbbing.

The light hit the landscape. From our height, the valley below us opened slowly into depth and distance. Layered forest greens emerged beside the browns of exposed rock under the blue sky.

Except the landscape wasn't right.

The mountain on the opposite side of the valley, whole and unremarkable yesterday, was missing a piece. Even from this distance, it showed. A section of the western flank, roughly three hundred paces wide, simply absent. The silhouette was wrong. The shape my eyes expected, the shape that had been there every time I'd looked east, had a gap in it.

"Do you see that?" I said.

You were already looking. Your notebook was open. "The western flank. Something's missing."

The word missing might be wrong. From here, across the valley, the mountain looked complete, with smooth slopes where I remembered more stone. Its whole silhouette belonged to a different mountain.

"We need to get closer," you said.

We descended. The trail into the valley was steep, its switchbacks hard on the knees and best taken slowly. The headache pulsed with every step. Once a rock shifted under my foot on ground that should have been solid. I caught myself and went on.

At the valley floor we crossed a shallow stream on stones above the cold water. We started up the opposite slope. The ascent took most of the morning through dense undergrowth and loose scree, a mountain face better skirted than climbed directly.

By early afternoon, we reached the flank.

The edge was sharper than distance had suggested. The mountain simply stopped. One step landed on solid rock and soil. The normal geology of a mountainside that had been there for millennia. The next step would have been grass. The cut was horizontal, clean, as if someone had drawn a line and everything above had ceased to exist.

Established grass grew across the plateau, grass that takes months to root and years to thicken. It reached right up to the line where we stood, dense as any old meadow. Yellow wildflowers grew among it in casual abundance. Butterflies moved between the blossoms, settling with wings spread, then lifting to the next patch.

It looked as though this part of the mountain had never been there. Forgotten.

You knelt at the edge to touch the grass. You pulled a blade and examined its healthy roots, several tendays of growth at minimum. You held it between your fingers, your expression that of a man whose categories were failing.

"This grass didn't grow overnight," you said. "But the mountain was there yesterday."

You opened your notebook to sketch the mountain's shape, the missing section, and the smooth boundary where absence began. You annotated the flowers, the grass depth, and the butterflies. Your hand moved with the stubborn persistence of someone who had decided that documentation is the only response that doesn't involve screaming.

Evidence of nothing. Evidence of nothing having always been nothing.

The field must have done this. The mountain had still been whole yesterday. Whatever its collapse had set in motion was spreading still. Overnight, while we slept, a portion of the mountain had vanished as completely as the memories I lost. We had our recollections of its shape. The grass beneath our hands offered no support for them.

"Three hundred paces across," you said, measuring with your eye. "The depth—" You leaned forward carefully to look along the face. "At least two hundred paces thick at this elevation."

More rock than a hundred thousand wagons could carry. Stone, mineral, and the compressed history of geological time. No debris in the valley. No dust in the air. No indication that the material existed anywhere else.

Just gone. As if it had never been.

"Four hundred and eighty heartbeats," you said. You were staring at the edge. "That's how long the field ran. It had the power to preserve everything inside it. Where did that strength come from?"

I didn't answer.

You closed your notebook, opened it again, and closed it. "What if the field was fueled by this?" You gestured at the absence. "What if it drew its strength out of the mountain, until there was nothing left?"

You said it like a question. Like something you wanted me to disprove.

I couldn't.

"It didn't break the mountain. It ate it. And what's left—" You looked at the grass growing where stone should be. "It removed it. The way—"

You stopped.

"Say it."

"The way you forget things." You looked at me. "Wholesale. Complete. With no trace that there was anything to forget."

My hands were cold even in the mild air. That was the cruelest part. The sky was clear, the light golden, the breeze clean with pine resin and the mineral sharpness of high altitude. Birds sang. Wind moved through the pines. Everything was exactly as it should be.

Except for the hole in the mountain where the mountain should have been.

I sat down at the edge. The grass was soft and warm under my hands. The same grass that couldn't possibly be here, growing in soil that couldn't possibly exist, rooted in a reality that had been rewritten while I slept.

A stone shifted under my foot. A small thing, a loose stone on a slope, something that happens a thousand times a day on any mountain. My foot slipped.

But the stone shouldn't have shifted. The ground had been solid.

Your flask: a thin film of moisture seeping through the seam where the leather was stitched. Not much, a few drops. The flask was new, well-made, quality equipment that doesn't leak unless it's been damaged.

It hadn't been damaged.

It was just leaking, for no reason.

A loose stone that shouldn't have shifted. A flask that shouldn't have leaked.

I had spent something enormous. A weight of rock beyond counting, erased, consumed, and burned as fuel for something I hadn't asked for.

The bill was arriving. In small installments. A loose stone. A leaking flask. The trivial, accumulating math of small things going wrong.

"Let's move on," I said.

You gave the edge one more long look. The flowers. The butterflies still moving between them, barely bending the stems when they landed.

You turned and we went.
