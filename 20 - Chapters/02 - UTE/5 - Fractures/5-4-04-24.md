I lay on my back next to the dying fire, unable to sleep through the steady ache behind my eyes. The clouds had cleared enough to show stars. I checked each constellation against my memory, then the moon's position for the season. Everything seemed right. I went over it again to be sure.

You slept with your hand resting on the notebook. Even after your fingers relaxed, you held it close. I listened to your breathing for a while.

Smoke from the embers brought back a village. Fire had risen through its rooftops. I remembered destruction without being able to place the settlement or what had happened to it.

Then I remembered pressing my hand against a cool stone wall. My fingers followed carved grooves in the same motion I'd used in the ruins. Someone had cut those lines. I might even have cut them myself, though I couldn't remember doing it.

A young man's face followed, with brown eyes and an open mouth. He was speaking. I strained for the words, holding the image until my chest tightened. No sound came back.

My head pulsed.

I couldn't fit the memories together. Each attempt made the headache worse.

A narrow mountain path came back to me next. It switched between two ridges, with loose scree at the second turn. In one place it narrowed to shoulder-width above a sheer drop of two hundred paces to the river. I remembered the three peaks to the south. The leftmost was blunt, with a flat top. The middle peak leaned east to a narrow point. A cleft split the rightmost down its center, wide enough to show blue sky on a clear day.

No other place in the world looked like that. I had walked thousands of paths across thousands of mountains and this view existed exactly once.

At the third switchback, scree gave way to solid granite. The trail widened just enough for a single tree to grow from a crack. I remembered passing it on my left. I could place my shoulder next to its trunk as clearly as if I were walking there now.

I also remembered the tree on my right. The path was the same; the three peaks stood in the same positions beyond it. I followed each remembered walk toward the third turn. Each remained equally clear.

Nobody except me.

The headache sharpened. I pressed my fist against my forehead and held it there.

Not now.

The words interrupted my attempt to compare the two walks. When I tried to picture the tree again, I could find only the unease it had left.

I opened my eyes. The stars were still right.

I got up and walked to the stream.

I knelt at the stream and put my hands in the cold water. They were already clean. I rubbed one palm with the other, then reversed the motion. I thought of how close I'd come to understanding.

I washed for long enough that a fresh cup of tea would have cooled next to me. Clear water ran over my skin. I rubbed on.

You found me there.

You waited behind me without speaking. I knew that patience from the ruins, when I'd searched for words you could see I couldn't find. Your feet were still on the bank.

I stopped washing and looked down at my hands. Clean. Empty.

"Something happened," I said.

"Yes."

"Something important."

"Yes."

"I don't remember."

For a long time I heard only water running over the stones. You waited until I lowered my hands.

"I wrote it down," you said.

I turned. You held out the open notebook. The page was filled with your careful handwriting. The separate entries showed from where I knelt.

I read.

*First attempt: I explained the pattern. She understood. Ten heartbeats later, she asked, "What were you saying?"*

*Second attempt: I repeated the explanation. She understood. After one hundred and twenty heartbeats, she had lost the connections one by one.*

*Third attempt: I repeated the explanation. She understood. I watched her eyes lose their focus. The understanding was taken from her.*

I read the entries three times. They described the night I'd just lived through. I remembered sitting by the fire while you spoke, and each moment of understanding. The connections themselves were gone. Your account let me place all three losses and brought back none of what I'd lost.

I handed back the notebook. You took it, closed it, and held it against your chest.

"The gap," I said. "In me. It's bigger now."

"I know."

"Whatever is in there, whatever is being protected, it's important."

"I know."

"And I can't reach it."

"Not yet."

"Maybe not ever."

The morning arrived as a thin gray line along the eastern ridge. In the growing dawn, your face was tired. Older than it should have been.

"You'll reach it," you said. "Perhaps another way. Thinking makes it worse. My explanations don't stay with you. Something happens between understanding and remembering."

"Then how?"

You turned to the stream.

"I don't know yet," you said.

I stood, wiped my hands on my robe, and walked back to the fire.

The embers were dying. The scrolls lay where we'd left them. The sand drawings had faded, softened by the night wind, but their patterns still showed in the dirt.

I looked from the drawings to the scrolls, then to the notebook you held against your chest.

"Why are these still here?" I said.

You followed my gaze. The scrolls. The maps. The evidence.

"Whatever does this to me can move chalk. It can shift paths. It can rearrange terrain." I gestured at the documents. "So why not change these? Why leave the evidence intact?"

You looked down at the notebook in your hands.

"You're right," you said slowly. "The scrolls survived for thousands of years. My notebook is untouched." You turned it over to examine the cover. "If whatever is in you can change the physical world, why not destroy the records?"

"It doesn't make sense."

"Unless it can't."

"It bends trees."

"But trees don't contain information about you."

We looked at each other. Water murmured over the stones as the gray light thickened.

"Or it doesn't want to," you said. "Not can't. Doesn't want to. The distinction matters."

"Assuming it has its own will. Why wouldn't it want to? It keeps me from remembering. It keeps me from understanding. Why would it want to preserve evidence that could lead me there?"

You opened your mouth, then paused. Your grip tightened on the notebook.

"I don't know," you said. "But we should keep looking at the difference. The chalk changed. The paths changed. You lost what you understood. Yet the paper, ink, and stone carvings remain."

I turned my hands over. They were still cold from the washing, as clean as they'd been when I began.

"There's a pattern," I said. "In what it changes and what it leaves alone. If I could see that pattern—"

The headache pulsed. I narrowed my eyes against the pain.

"Later," you said, watching my face. "We'll come back to it."

The bowl of rice from last night sat where you had left it. Cold now. The grains stuck together in a single congealed mass.

I left it there.

The gap pulsed.

I went to sleep.
