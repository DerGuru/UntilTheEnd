Your breathing became audible as we climbed the ridge to the observatory. Its stone walls formed concentric circles under a thin, clear sky. Openings in the walls aligned with star positions that no longer matched it. The astronomers who'd designed them had been meticulous. I checked the angles, trying to account for the difference.

I tasted chalk the moment we stepped inside. Metal braces held the observation slits at adjustable angles, with counterweights to keep them steady. Sophisticated work for the era. The braces were cold enough to sting. The incense was there too, faint but unmistakable.

Three markers. Every time.

The qi underneath the observatory matched. Foundation layer. Old. My signature, or its sibling. Pressed into volcanic rock that had cooled ten thousand years before anyone had thought to build anything here.

In the lower chamber, where the astronomers had stored their charts and calculations, I found inscriptions in a different hand. The script belonged to a different century. Yet again, I read observations of things that failed to match the writer's records.

Same ending. Mid-character. Mid-thought. Gone.

Three researchers at three sites, separated by roughly five thousand years. Each had independently noticed what I was noticing. Their measurements followed the same kinds of discrepancy. The final inscription supplied the next question, one I had been asking myself: where did these changes begin?

Every record stopped before an answer.

"It follows a pattern," you said that evening. We were camped below the ridge, where the air was thick enough to cook in. The fire crackled with pine resin, spitting sparks into the dark. Your notebook was open across your knees, filled with the day's observations.

"Not just a pattern. A design." I disliked the word as soon as I said it. With the resin snapping in the fire, I pictured someone planning all those interruptions. What we'd found felt quicker than planning: pressure applied, then a recoil.

"A design that does what?"

"Erases people who ask the right questions."

You eyed me over the notebook. The firelight caught your face at an angle that made you look older than you were, or younger, depending on which shadow you followed. "And you're asking the right questions."

"Apparently."

"Then why are you still here?"

A fair question. Why hadn't it happened to me? I'd been asking myself through the quiet stretches between discoveries. At night, when I would once have meditated, I lay down instead. I wanted sleep despite being rested. After thousands of years of rarely sleeping, I sought it whenever the questions got difficult.

Across the fire, you waited with your pen above the page. I drew my knees closer. I could still search, still remember the inscriptions, and still recognize what those researchers had found. Why had I been allowed to get this far?

There were several possible answers. None of them were comforting.

"I don't know," I said. Which was true. And incomplete.

I suspected it had something to do with the qi under those ruins. Perhaps whatever had laid that foundation had also stopped the researchers studying it. Dust still caught around my nails. The signature in every stone had been close to mine.

My hand.

The headache hit. Sharp. Centered. A needle through the space behind my left eye, driven with precision and withdrawn just as quickly. I pressed my thumb against my temple and held it there until the afterimage faded.

Not now.

Again, those words arrived with an ease that troubled me. I kept my thumb against my temple. I had stopped a thought this way before, often enough that the interruption had become routine. How long had I been doing it?

I released my temple and opened my eyes. You were watching me.

"The headaches are getting worse," you said.

"They're manageable."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's what I answered."

You held my gaze. Then you looked down at your notebook and wrote something I couldn't see.

In the days that followed, we found four more sites. At a half-submerged temple in a river valley, the qi was so concentrated in the waterlogged stone that the water flowed warmer around its foundations. In the western foothills, we searched a hermit's cave barely large enough for one person. Someone had scratched inscriptions into its walls with a fingernail.

Forest had overtaken an abandoned sect compound, its trees rooted along the qi lines under the soil. Even a small roadside shrine held the same deep foundation. I crouched by its unremarkable stones. That signature was there again.

Every site had chalk dust, cold metal, and incense.

Every site had inscriptions by a different researcher.

Every site had that ending.

Seven sites across thousands of years. Seven researchers had followed the same questions. Every one of their records ended unfinished.

Under every site lay qi I couldn't tell from my own.

The list had grown to fourteen anomalies in twenty-one days. I could still picture the curved path and the plateau where the valley should have been. I remembered the wrong wind against my cheek. There was the owl outside its range, the widened river, and the warmth at altitudes that should have been cold. Even the stars sat out of place.

I could have dismissed any one of them. A faulty memory might explain a path. Changing weather might account for warmth or a bird's altered range. Taken together, they were harder to explain. I kept comparing each discovery with the sites we'd already searched.

The changes were small enough to pass unnoticed. A path bent further. A species lived a hundred li beyond its usual range. Qi under a ruin was older than its surroundings. Every altered thing was as settled as the untouched ground next to it.

I tried to picture what could leave so few visible traces. Then I touched the stone again and met that likeness to my qi. Was it mine? Had it once been mine? I could even imagine finding that signature in myself someday, changed by years I hadn't yet lived.

I hadn't slept well since the library. The headaches came when I followed certain questions too far. I began to note what provoked them, what eased them, and where I'd stopped thinking when they struck.

Every time, I had been close to connecting my own qi with what those seven researchers had found. I could compare their observations without pain. As soon as I asked what the comparison meant about me, my temple began to throb.

The fourteenth anomaly was the worst.

We were walking through a bamboo grove, tall, mature growth, the kind that takes decades to establish. The path was well-maintained, a local trail, marked by stone cairns at regular intervals. Normal grove. Normal path.

I had walked this grove forty years ago. It had been a clearing then. Open ground, grass, a single dead tree in the center that birds used as a perching station. I'd rested there during a long walk south. I'd sat with my back against the dead tree and counted hawks.

The tree was gone. Thick, mature bamboo filled the clearing. Its roots ran deep under the path. Overhead, the canopy shut out the sky. I pressed my foot into the ground where the dead tree should have stood. There was only the densely rooted soil of the grove.

I had sat against that tree counting hawks. I remembered the bark against my back too clearly to dismiss it.

I stopped in the center of the grove. Wind rustled the bamboo leaves, and the stems creaked hollowly.

"Yun?" you said.

"This was a clearing."

You looked around. Tall bamboo in every direction, as far as the filtered light reached. "When?"

"Forty years ago."

"Bamboo can grow—"

"Not this much. Not this established. This grove is at least eighty years old." I touched a stalk. Thick. Solid. "This bamboo was growing here when I sat in this clearing. And it wasn't."

You were quiet. The bamboo whispered.

"Both can't be true," you said finally.

"No," I said. "They can't."

Unless someone changed it. The headache was already building. I kept the thought to myself and left my hand on the stalk while you looked around us.

That night, I couldn't sleep.

Your breathing was steady in the dark. I sat at the low table in the roadside inn where we'd stopped and spread your notebook open in front of me. Your handwriting was precise, even beautiful. You'd taken care over every entry, even on the days when we had arrived tired or hungry. Every anomaly had a date and a location.

The fourteenth entry was the last. Below it, in your shorthand, was a summary: *spatial inconsistencies, qi foundation anomalies, signature match for Yun, terrain deviation, and temporal impossibilities.*

I read it twice.

Then I tore the page out.

I followed the binding carefully. The paper tore with a small sound, loud enough that I paused to listen to your breathing. I folded the page twice, then tucked it into the inner pocket of my robe. It lay flat against my skin.

The tear was barely visible. You'd have to know the page was missing to look for its absence.

I kept my palm over the pocket. Your tidy handwriting had put me next to the same observations those seven researchers had made. You were drawing their conclusions yourself. If I left the summary in your notebook, you would keep following them.

I knew how those stories ended.

I listened to you breathe, telling myself I was protecting you. Whatever had stopped those researchers might reach you through the same knowledge. I didn't understand it well enough to promise you safety. Taking the page was something I could do now, quietly, before you read it again.

I smoothed the cover of your notebook. You might accept that explanation. I wanted to accept it myself.

My hands wouldn't settle. I folded the hem of my sleeve, then pressed the callus on my thumb. If you looked at the evidence you'd gathered, you might finally understand what I was. You might leave. I couldn't bear that. Or you might stay to help, which I was afraid you wouldn't survive.

The folded page pressed against my skin.

You breathed.

I sat in the dark.
