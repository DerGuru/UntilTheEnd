I woke warm. The cold was still there underneath, as it had been since the clearing, but the fire I'd fed before sleeping had done its work. Its coals glowed orange in the gray, the heat reaching my face and hands before I opened my eyes. Proof that something I'd taken care of had lasted through the night.

Your robe was around me. Its smell was still unfamiliar: woodsmoke, dust, and a trace of the pine resin you'd used on your boots. I'd had it for one day and already the weight on my shoulders was something I'd miss. The ground was hard under my back, but the hardness was known. The cold was known. I was still learning the robe, how it gathered at my elbows, how much warmth it held when I kept it close.

It was almost comfortable.

Then the morning came in full.

The cold sharpened. The bird calls that had been soft background in the last heartbeats of sleep separated into individual voices, each carrying pitch, rhythm, and direction. Light pressed through my eyelids. Even the pre-dawn gray had become a sharp, exhausting sensation I needed to learn to bear.

I opened my eyes. The world was there. Even sharper and louder. More detailed than any world had a right to be this early.

The crack was there too.

Like a loose tooth. I'd had one as a child, the kind that loosened gradually, then sat in the gum for days, moving at every touch of the tongue. Its small, persistent wrongness drew my attention. The crack was like that. Pressed mentally, it gave, with the resistance of something still attached. Something that might eventually come free.

I pressed gently, to confirm that the change was real.

The crack responded with a draft. The interior equivalent of air through a gap, the faintest difference in pressure, the sign that there was another side and that it had its own atmosphere.

Something on the other side was breathing.

I sat up and the robe fell from my shoulders.

Movement at the edge of vision. A fox-spirit.

Small. Reddish-brown, with a white chin and ears too large for its skull. A minor spirit beast, with little intelligence but enough sensitivity to follow qi as a dog follows scent. I'd seen it once before, many days ago, stirring in the undergrowth when my emotions had done something loud to the landscape. It had stared at me from its hiding place, head tilted, ears forward, then vanished into the brush before I could decide whether it mattered.

Now it lay openly in the grass three paces from my bedroll, settled into the spot with the easy sprawl of a cat on someone else's bedding.

It had come closer. Considerably.

I didn't move, the robe pooled around me, my arms cold. The fox-spirit watched me with amber eyes from a distance it had never allowed before. Spirit beasts respond to qi as readily as people respond to faces. Whatever had altered in me overnight had drawn this one closer. I let my hands rest in the cloth. Reaching toward it would have changed the small arrangement we had, and I wanted it to stay.

You were already up and moving around camp through the morning routine, but differently. Softer. Your gestures had a care to them that hadn't been there before the clearing, as if you were handling fragile things near a fragile person.

You walked to the fire kit, the water skin, and the packs. Each time your path curved around the fox-spirit in a wide arc, though you gave no sign of noticing it. Your eyes passed over the patch of grass as if it held nothing but grass.

That was interesting. The fox-spirit's presence was blurred to your senses. An old trick: minor spirit beasts rarely had the qi for true concealment, but a fox could obscure itself enough to slide past mortal attention. You still detoured around it.

The question I couldn't remember had cost you something. Its price sat behind your eyes, a strain that hadn't been there before the clearing.

You handed me the tea. Its warmth went through my fingers. The gesture was the same as always, but the act of giving it had changed. A small kindness that said, without words, *I want you to be okay.*

"How are you?" you asked. The question was genuine. You wanted to know, the way a surgeon needs to know whether the patient survived.

"I can still feel the crack," I said.

You nodded, set down your cup, and reached for the notebook. Then you hesitated. You didn't open it. This was new. Before the clearing, the notebook would have been open already, the pen moving, observation becoming record with the automatic efficiency of habit. Now you waited, as if recording me required something you had never asked for before. Your hand stopped on the cover. You had made room for an answer beyond the one I could give.

"Like a door that's swollen shut," I continued. "I can feel it move when I lean into it. But it won't open."

"Does it hurt?"

I considered. "Not yet."

You took a breath and let it out. The exhale carried something you wanted to say and chose not to. The silence was disciplined: you had learned that sometimes the kindest thing was not to provide information.

We packed. The fox-spirit watched, amber eyes tracking our movements. When we lifted our packs and turned toward the trail, it stood. It stretched its whole body, front paws extended in the grass, then followed.

Ten paces behind. Near enough to follow, far enough to slip away.

We walked east, still east, following the paths that kept turning me toward isolation. But the walking felt different now. For days I had gone head down, one foot after the other, moving because stopping was worse. Today there was something beyond that need to keep going.

This was walking with something in my chest. A draft where there used to be a wall. A fox where there used to be empty grass. A world that tasted like itself for the first time.

Behind us, three sect cultivators appeared at the tree line, watching us with the patient attention of people who had upgraded their classification of me from threat to phenomenon.

Forward. The only direction that mattered.
