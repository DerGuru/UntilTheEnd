We rested by a stream four days past the library. You'd built a fire with the efficiency of long practice. The pot was already on, the water coming to a boil. You cut a root vegetable into even pieces. It was the kind that looks like a mistake until you cook it.

"You're searching for something concrete," you said. "What?"

You were better at this than me. Always had been. I could unmake a mountain but I'd overcook rice. The irony didn't escape me. It rarely did.

"Me," I said.

You stopped cutting to look up. The knife hovered over the root, a bead of moisture running down the flat of the blade.

"You?"

"Me."

"That's not—" You caught yourself. You set the knife down before trying a different question. "What does that mean, specifically?"

"Specifically." I watched the stream run downhill, clear and cold from the mountain. I followed a ripple around a stone. Its course was ordinary. "I'm looking for the places where I've shown up. In the world. The marks I've left. The impressions."

"Everyone leaves marks."

"Not like this."

You waited. You'd become better at disguising your impatience, which served just as well if I didn't look closely. I looked closely. Your jaw tightened. You leaned forward, reaching for your notebook without taking your eyes off me.

"The shrine had qi older than the building," I said. "The library too. Matching signature. The path we walked should have gone through a valley. There's a plateau instead."

"People's memories of terrain can be—"

"Mine can't."

You opened your mouth, then glanced at the notebook. You had recorded things I'd said with equal certainty and later couldn't remember saying. I expected you to remind me. Instead you studied me.

"You're remembering something," you said slowly. "Not forgetting something."

I met your eyes.

"That's different." You picked up your pen to write something. "All right. The terrain changed. What does that have to do with searching for you?"

I picked up a smooth gray pebble from the stream bank. Its cold was the cold of every mountain stream I'd ever put my hands into. After the fittings at the shrine, that mattered. I turned it in my fingers.

"Everything I've found so far has one thing in common. The qi that doesn't belong, the qi that's too old, too deep, too embedded in the stone to have gotten there naturally. And it matches mine."

You held your breath for about three heartbeats, then released it. To your credit, that was the only visible reaction.

"Similar how?"

"In every way that counts. Same shape. Same pattern. Same source."

"You're saying you left those qi traces."

"I'm saying something with my signature left them. Centuries before the buildings existed. In stone that was bare ground at the time."

You looked down at your notes. The water boiled. You added the root vegetables without taking your eyes from the page.

"And the terrain changes? The path, the valley?"

I threw the pebble back. It hit the water and sank without a sound worth noting.

"I don't know yet."

That was true. I had a suspicion, but I hadn't tested it. I rubbed the damp grit from my fingers, watching it fall to the bank. I wanted another site before I said anything more. Perhaps I would want another after that.

You pushed a bowl toward me. I looked at it. You waited.

I ate.

You'd seasoned the food with something green and peppery from the stream bank. It was better than it had any right to be. I took another mouthful while you waited with your own bowl untouched. Refusing food you'd prepared had begun to require more indifference than I could manage.

The stream murmured over its stones. Wind moved through the trees from the east, at the strength I expected. It touched my cheek. One more right thing.

"How many anomalies so far?" you asked.

"Seven."

"Seven in—"

"Twelve days."

You wrote it down. Your hand moved, the pen precise, the characters aligned. You dated the entry. Location. Conditions. A brief description in shorthand I couldn't read from this distance and didn't need to.

"There was talk at the last town," you said, not looking up. "Someone in the south consolidating Minor Sects. An academic, apparently. Using old technique research as leverage." You shrugged. "Probably nothing."

I said nothing. Sect politics didn't concern me. Sects rose and fell like weather.

"What does 'similar to yours' mean in terms of—" You hesitated. You didn't often hesitate. "In terms of scale? Is it a trace? A mark? Or—"

"A foundation."

"A foundation?"

"The qi under these sites isn't residual. It's structural. It bears weight. As if something laid down a layer of energy and everything else was built on top of it. The buildings, the roads, and the geography." I paused. "The geography."

Your pen stopped. You looked across the stream toward the far bank, then back at me. The small movement of your shoulders might have been a flinch. You set the notebook more firmly against your knee.

"You're saying the landscape itself—"

"I'm saying I need to see more."

You accepted the deflection. We'd done this before: I would approach something I could almost name, then turn to the next task. You would record where I'd stopped. Now you added a short line under your last entry. I watched the ink dry, aware that I could still answer you.

My hands were cold. I pressed them against my thighs.

If the ground under us bore my mark, I had already done something here. Finding more sites would tell me how much. I pressed harder until I felt the bones under my palms.

I stood to stretch. The headache was there again, a dull weight behind my eyes that came and went without pattern. It had begun after we left the shrine. Now it came more frequently. I rubbed my temple, then caught myself doing it again a few breaths later.

"Next site?" you asked.

"Further south. A few days. A meditation cave complex."

"You've been there?"

"Once. A long time ago."

"Will it be where you remember?"

Our eyes met.

"We'll see," I said.

I didn't sleep well that night. The ground was comfortable enough. I've slept on worse. I've slept on nearly everything. Each time I closed my eyes, I pictured the inscriptions in the library. The researcher had been cataloguing those anomalies right up to the final, unfinished stroke.

What had stopped them?

I turned onto my back. Distant stars showed through the canopy.

Death was the obvious answer. Death stopped most things. Yet the wall around that final character had been undamaged. I had found neither scorch marks nor signs of combat. The person might have stopped halfway through the stroke, lowered their hand, and walked away. They might have forgotten what they were doing.

Forgotten.

The headache spiked. I pressed my fist against my forehead.

Not now.

The words came so readily that I held still. A moment ago I'd been following a thought. Now there was only the pressure of my fist against my forehead.

I lay in the dark and listened to you breathe. Slow. Even. You trusted the world enough to sleep in it.

My hands were still. The headache throbbed behind my left eye.
