At midday, Wei stood at the well with Lao Chen's neighbor, a farmer whose name I knew and no longer cared about. Relevance had compressed to a single point. The flicker.

They were talking, an ordinary conversation between a boy and a man at a well. Water, weather, the state of the harvest recovery. Wei was animated, bright, fourteen, and recently a hero.

Then his core pulsed.

It broke through his dampening, a visible, undeniable eruption of qi. Golden heat radiated from his body in a pulse anyone with eyes could see. Cultivators, sensitives, and ordinary people alike were within sight of it.

For one heartbeat, Wei looked like a lantern. A human-shaped light source. A boy broadcasting at a frequency visible in the mundane spectrum.

The farmer stepped back, startled by something he couldn't fathom. He stared at Wei, his question plain before he spoke.

"What IS that? Your qi?"

Wei blinked, deciding whether what he had felt was real or imagined. He looked at his hands. Both. The tremor was there, bilateral and fine, the vibration I had catalogued at Stage 2. Now someone other than me could see it, in daylight, at a well.

"Nothing. Just—"

"That wasn't 'nothing.'"

Wei looked at his hands again. He closed them, opened them, and closed them. The tremor persisted through the first close, diminished through the second, and stopped.

"I'm just tired," he said.

The farmer didn't believe him but lacked the information to contradict him. He would forget it until it happened again.

Wei moved on. Toward the market stall.

I held my position at the wall with my cold tea, the observation post I had occupied all morning. It gave me an uninterrupted view of everything.

The flicker had been visible to at least four people: the farmer, a woman at the nearest stall, a child, and Fang Liang.

Fang Liang was there too.

He had arrived at the settlement's edge in simpler clothes, carrying a satchel of herbs, presenting as a traveling merchant rather than a sect disciple. The disguise was competent. It was also irrelevant, because his qi-signature was the same and I already knew his qi-signature.

He was distributing salves and herbal remedies, helping settlers. Genuine benevolence and strategic positioning at once. He could do both, which was what made him dangerous.

Wei saw him. Recognition crossed Wei's face: surprise, recalibration, decision. He approached.

They spoke.

I was too far to hear the words, but Wei's posture was readable: the attention he gave when someone told him something he didn't want to hear but couldn't dismiss.

Fang Liang handed Wei something small. A jar with healing salve, probably. For the shoulder. The kindness of a handler maintaining contact through care.

Then the wind blew a sentence over. Or I read it on Fang Liang's lips, a skill I rarely needed and now did.

"You have to decide, boy. Soon no wall will be enough."

The smile was gone from his face. For the first time since I'd seen him, the infrastructure of friendliness was down. His expression was naked, stripped of the warmth of the market encounter. I had seen his coldness in the jade-tablet report; this was harder to read. Present. Unmediated.

Worried? Maybe. Or pragmatic, a man losing a resource. His concern looked like worry but might have been investment protection.

Or maybe he was a man in his twenties who had met a boy and recognized something in him. He was watching that boy walk toward something coming. The warning was the truth, an unsmiling one.

No wall will be enough.

Ambiguous. The walls of the settlement against the sect. Or the walls of Wei's body against his core. Both readings valid. Both true.

Wei stood in the afternoon light after Fang Liang left, holding a jar of healing salve and looking at the road east toward the sect gate. The door he had closed that was now being opened from the other side.

When he finally came back for the evening meal and sat across the table with rice and tea, he didn't mention the conversation.

"Long day," I said.

"Mm."

"How is your shoulder?"

"Fine."

The first thing Wei didn't share. The first information he held back.

The symmetry was perfect and terrible. I was withholding information from him. He was withholding information from me. We sat across a table from each other with our separate secrets, and the table measured the width of unsaid things.

I drank my tea. He drank his.

The table held.
