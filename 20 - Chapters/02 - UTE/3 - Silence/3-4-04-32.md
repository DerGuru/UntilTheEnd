The qi source lay about half a watch's hike up the mountain slope, through forest that was sick now. The trees showed stress in their canopy, leaves curled inward against something invisible, intangible.

Wei led. Four settlers followed, the supply carriers he'd selected. Adults. Middle-aged. Following a boy up a mountain because he was the one who knew where the mountain hurt.

I followed them. Far enough behind to not interfere, close enough to be available.

The source was in a small cave. A fissure cracked the rock face, barely wide enough for one person, descending into darkness. Inside lay the qi-source, the convergence point where underground energy streams met and distributed. The valley's qi-heart. Arrhythmic now.

Wei stopped at the entrance and looked back at the settlers.

"Wait here."

"Wei—"

"It's narrow. And the qi-pressure inside is going to be strong. Anyone without cultivation will get sick."

He was right. The corridor would be saturated with compressed qi from a destabilized source in a confined space. For ordinary bodies that meant migraine, nausea, and vertigo. Even for cultivators it was barely manageable.

He turned to me.

The look asked for permission and trust. The permission was a release, a loosening of the tie between his actions and my approval. The trust was that of someone about to walk into danger who wanted to know the person behind him was steady.

I could have gone in. Walked past him, descended, identified the disruption in heartbeats, corrected it with a fraction of my capacity. Done. The settlers would have gone home. The fields would recover. Everything fixed, everything stable, everything: Yun handled it.

And Wei would have stood at the entrance. Watching. Bypassed. Rendered irrelevant by the efficiency of someone trained by millennia, making everyone around me unnecessary. Unnecessary was the wound. Healing required this. How could someone like him compare to someone like me?

He couldn't. And he shouldn't. This was his moment.

"I'll be at the entrance," I said.

He nodded. Turned. Descended.

The darkness swallowed him incrementally, light yielding to curvature and curvature to depth.

I stood at the entrance. The settlers behind me. The forest sick around us. The mountain containing the source and my boy approaching it.

His qi-signature, bright and distinctive, was one I could find in any crowd, in any cave on any continent. I tracked his progress.

He moved deeper. Steady. Descending. Approaching the node.

A quarter of a watch.

He reached the node. The ambient qi-pressure jumped as the source responded to his presence.

He began.

He channeled qi into the disrupted node and found the imbalances, the energy-tilts, and the dislocations that had caused the surface symptoms. Layer by layer he probed. Methodical. For a fourteen-year-old this was extraordinary, work trained specialists performed after years of study. It required sensitivity, control, and the patience to correct without overcorrecting. Patience was Wei's least natural quality, which made his doing it anyway the triumph.

I counted the twitches.

During the stabilization, with his qi extended into the source and his control at maximum, his core twitched.

The first twitch was light. Like a stumble in the signal, his core's output flickering, catching and recovering. A foot missing a step and correcting.

I gripped the cave wall. Stone under my fingers. Cold.

He continued, deeper into the work. The source responded slowly, the energy patterns beginning to realign.

He was doing it.

The second twitch was stronger. His core pulsed, the overcorrection of a system compensating for the first instability by adding force. Like bailing water with a bigger bucket, which was temporarily effective but structurally damaging.

My nails dug into the stone.

He was going to make it. The source began to stabilize. The patterns aligning. Almost done. Almost—

The third twitch was the strongest. His core FLARED, bright, hot, a full-intensity discharge from a system that had exceeded its capacity. Like a gong struck too hard. The note was correct but the vibration was excessive, lingering past where it should have stopped.

The gong rang on, and Wei finished. The source stabilized. The patterns aligned. The valley was saved.

He emerged from the cave into the light, dusty and sweating, his face pale with exertion. Yet he stood grinning.

"It's done," he said. "The qi is stable again."

The settlers felt it immediately. The qi-pressure lifted. Headaches receded. The air was clearer. The relief when the world goes from wrong to right.

They cheered, a spontaneous roar of relief from people whose pain had stopped. His name. Wei. The boy who'd fixed the earth.

He stood in the middle, surrounded by grateful adults. Old hands on his shoulders, voices thanking him, eyes full of the gratitude of people who had been afraid and no longer were.

He smiled, the quieter, deeper version, the smile of someone who had done something real. Something that mattered.

I saw the tremors before he did, in both hands, a post-exertion vibration that was not normal. Normal tremors were coarse and asymmetric. This was fine and symmetric. Core-mediated, not muscular.

He balled his fists and the trembling stopped.

"Just tired," he said.

To himself. To no one. The phrase becoming a refrain.

He didn't notice the tremor. Or he noticed it and filed it under normal, the category his fourteen-year-old psychology assigned to things that happened after effort. Sufficient, because sufficiency was what he needed.

The settlers started down the mountain.

I followed, organizing my thoughts. Three twitches during stabilization, each stronger than the last. The hands were trembling symmetrically.

Qi-Clock, Stage 2. Confirmed.

The gong was still ringing.
