We found a house with four walls, two rooms, and a roof that stopped most of the rain. The southeast corner leaked. Calling it a good house would have been generous; a house was enough. The door stuck. Wei tried to fix it three times. It stuck three times.

The settlement was small. Thirty houses, a market, and a well. Mountains to the north, the same mountains we'd descended from, like an acquaintance met in a different context and found to look entirely different from the front. Forest to the east, dense with green, its trees thriving in tangled, uneven growth that no one had troubled to prune. The nearest sect territory was three valleys away. Close enough for trade, far enough for breathing.

We'd been here for how long? A month. Maybe forty days. Long enough for the word "here" to shift from "this place we're at" to "home," and the shift happened without announcement, as it did when people stopped moving.

I'd been here before. Eight hundred years ago, when there were fewer houses and no well. The mountains hadn't changed. Mountains don't. I'd told a farmer where to dig; the water table was obvious if you knew what to listen for. He'd dug. He'd found water three paces deep.

Wei trained. Outside. Barefoot on the packed earth of what we'd designated the yard, which was a generous term for a patch of ground between our door and the neighbor's fence. His qi flowed: clean, fast, and controlled. His movements were no longer a child's. He was fourteen. His body was catching up to his cultivation in spurts, with the awkwardness of a structure being renovated while still occupied.

I stood in the doorway, tea in hand.

This was our morning. The arrangement was comfortable. The word felt dangerous. Anything that assumed a tomorrow identical to today trusted the universe to maintain a pattern, and the universe had never maintained a pattern.

But this morning I stood in the doorway with tea in my hand, watching Wei train in the sun in the yard. The settlement was waking around us: chickens, a hammer somewhere, and a woman calling for someone who wasn't answering. One could get tricked into thinking that there was a pattern.

Wei had planted a garden. Three herbs in clay pots, arranged along the south-facing wall with great care, as if arrangement mattered.

Two of the three were wrong. He'd planted weeds, a variant of dock grass and something that might have been wild nettle absent the defining characteristics. He'd mistaken them for healing herbs. The identification process had involved confidence, enthusiasm, and the notable absence of reference materials.

The third plant was actually a herb. Lemongrass. By accident.

I hadn't told him. He watered all three with equal care, mornings and evenings. The wrong plants got the same measured pour as the right one, the same careful adjustment of the pot's position to track the sun.

That was Wei. Everything or nothing. Even the weeds got water.

After training, he disappeared as fourteen-year-olds do: into the vicinity of the nearest person who isn't their parent or guardian. He wanted an adult to exist alongside him for a while, without a lesson or an eye on his progress.

The Blade Whisperer. That was my name for him, the old smith three houses down. Lao Chen. His workshop was small. Dark. Full of the smell of hot oil, the conversation between stone and steel. He sharpened scythes, repaired tools, and talked to the blades as other people talked to pets, with endearments, encouragement, or the occasional gentle reproof.

"Every blade has a voice," he told Wei. I heard it through the window. The old man's voice, creaking at the hinges, warm where it mattered. "Yours is too loud, boy."

He didn't have a blade. Lao Chen was speaking diagnostically, as craftsmen diagnosed things beyond their stated domain because they understood materials and Wei was a material with a resonance.

He showed Wei how to sharpen a scythe. With patience, not force. The angle, the pressure, and the rhythm of stone on steel that was a conversation rather than an assault. Wei listened. For once, quiet. Attentive.

Through the window I saw a normal boy with a normal man, the ordinary transaction of knowledge from old hands to young ones, passed along in the small adjustments Wei made as he watched the smith work.

I could never have been that. The thought arrived as a simple fact while the stone rasped across the blade. No fourteen-year-old boy sat beside me to learn patience. My patience was geological and his need was human. Lao Chen could cross that gap. Ordinary gifts were the best kind because the people giving them rarely knew what they gave.

Wei came home for lunch carrying a piece of metal: a broken blade fragment, palm-sized, polished until the surface reflected.

"For the garden," he said.

"That's a blade fragment."

"For the garden."

He placed it among the pots. Between the dock grass and the lemongrass. A shard of steel in a garden of wrong plants.

I drank my tea. He ate his rice. The door stuck when he closed it. He hit it with his hip. The movement was practiced, automatic; a structural defect had become part of his daily choreography.

The sun moved. The shadows shortened. Somewhere, a child laughed.

Normal. Absolutely, terrifyingly normal.
