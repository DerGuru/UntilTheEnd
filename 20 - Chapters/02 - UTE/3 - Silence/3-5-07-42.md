Wei sat barefoot on the veranda, legs hanging over the edge, as he had at nine. The world had been large then, the veranda tall, and his feet hadn't reached the ground yet.

There was no way to know that this was the last evening. No, that's a lie. I knew. The signatures, the positioning, and the pattern I had seen before. In other lives. Other wars. Other operations that targeted, extracted, and eliminated. Tomorrow.

He had finished with training, sword-sharpening, bragging, and storytelling for the day. Now he sat watching the sun go down, low and yellow-orange. It hit the mountains and turned them gold. The gold would turn red, and the red would turn to night.

I sat next to him with a deliberate gap, the distance two people maintained when both knew and neither wanted to.

We were quiet. The settlement behind us hummed with pulsing barriers. Protection signs lay below hearing, and the escape routes were marked. Everything was ready for what was about to come. Everything except the boy next to me.

Wei breathed and suddenly there was light.

The warmth at his sternum, through his shirt. I'd catalogued it a hundred times: in sleep, in surges, and after fights or forced marches. Every time it had faded after a few heartbeats, sometimes as many as sixty. Then the dampening kicked in and containment resumed.

This time it didn't fade.

Wei glanced down casually. He regarded it like a lingering cough, a nuisance he had learned to live with, though his eyes kept returning to it.

Ten heartbeats. His jaw shifted. The glow was still there. He straightened and rolled his shoulders, the movement that usually shook it loose and reset the dampening.

The light held.

Twenty heartbeats. His eyes widened. Both hands on his chest now, pressing flat against the glow as if he could push it back in. The light bled between his fingers, steady and round, bright even against the setting sun.

He took his hands away and looked at them, then at his chest, then at his hands again.

Sixty heartbeats. His breathing changed. Faster. Shallow. The first time I had ever seen Wei afraid of his own body.

"What's—" He stood up, sat down, and stood up again. The glow followed him. "What's happening to me?"

He was asking himself as much as anyone. The question of a boy who had always trusted his body and whose body had just become something else.

"Wei." Quiet. The voice I used for stabilization, level and low. The frequency that said the situation was under control regardless of whether it was. "Breathe."

"I am breathing. It's not—"

"Slower."

He breathed. Faster than I'd asked. Then slower. Then something close to normal.

"It's a qi-surge," I said. True enough, with most of the truth carefully withheld. "Your core is recalibrating after the output these last seven days. The glow is excess energy finding a surface."

He met my eyes, wanting to believe.

"It'll settle," I said.

It wouldn't.

He sat back down slowly, lowering himself as if the ground had become uncertain. His hands gripped the edge of the veranda. The light at his sternum continued, steady through his panic, through the pressure of his hands, and through everything.

He was at rest, combat, training, and exertion over. Barefoot, just sitting. His core still glowed.

Stage 3. The light persisted, confirming what I had feared: the process continued between episodes, even at rest. Autonomous now. The degradation had become the baseline. The baseline was the countdown.

The silence between us hung thick, the silence of two people who could see the same light. It was the closest thing to honesty either of us had managed in many days.

"I'm not going to stop," he said.

He spoke toward the mountains, or to the light. To whatever part of himself was producing it.

"I know what you'll say." He picked at the wood of the veranda, eyes down, busy with something small. "That I should rest. That I should be careful. That the core—"

"I wasn't going to say that."

His head came up.

The light at his sternum was still there. The symptom had become a condition: a core consuming faster than it regenerated.

"What were you going to say?"

What would I say? The truth? That the light was the stage before the final stage? That the time between this light and the end was measured in days or tens of days instead of months?

That I could slow it? That my hands on his sternum, my qi in his pathways, could buy him time? Could I stabilize the cascade and extend the countdown? Maybe, if the attenuation held. If the river didn't break the reed. If I didn't break him.

So I was choosing not to?

The risk was real. I also knew what I was hiding behind it.

"That I know," I said.

A pause.

"This time I fight," he said.

The same words as in the forge. The fight was his choice. Non-negotiable.

"I know," I said.

The sun set and the mountains went dark. The light at his sternum burned on, faint and steady, on its own fuel. Finite as it was.

Stars came out.

And today would become tomorrow.

Neither of us moved. Neither of us went inside. Neither of us made the decision normal people make at the end of normal evenings.

We stayed.

The veranda. The dark. The stars. The light through his shirt like a second heartbeat.

Tomorrow would be battle. Battle was what he wanted. Wanting it was what made him Wei. Being Wei was what was killing him.

And the killing was what I could have stopped.

The stars moved. The night deepened. His light continued.

Somewhere in the darkness waited eight signatures, a commander, and a plan.

Somewhere closer, a sharpened sword leaned against a bed.

I sat between the signatures and the sword. Between the plan and the boy. Between the barrier-protected walls and the unprotected core.

Morning would come.
