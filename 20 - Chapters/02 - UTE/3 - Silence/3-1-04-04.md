One morning I walked into the kitchen and Wei was cutting vegetables like an adult. He'd taken over the cooking. The whole meal, on his own.

The local militia had started taking Wei for drills every three or four days. Basic sword work: footwork, grip, and angles. Nothing sophisticated. I'd allowed it because he needed the movement patterns and because settling down had created a problem: his qi built pressure without the constant travel to bleed it off. Structured physical training gave the excess somewhere to go. The militia didn't know what they were venting. They thought they were teaching a skinny boy how to hold a blade without cutting himself.

The knife moved in his hands. Fast. Precise. The same wrist rotation I'd seen in his sword training, and the same discovery: cutting was cutting, whatever the medium.

Carrots. Radish. Something green that I didn't recognize. He'd acquired it from the market or the forest or the neighbor's garden through methods he hadn't disclosed and that I hadn't investigated.

"How did you—"

"Taught myself."

Two words. Delivered without looking up. Without pausing the knife.

He'd learned from Auntie Huang, the neighbor three doors down whose primary characteristic was volume. Auntie Huang cooked as she spoke: with conviction and amplitude. Her kitchen produced sounds that traveled. Flavors that lingered on the tongue. Rice that had texture. She had taught Wei, or Wei had taught himself by existing in her proximity, which for Wei was the same thing.

"The radish is thin," I said.

"It cooks faster that way."

"You're cutting it on the bias."

"Auntie Huang says bias cuts release more flavor."

"Auntie Huang is not wrong."

He looked up briefly, pleased to have received validation from a source that mattered, then immediately embarrassed by the pleasure.

We ate together and Auntie Huang was not wrong. The radish was tender and flavorful. The carrots had a sweetness that surprised me. The mystery green was delicious, crisp but not raw, fresh and earthy.

In the afternoon Wei went out to train.

He trained differently now. The base exercises, the ones I'd given him in our first days together, were still there, but he'd adapted them to himself. Xu Ran's borrowed stability principles had been absorbed months ago into the foundation of his practice and were invisible now. These modifications were his own, innovations from a mind that understood its body's relationship with qi in ways I couldn't teach. Teaching it would have required experiencing what he experienced, and I experienced something different.

His form was precise and went beyond technique. The movements had become personal, a signature as individual and revealing as handwriting. Wei had translated the cultivation I'd provided into his own language.

I sat on the fence and said nothing.

He finished, wiped sweat from his face, and glanced at me, the reflexive habit of seeking evaluation that hadn't died even as the need for it faded.

"How was that?"

"Fine."

"Fine?"

"Fine."

He nodded, then went to the well. He drank water and came back.

"I changed the third sequence," he said. "The hip rotation, it wasn't right for my frame. Too wide. I narrowed it. Just a fraction."

"I noticed."

"And?"

"If it suits you better, it is correct."

He stood there. Dripping. Fourteen. A body growing into itself, discovering its dimensions through the practical process of using them.

He knew it was correct. He'd felt it in the movement: the angle was right, the qi-flow matched, and his hip turned naturally.

He didn't need me to tell him.

He didn't need me.

I went inside and made tea. The door stuck. I hit it with my hip. It opened.

The house smelled like radish. Bias-cut, by a boy who'd taught himself.
