Wei was at the door when he told me, "I'm going out." He'd told me, at least. Asking was another matter.

"Where?"

"Out."

He was already hitting the door with his hip. Already through. Out into the sunlight and dust, with all the energy of a fourteen-year-old boy encountering a day that hadn't been assigned a purpose. It was therefore available for exploration.

I stood in the doorway as he went down the path, past the neighbor's fence, past the well where two women were discussing something that required gestures and volume. He passed the market, where the morning's commerce was beginning, then crossed the settlement's edge. Trees and hills surrounded us, as they had before the houses stood here, as they would after the houses were gone.

He didn't look back.

He was certain I was watching and certainty eliminated the need for confirmation.

I drank my tea. It was cold, but I drank it anyway.

He was gone for several watches.

The time was not interesting. Unless you were a being that noticed absence as other beings noticed presence. I was sitting in the shape Wei had left behind, drinking cold tea and performing the ancient art of waiting.

I watered the garden. The dock grass was thriving. The nettle-cousin was producing something that might have been a bud. The lemongrass was lemongrass, doing what lemongrass did, which was growing at the same rate in the same direction with the quiet reliability of a species that had been doing exactly this for millennia.

I visited Lao Chen. I wanted company more than conversation. His workshop was open, the sound of stone on steel emerging in the rhythm of his daily practice. He was sharpening a hoe, treating it with the same reverence he applied to blades. For Lao Chen, all metal was equal and all edges were worthy of attention.

"The boy's not here," he said without looking up.

"I know."

"Good. He needs a day without supervision."

I left, walked back and sat in the doorway.

Evening came. The sun descended. The shadows lengthened. The settlement's daytime activities yielded to its evening activities with a smooth handoff.

Wei returned.

His shoes were muddy. His face was sunburned on one side, the side that had been facing south. Perhaps he'd walked east in the morning, returning by some circuitous route from the north. Either he'd circumnavigated something or he'd gotten lost and applied stubbornness rather than orientation.

He was grinning.
