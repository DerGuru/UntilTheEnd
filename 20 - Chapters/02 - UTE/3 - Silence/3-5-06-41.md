The signatures were multiplying around the settlement. Three became six yesterday and eight today. Observation had become deployment: bodies moved into position with the discipline of an operation that had been planned, authorized, and was now under way.

The triangle formation had expanded into a net: overlapping coverage, containment rather than observation. They had finished watching and were arranging.

A commander had entered the field. I detected his presence through its effect: the shift from autonomous positioning to coordinated positioning. The scouts now moved as a unit.

Wei knew.

The formations, the commander, and the details were out of his reach. But he had learned enough to feel the attention turned toward him. Fourteen years old, and trained enough to know he was being hunted.

He went to Lao Chen's.

The forge was dim and hot, always running. Lao Chen ran it as he ran his life: continuously, without breaks. Stopping was dying and dying was not in his schedule.

Wei sat with his sword, the functional blade Lao Chen had given him. Its steel had been folded, hammered, and cooled by hands that knew metal.

He sharpened it. Stone against edge, long strokes, even pressure. Metalwork required a patience counter to Wei's nature, but he practiced it anyway. The blade was the one thing he treated with unconditional respect.

"Sounds right," Lao Chen said from across the forge, his eyes on his own work.

Wei paused, then put his ear to the metal. Lao Chen's gesture: listen to the blade.

"Is it ready?"

"You're asking me or the steel?"

Wei gave the small version of his grin, the one that appeared when fighting was coming and the fighting was real.

"This time I fight," he said. To the blade. To the forge. To the coals.

Lao Chen didn't look up. His hammer found its rhythm again.

"Then fight well," he said. Quiet. Meaning everything.

I worked at night.

Wei slept with the core flickering through his shirt, the light now visible from across the room. Brighter again.

I slipped out of the house and into the dark beyond the door.

The settlement walls were low: stone and wood, agricultural boundaries rather than fortifications. Their builders had made no provision for defense.

I traced qi-barriers into the stone. Precise, burned into the material with controlled, ancient, efficient energy. Defenses that would resist qi-assault at any level below Immortal-stage. My hands on the stone, my qi in the stone, the markings taking hold under my palms.

Every wall. Every section. The perimeter, complete.

Protection signs at doors, windows, and the well. Each one set by hand, tailored to the angle, material, and vulnerability of its position. Craftsman's work, done because it was the thing I could do. Doing was the alternative to thinking.

Escape routes, three of them: north, south, and southeast. Each ran through the forest, marked with qi-signs on trees, visible to anyone with rudimentary sensitivity, invisible to anyone without. Wide enough for families, gentle enough for the elderly, long enough to reach the next settlement.

Watches. The entire night. I worked methodically, and each section of stone occupied me completely.

Everything.

I protected everything. Every wall, window, well, and path. Every door, every house, and every structure holding a person whose life was under threat.

Everything except.

His core.

I could have stabilized it. The technique existed; I had possessed it for millennia. An intervention that could slow the degradation, reinforce the structure, and buy time.

The problem was scale. My qi touching a Foundation-stage core was a river pressed through a reed. The technique required attenuation, precision at the finest boundary between my energy and his structure. One degree of miscalibration would shatter his core instead of stabilizing it. Instantly. The intervention that could save him was the same intervention that could kill him faster than the degradation ever would.

That was the reason. The real one. The technical, defensible, and rational reason.

And underneath the reason was the knowledge that I had attenuated qi before. My control was absolute. The risk was real but manageable, as all risks were when the hands had been doing this since before risk had a name.

The reason was the cover. The fear was the architecture.

Fear of what success would mean. I would have reached into his chest and touched the thing that was killing him. I would have called it by its name. The name was: I cannot lose you. And the name, once spoken inside his core, could not be unspoken. Unspeaking was the skill I had practiced longest.

I didn't stabilize his core.

I protected the walls. The windows. The paths.

Everything except him.

I finished before dawn and sat on the step with cold tea as the stars faded. The settlement was protected, barriered, and signed, as safe as I could make it.

Inside were the boy, the light, and the flickering.

The sword, sharpened and ready, leaned against his bed.
