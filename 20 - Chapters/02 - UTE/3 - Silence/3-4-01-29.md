The morning was wrong.

I woke to the weight of it. Qi-weight, the heaviness of qi-flows shifting in the region.

Like a storm that wouldn't arrive. The promise of violence existed as pressure rather than precipitation, atmosphere pressing down without release.

Wei felt it too. He sat up slowly, with none of the usual explosion of waking, his body registering environmental data before consciousness could sort it.

"It feels heavy," he said.

He was right. It did.

Outside, the settlement was already wrong. Things had changed overnight, and it showed in the mundane: the fields, the animals, the plants. A community that depended on stability had met the instability of qi-shifts.

The fields. Three of them: the barley, the root vegetables, the grain Auntie Huang called "the good one." Brown. Wilted overnight, the crops had collapsed cell by cell once their qi-nourished soil was disrupted. The plants spoke disruption in their own vocabulary. Droop, brown, fall.

Wei's garden. I rushed to check before he did and stood in the doorway, looking at the three pots.

The dock grass was wilting. Yesterday's vertical stiffness was gone, the stems bending toward the earth, heavy with water they couldn't process. The nettle-cousin was worse. Its ambiguous bloom, the small white flower, was closed. Sealed. Folded inward like something that had been open and no longer was.

The lemongrass was holding. Barely. The accidental rightness of this plant had proved more resilient than the undeliberate wrongness of the others. The one thing he'd planted correctly was the one thing that survived longest.

The animals felt it, too. Dogs were barking, the sustained, directionless barking of something wrong. Lao Chen's cat couldn't be found. Three chickens from Auntie Huang's coop huddled quietly on the porch. Birds that should have been loud didn't make a peep. Their silence was louder than their noise ever was. Lao Chen's cat, in fairness, vanished on a biweekly basis for reasons that predated qi-disruption and likely involved the fish stall two settlements over. But the timing was suspicious.

People had headaches and nausea. The physical symptoms of qi-pressure on uncultivated bodies. They felt the shift as discomfort, as malaise, as something hostile they couldn't name.

Auntie Huang was out early, loud, but higher-pitched than usual and edged with fear.

"Something's wrong with the water," she announced to no one and yet to everyone. "It tastes like metal."

Her methods lacked refinement. Her conclusions did not. I knew this. Wei knew this. It was like déjà vu, a pattern recognized, the understanding that this had happened before.

I tasted the water. Ferric, its mineral content altered by qi-flow disruption, shifts deep enough to reach the aquifer.

I reached for the qi-flows themselves, extending my perception outward and tracing the energy patterns beneath the settlement, the valley, and the region. They were moving, and the movement was structural, unlike the tidal shifts qi-flows made in response to season, moon, and the ambient physics of cultivation environments. The qi equivalent of a river changing course: underground energy streams that had been stable for longer than my last visit were rerouting, compressing in some areas and depleting in others.

I guided my senses to the source in the northeast, the qi-node that fed the valley, a concentration point where energy streams converged and distributed, the heart of the circulatory system for this corner of the world. It was destabilizing. Shifting rather than collapsing, like a bowl being tilted, the contents sliding to one side.

Wei came outside. His qi-sensitivity picked up the disruption, the wrongness in the ambient field. His face was serious, the grin replaced by context.

He knelt by his garden and touched the dock grass, the wilting stem between his fingers.

"What's happening?" he asked.

"The qi-flows are shifting. The source northeast is destabilizing."

He turned to me. Then to the settlement. To the brown fields. To the barking dogs. To the people holding their heads.

He stood.

He stood like someone about to act.
