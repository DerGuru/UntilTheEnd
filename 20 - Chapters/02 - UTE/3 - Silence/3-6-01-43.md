Morning came. A gray morning with no sun.

The sky was flat and low, the color of metal left in the rain, far from the golden mornings I'd spent on the step with tea and the feeling that things might be okay. This morning was their opposite, which had a certain honesty of its own.

I was high at the west wall, a position I'd chosen for the overview. Barriers hummed under my palms, the signs I'd carved last night, my qi in the stone, functional, alive.

The first qi-blast hit the horizon. East. A line of white-blue light cutting through the gray.

Then two more. Then four more. Seven total. Coordinated: the timing was the signature, each blast offset by precisely two heartbeats. A rehearsed pattern. Professional.

Guo of the Iron Lotus Sect. Of course it was him.

I'd met him once, centuries ago. A border dispute that neither of us had started and both of us had ended, standing on opposite sides of a river that was the excuse, not the reason. He had studied me across the water and known what I was. Nobody knew specifically, but he knew enough. A dog knows a wolf by the shape of the silence around it. He had inclined his head in recognition. Guo catalogued threats by precision rather than fear.

I had not returned it. I rarely did, with sect people. With most of them the omission was contempt. With Guo, it was closer to caution, reserved for competence I couldn't dismiss. He would sacrifice two of his own people to gain three heartbeats of tactical advantage and then sleep soundly, not because he was cruel but because the arithmetic was clean. I didn't like him. He didn't like me. It was the dislike of professionals who would rather the other didn't exist but acknowledged that the other did, and did well.

The well-gong rang, someone I couldn't see swinging the hammer. The toll spread across the settlement, bouncing off walls, off roofs, off the barriers, which carried it further. Alarm.

Movement below. Fast. Fragmented. The settlement waking into its emergency, the pattern I'd never trained but that humans adopted naturally under threat. Women and children ushered toward the marked escape routes, men running toward the walls, tools in hands. My protection signs at the doors glowed faintly as people passed through them, each activation confirming that the wards worked, and the wards working was the only thing working.

Auntie Huang stood at her door with Shen, the boy who didn't hear well and followed Wei like gravity was a person.

Auntie Huang pushed him toward the southern escape route. Her mouth moving. Shouting, probably, at the volume that was her nature. He resisted. She pushed harder. Her hands on his shoulders, turning him, aiming him toward the trees.

He went. She watched him go and turned back.

The qi-blasts came closer. At the east perimeter, my barriers absorbed and deflected. They would hold. Against this level, they would hold indefinitely. The blasts were cover. What mattered was behind them: the bodies moving in formation, the signatures I'd been tracking for days.

Eight. Plus Guo. Plus the deployment pattern.

Wei came through the door already standing, ready. The sword was in his right hand, Lao Chen's blade. His feet were bare, and the light at his sternum bled through his shirt in the gray morning air, a second heartbeat visible to anyone with eyes.

He ran.

Across the central path, between the houses, toward the east gate. Running as he always did: fast, direct, the straight line that adolescence believed was the shortest distance between two points, and adolescence was right about that.

He didn't look back at the house or at me. He'd done the same so often on his way to Lao Chen's, the morning receding behind him. Then it had meant safety. Now it meant the opposite.

I tracked from the west wall, the formation visible in its precision. The primary assault vector headed for the east gate. The secondary came in from the southwest, two signatures flanking. In the north, one, maybe two, held position.

Containment. Guo wanted something exact.

A qi-blast hit the east wall again. Stone cracked. My barrier absorbed the follow-through, dispersed it, scattered it. The crack was cosmetic; the barrier held.

Another blast. Another. Every two heartbeats, like clockwork, a professional unit executing its operation.

I held the high ground on the west wall.

Below me, the settlement responded. Settlers manned the walls, armed with farming tools: shovels, hoes, the instruments of agriculture repurposed as instruments of survival. They were no soldiers. They were civilians with steel.

Wei arrived at the east gate. He stood in the opening. His sword up. His qi flaring.

And west, behind me, 200 paces into the treeline and up the slope: something else.

A signature.

It belonged to nobody in Guo's force or the sect. Something larger. Deeper. A presence that displaced the air around it like an announcement.

*I am here and here is wherever I choose to be.*

I marked it, filed it away, and turned back to the east gate.

Wei was about to fight.
