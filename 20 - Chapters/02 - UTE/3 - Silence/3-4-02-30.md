The well was the settlement's gathering point, the only structure that belonged to everyone. Water brought them together now, a circle around the stone rim, the metal taste in their mouths, headaches in their temples, the dogs still barking.

The elder, sixty-three and weathered, held the authority of having been here longest and survived longest. He stood at the well's edge and said the thing no one else was willing to say.

"The qi is... wrong? Can anyone—?"

He stopped because he didn't know how to finish. His inventory held rain, drought, blight, the ordinary agricultural catastrophes with ordinary agricultural solutions, and no vocabulary for qi-crisis. This was the earth itself being sick in a language he didn't speak.

The looks went to Wei.

They skipped me. They skipped Lao Chen, whose metal-working touched qi but touching wasn't competence. They skipped Healer Mao, present but expert in bones and fevers, not qi-flows and geological destabilization.

To Wei.

Because Wei was the cultivator. The only cultivator, so far as they knew. The one who trained in the clearing, fought rock lynxes, and carried wounded woodcutters. This boy can do things. This boy understands the language the earth is speaking. This boy is fourteen, has scars, and has glowing hands.

Wei felt the looks. The collective attention arrived, pairs of eyes carrying the same expectation.

Help us. Please.

He stood straighter. A posture that said he was there, he heard them, and he would respond.

"The qi source is northeast," he said. "At the mountain slope. It's shifted, but if we can stabilize it—"

He stopped and organized his thoughts. "We might be able to reverse it."

He separated them into four groups. It was natural, unbidden organization from someone who didn't know he was organizing, because organizing was a function rather than a decision. He assigned them with the clarity martial training produced, the decisiveness of forms and practice and the understanding that in crisis hesitation was the enemy.

"First group: find the source. I'll lead. Anyone who can carry supplies."

"Second: secure the fields. Move what you can. The grain first; it's the most vulnerable."

"Third: anyone with medical knowledge, go to the sick. Water and shade for the headaches. Mao, can you?"

Healer Mao nodded. Already moving.

"Fourth: barriers. Dig channels around the north-facing structures. If the soil shifts further—"

He was right about all of it. The sequence, the priorities, the distribution of labor: he had assessed the situation, identified the resources, and allocated them. Not perfectly. There were inefficiencies I could see and he couldn't, because he was fourteen and I was considerably older. But they were minor. The structure was sound.

Auntie Huang stood at the edge of the circle. Her son was next to her, close for once. The wrongness of the morning had penetrated even his twelve-year-old imperviousness, and the boy who was usually gone by dawn was at his mother's side.

"The boy knows what he's doing," she said. To no one. To everyone. The Auntie Huang broadcast, delivered at the volume that ensured universal reception.

I stood apart with cold tea in my hand, outside the circle, outside the organization, watching Wei in the center, surrounded by adults who were listening to him. Fourteen, clearer than anyone in the circle, more certain than the more experienced. Certainty was his compass.

A brief warmth, rare, measured in fractions of heartbeats, the involuntary response of someone who had built something and was watching it perform. The teaching worked.

Then another unbidden realization arrived.

He didn't need me. Not here. Not now. Not anymore. Not in this circle where he was organizing, delegating, and being exactly what the settlement required. Sufficient.

That was the goal. Had always been the goal. But it stung anyway.

He was. Functioning. Without me.
