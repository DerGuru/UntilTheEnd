In front of the east gate's wall, on the road between the settlement and the treeline, in the space that was neither defended nor offensive but simply occupied. Wei held the ground, claimed by a barefoot fourteen-year-old with a sword and a core that glowed through his shirt.

He released his qi.

Everything. The full, undampened reservoir that had been building for years, the core too bright, too fast, too much. He opened it.

The wave rolled forward, visible as heat distortion in the gray air, the qi displacing atmosphere, bending light. It hit the treeline and the trees shuddered. Leaves fell. The ground vibrated.

The attackers felt it. Their signatures hesitated, recalculated. Three of them in the first wave: trained, capable, sect-bred cultivators on assignment. They'd expected an unstable boy. They hadn't expected a qi-wave that hit like a wall, carrying density that exceeded anything a fourteen-year-old should possess.

Two of them paused. One stepped back.

Wei didn't wait.

He moved forward into the killing ground between the gate and the treeline, the open area where defensive advantages ended and offense took the upper hand. He entered it without calculation or tactical consideration, with a directness his nature had refined into something resembling instinct. What looked like instinct was confidence.

His sword, Lao Chen's blade. Folded steel, properly balanced, a weapon that responded to its wielder's qi. Lao Chen's craft, alive in the partnership between steel and hand. Wei's technique rested on the foundation I had given him: the forms, the positions, the breathing patterns. Plus his adaptations, the things he'd changed because he was Wei and Wei changed things by existing near them. Plus instinct.

The first attacker engaged.

Wei was faster. He opened with a diagonal cut, low to high, the arc that exposed the opposing cultivator's qi-guard on the left side. The attacker blocked, barely. I think even Mao would have heard the impact on the west wall. Steel on reinforced qi. Sparks that were part metal and part energy.

Wei followed through with a second cut. A third. Efficient rather than elegant. Each movement was a decision, each decision executed before the previous one had come to a halt, continuous forward pressure that overwhelmed technique with speed.

The attacker went down, disabled but alive. Wei's blade in his shoulder, the angle that severed qi-channels without severing arteries. Controlled damage. Precise.

He could have killed and chose not to. That was control.

A second attacker came flanking from Wei's left. The angle that exploited the opening created by his first exchange. Wei turned instantly and blocked. The impact pushed him back three steps, heels dragging in dirt, bare feet finding purchase.

He stabilized and pushed forward. His qi flared in a concentrated burst. A technique I'd shown him for close-quarters disruption. The attacker's guard shattered. Fragments of qi-structure scattered like glass.

Wei drove the pommel into his jaw. Simple, brutal, and effective. The attacker went down.

The settlers stood behind him at the walls and the gate, in a formation made up on the spot. Civilians, organized, with tools and the willingness to stand. He was their point. Their shield. The sharp end of a blunt force that would not survive without it.

The third attacker approached, faster than the first two and better. A sect-trained combat specialist, his years of training showing in every fluid movement. He engaged Wei with a nine-chain northern style sequence, the pattern that targeted core stability through disruption of peripheral qi-channels.

Wei countered with something improvised, born in the moment, the textbook discarded. He found solutions faster than he could name them.

The exchange lasted four heartbeats. Six strikes exchanged, three blocks, one dodge. The specialist retreated two steps. Reassessing.

Wei was breathing hard, sweat on his face, the core-light at his sternum brighter now, pulsing with exertion.

A fourteen-year-old fighting sect-trained cultivators with bare feet and a borrowed sword, and he was winning. It was everything I had taught him and everything he had become on his own.

The settlers saw it. Their faces at the wall, at the gate, at the positions they held with farming tools: people watching something they didn't fully understand but trusted.

Wei between them and the qi-blasts.

The only protection they had.

He fought.
