Wei joined me a short while later. As if he knew we were done talking about him.

It was evening, so I kindled a fire, small and domestic, orange and crackling, the kind the settlement's houses shared with the night. Controlled combustion turned proximity into comfort and comfort into the illusion that proximity was the point.

Wei sat cross-legged on the porch like someone about to perform. The storyteller's arrangement, which in Wei's case meant leaning forward, hands active, voice set to the register that preceded embellishment.

"So the lynx came out of NOWHERE—"

"He was territorial," I said. "You'd been training in his—"

"Out of NOWHERE," Wei repeated. Louder, at the volume that said: correction noted and rejected, because my version is better, and better means the story works. "And it was HUGE. Like..." He spread his arms wide, a wingspan fit for something significantly larger than a rock lynx.

"It was wolf-height," I said. "Wolf-height isn't—"

"BIGGER than wolf-height. At least... look, the important thing is—"

"You rolled."

He stopped, a narrator interrupted at the wrong moment by the wrong detail with the wrong truth.

"I dodged first."

"You rolled."

"I dodged THEN rolled. You were there!"

He was right. He had dodged first. I was teasing him because it annoyed him, and that was entertaining.

"You rolled first."

A pause. He recalibrated, a storyteller whose fact-checker was sitting right there.

"Okay you dodged first."

He grinned. Victory on his terms: his facts, my facts, the grin sitting on his face.

"And when I hit it with my qi-strike. Full power. It—"

"Staggered."

"FLEW."

"It staggered, then retreated."

"It flew BACKWARD and CRASHED into the bushes."

We faced each other. The negotiation: his version (operatic, large, impressive) versus mine (accurate, clinical, smaller). It always ended the same way. His version went to the public, mine to the historical record, which nobody read and which was still the truth.

Almost a smile, the involuntary response to his exaggeration. He inflated every detail by roughly the same amount, consistently enough that I'd been tracking the ratio. It had become its own quiet entertainment.

"And then," Wei said, leaning further forward, eyes bright with enthusiasm and with the other brightness, the one I was counting. "Then I just STOOD there. In the middle. Blood everywhere..."

"Primarily on your left arm."

"Blood EVERYWHERE. And I looked at it. And it looked at me. And I said..."

"You screamed."

"I said, very calmly..."

"You screamed once, then stopped."

He glared. You are the worst audience.

"I said. Very. CALMLY. 'I can do this.' And I DID."

That part was true. He had said it, to himself, not calmly, but he'd said it and he'd done it. The doing was the truth underneath the embellishment.

He continued. The story grew. The lynx became larger with each telling; this was the third, and it had reached the size of a small horse. His hands now described teeth that suggested tusks, which suggested a different animal entirely, one that existed only in the narrative he was building. His narrative. His victory.

I listened to the story and, under it, to him. The Wei-frequencies beneath the performance. His voice was strong and present, full of health and energy. His qi hummed, elevated after the fight, running warm. His core sat behind the performance, behind the grin, behind the enthusiasm that was only the surface.

Four times in sixty heartbeats.

I counted while he talked, while he spread his arms and described attacks that hadn't happened in the geometry he was describing, while his face was bright, his voice loud, his story growing. The growing was the joy.

Four times in sixty heartbeats, his core twitched, behind his sternum, behind his shirt, behind the grin.

He couldn't feel it. He couldn't see me counting it. He couldn't know that while he constructed the narrative of his victory, true in its essence and false in its details, I was constructing a different one. True in its details, terrifying in its essence.

"—and then it just RAN," he concluded. Triumphant. A long stretch of telling for forty-five heartbeats of actual combat. The ratio measured how much it mattered to him.

"You rolled," I said.

He threw a twig at me.

Almost a smile. The same almost, the same permission, the same involuntary response to the same boy performing the same joy.

But behind the almost: the count. Four. And the count's trajectory. And the trajectory's endpoint. And the endpoint's—

*He's different. He has to be.*

The fire crackled. The night continued. He told the story a fourth time. The lynx was now the size of a bear.

I listened, counted, and held the almost-smile and the number in the same space, too small for both, containing both anyway.
