Wei drank his tea. I drank mine. We sat in the small kitchen that was ours, the place he'd chosen over the Iron Lotus training grounds and their impressive, uninteresting halls.

"They had real masters," he said. After a while. Into the quiet.

"I know."

"Formations I've never seen. Training yards full of equipment. A weapons hall."

He was taking stock of what he'd walked away from, laying it out between us like goods on a market table. He wanted to look at it once more, now that he was home.

"And you found it boring," I said.

"Boring isn't the right word." He turned the cup. "They looked at me like—like I was a thing. A number. Like they already knew what I'd become and were deciding if it was worth the investment."

He held my eyes.

"You don't look at me like that."

I didn't answer. The answer would have been complicated and I didn't want to bring that into the kitchen yet.

He went to his room. I sat and thought. I'd had enough contact with sects to recognize patterns in how they operated across millennia.

It was always the same.

A senior disciple identifies talent. The report reduces a boy to what a sect can use: qi-signature, range, signs of instability, and training level relative to age. His grin has no place in it. The hip correction or his grip on a borrowed sword matters only as evidence of ability. The report sets what he is against what he could become with proper resources, and the sect offers to close that gap.

The report goes on jade. Always jade. Paper is for correspondence; jade is for assessment. The report travels up a hierarchy to someone who reads it and produces one of three conclusions: ignore, monitor, or acquire.

Wei's profile was extraordinary. His sheer amount of qi, the signs of instability, and his previous training from a competent but unaffiliated source would not produce "ignore." I had seen this pattern play out hundreds of times. The variables changed. The outcome didn't.

Fang Liang had Wei's qi-signature. He'd been close enough for long enough that a senior disciple could have sensed everything. The friendliness, the mentoring, and the recognition were probably all real. They were also compatible with assessment. Warmth and evaluation were not mutually exclusive. That was what made good recruiters good.

I didn't know what Fang Liang had done after Wei left. I didn't need to know. I knew sects.

Wei's profile would be read by someone trained to read it.

That someone would belong to an institution with protocols for exactly this.

I had not warned him.

I was not going to warn him.

The reason was the principle. He had to learn to identify threats himself. Everything else would create a gap, and the gap would be lethal in the long run.

The gap might be lethal regardless.

I sat in the kitchen. Wei slept in his room, his core shining soft gold through his shirt.

Somewhere, a report would be passing from hand to hand. I couldn't see it, and I didn't need to. I had seen it over and over. The pattern was predictable.

I had conclusions. Wei did not. I was maintaining that asymmetry deliberately, as policy.

The policy was the principle.

And the principle might kill him. Or save him.

The gamble was all I had.
