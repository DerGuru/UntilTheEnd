Another gray morning came.

The settlement existed, damaged: the east wall cracked where the qi-blasts had hit, the south-west corner charred where the fire had burned, two roofs caved from the shockwave of Wei's discharge. Still, the walls stood. The barriers hummed. The protection signs glowed in the gray light, faint but functional.

And Wei did not exist anymore.

The garden. His garden. Three pots in a row, clay, unevenly shaped. He'd made them himself, pressed and dried in the sun behind the house. The blade fragment at the edge. The stone he'd found interesting for reasons he'd explained once and I had not retained.

The herbs: the dock grass was wrong, the nettle-cousin was wrong, the lemongrass was right by accident, the one success in a row of failures. He'd treated it no differently, because treating them differently was not his method. His method was equal attention, equal water, equal stubbornness.

They were all alive, green in the gray morning, growing in pots made by hands that were now torn, still, and cold in the dirt outside the east gate.

The herbs grew. He didn't.

Lao Chen's workshop lay across the path. The door was open, unforced, left by someone who had left quickly and who didn't care about a door when everything behind it was already complete.

Inside, the workbench was clean and organized, tools in their positions: hammer here, tongs there, files arranged by size. The forge was cold with the coldness of a fire that had been out for watches. Permanent.

The grindstone was still wet.

Recently used, the water not yet evaporated. He had been here, working, sharpening. Then he stopped, and Lao Chen was gone.

Gone. Evacuated, or departed, or removed himself from a place that contained a dead boy and a woman whose proximity killed grass. Gone like a craftsman goes when the workshop is no longer a workshop but a reminder.

The wet grindstone was the evidence of his last act. What had he sharpened? The better question was when. Recently. Before the battle, during the battle, after. The stone was wet. The user was elsewhere.

Footsteps sounded outside, heavy, with the rhythm of someone who walked because stopping was not an option, because options were a luxury she did not have.

Auntie Huang.

She came around the side of the house with a bowl in her hands, ceramic, the same kind she'd used for years, practical, unchipped, the cookware of a woman who maintained her kitchen as she maintained her children: loudly and completely.

Rice with vegetables. A simple meal that required no ceremony, because the morning did not permit ceremony.

She set the bowl at the door.

She did not knock. She did not call. She did not shout, and that defined the moment, because Auntie Huang always shouted. That was her nature, her method. The Loud Mother. The woman whose volume was the settlement's weather.

She was silent.

Behind her came her son, the boy who didn't hear well, who had followed Wei through the gate and been pushed backward by hands that were now torn. That push was the reason he stood here, alive, present, shaking.

He was quiet, like someone whose capacity for sound had been altered by proximity to a shattering core.

He had seen it, Wei's discharge, the light, the ugly, raw, non-golden light. It was inside him now, where sound used to live, replaced by what he'd seen.

Auntie Huang's gaze went to the door, to the bowl, to the empty space I would have occupied if I were occupying spaces.

She stood there.

Her face held neither gratitude nor grief, none of the named emotions people expected from mothers whose sons had been saved by boys who died. It was the face of someone who understood the mathematics, not the qi, not the cultivation, not the cosmic architecture of the situation. The simple, human arithmetic of one life given so that another one lived.

The one who died was fourteen. The one who lived was hers.

She stood there with that arithmetic on her face and judged it unpayable.

Then she went back the way she came, her son following, their footsteps receding.

Steam rose from the bowl at the door, hot food against cold morning, condensation that would thin and eventually stop.

Inside, I had not moved for watches. Since when, I did not track. The house was a room. The room contained an absence shaped like a person who was not in it.

The bowl grew cold.
