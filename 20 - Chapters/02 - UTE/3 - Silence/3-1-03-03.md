In the kitchen, he talked for half a watch.

Half a watch. Continuous. He had compressed a day's experience into pressure and was now releasing it.

First, he found a mine. "Abandoned, totally, nobody there for years probably, but the walls... Yun, the walls have CRYSTALS. Not qi-crystals, just normal ones, but they catch the light and the whole tunnel looks like—like the inside of something alive."

He gestured with both arms in a rotation that was either descriptive or gravitational, and took up most of our kitchen.

Then he went to a waterfall. "Three tiers. The water comes off the top and hits the second pool. The sound is—you know when qi flows through a channel that's slightly too narrow? That sound. But water. And loud."

His qi had "sung" there, he said. He'd felt the sound in his teeth. His core had resonated with the water's force, producing a hum through his chest and up into his jaw. The physical shock of that harmony had made him laugh.

Last came a shrine. "Old. Really old. The characters on the lintel, I couldn't read them. Worn down. But inside there was a feeling. Like—like an old man sleeping. Not threatening. Just. Present. And dormant. Like he was waiting for something. Or someone."

He paused, the first pause in half a watch. The shrine had exceeded his vocabulary, and the search for words was more honest than any finding.

"I left something there," he said. "A stone. From the garden."

"Which stone?"

"The gray one. The one I brought seven days ago."

An offering, from a boy who understood that some places deserved acknowledgment.

"I brought another one back," he said. "For the garden."

He produced it. Smooth, gray-blue, and the size of his palm. River stone, water-worn, shaped by a current too gentle to feel.

"Granite," I said.

"And?"

"Nothing. It's a good stone."

He placed it next to the blade fragment. Between the dock grass and the lemongrass. Our garden was accumulating artifacts: wrong plants, a right stone, and a blade fragment. He had decided the space was important and therefore deserved decoration.

"Can we get a proper pot?" he asked. "For the third one. The clay's cracking."

"Tomorrow."

"You always say tomorrow."

"Tomorrow I'll say today."

He almost laughed. The corner of his mouth moved the distance of appreciation without completing the journey to sound.

"It was good," he said. Quiet. "Being out there. Alone."

"I know."

"Not alone-alone. Just... alone without someone watching."

"I know."

He looked at me, and I recognized something in it from a distance: gratitude, maybe, or its quieter cousin, acknowledgment that the watching had never been a cage. He could step out of it whenever he wanted. That freedom was already his.

"Good stone," he said again, patting it. Then he went to bed. The door stuck. He hit it. It opened. He went through. It stuck behind him.

I sat in the yard next to the stone. Night fell and the stars came out. The garden held its quiet: three pots, a blade fragment, and a good stone.

The settlement settled. Lights went out. Dogs performed their evening ritual of barking at things that probably weren't there.

Forty-two days. We'd been here forty-two days. We had a garden with the wrong plants and the right stone. We had a door that stuck. We had a boy who went out alone and came back. The coming back was the part that mattered.

I drank tea. It was still cold.

I drank it anyway.
