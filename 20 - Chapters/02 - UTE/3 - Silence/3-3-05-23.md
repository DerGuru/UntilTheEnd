The lynx charged lower still.

Wei didn't dodge, didn't roll, didn't redirect.

He channeled.

Everything. All of it. The qi held in reserve, the stored energy training had taught him to hold back as backup. The emergency fund you didn't touch unless there was an emergency. And the emergency was now.

His right hand erupted in golden light, the full, unmodulated discharge of a core running at maximum without limit.

The qi-pulse hit the lynx mid-charge.

The animal stopped dead. Seventy kilos of enhanced predator in flight, committed to the attack, met the boy's pulse: force against force, and his was significantly larger.

The lynx flew backward. Three paces, four, five, tumbling, fur rippling, its qi-lines stuttering and failing, overloaded by the input.

It landed hard on its side in the undergrowth.

There was nothing: the forest still, the birds silent, the stream trickling, the clearing suspended between violence and aftermath.

Then the lynx stood, slowly, shakily, its qi-lines dim, its posture changed. The aggression was gone.

The lynx turned and walked into the undergrowth.

Wei stood alone in the center of the clearing, in trampled grass and displaced earth, his blood on the ground and on the shredded sleeve.

His left shoulder was open, diagonal and deep, a wound that would scar. His hands trembled with the post-combat vibration of a body returning from past its capacity. His face was bloody, streaked with sweat, dirt, and the red smear of a hand that had touched his wound.

And his expression: a grin.

The radiant, undefeatable grin of a fourteen-year-old who had just fought a qi-enhanced rock lynx alone and won.

"I did it!"

Loud. At the forest. At the sky. At me.

"I DID IT!"

Then his breath stopped.

Two heartbeats of full cessation. His chest went immobile, his mouth still open, mid-grin, frozen. His eyes unchanged, still victorious, still bright, still the eyes of a boy who had won and didn't know the winning had a clause.

Across eight paces, his core bloomed with sudden heat, radiating outward through his chest, shoulders, and arms. Painless and pleasant, sunshine absorbed and redistributed.

Prickling on his skin: goosebumps, raised by a qi-event.

Two heartbeats, and his breath returned.

He blinked. Rubbed his chest, casually, as you'd rub a stitch. Filing an anomaly under exertion.

"I'm tired," he said. To himself. To the forest. To the grin that was still on his face.

I uncurled my fists. Slowly. The crescents in my palms releasing as my fingers straightened. Iron on my tongue.

The qi-clock had started. The two-heartbeat stop, the warmth, the prickling: symptoms of a core exceeding containment, physical overflow. Stage one. The beginning of what I'd been watching for.

Him, grinning, bloody, victorious, rubbing his chest.

Just tired.

"Come," I said. "Let me look at your shoulder."
