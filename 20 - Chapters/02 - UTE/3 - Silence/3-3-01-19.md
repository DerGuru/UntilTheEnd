The clearing where Wei trained was at the forest edge, one li from the settlement, through the tree line past the stream that was too small for a name.

He moved barefoot on moss. Layers of green compressed under the feet of a boy who knew this ground now: every root, every depression, and every patch of stone that interrupted the softness with the hardness of geological fact. He'd trained here every morning for twenty-one days. The ground showed it: flattened moss, scuffed stone, and the worn track of repeated stances.

His qi flowed clean and fast, at a pace his body could sustain without visible strain. Practice had given him the rhythm; proximity to competence had refined it.

I sat on a branch three paces up.

His forms were completely his now. The foundation was mine: the stances, the transitions, and the structural grammar of movement I had demonstrated for him to absorb. Over that foundation he had layered his own adaptations, flourishes, and a dialect of combat, developed by spending enough time with a teacher's language to begin speaking his own. He extended the third position half a fingerbreadth past orthodox. He compressed the sixth-form exit. There was his hip correction.

My legs hung off the branch, my tea cold and forgotten at the habitual temperature of inattention, my perception split between two tasks. Half went to his form, comparing it to yesterday's practice, to the practice seven days before, and to the progression I kept measuring. It told me what I didn't want to know. The other half ranged through the forest, keeping watch whether I wanted it to or not.

His qi was faster than yesterday. The acceleration was subtle, measurable only by someone who had been measuring for months, down to the smallest change in how much he drew on his capacity. Yesterday he had left three parts in a hundred unused. Today, one. Almost everything his body could bear was moving through him.

For a normal observing cultivator this would be impressive. A talented fourteen-year-old whose qi-flow approached his ceiling with the grace that training produced. They would nod. They would say that he was well-taught.

But the ceiling worried me. He was safe within his capacity, even this close to the limit. At this rate, though, another month would bring him to it. He would still be fourteen. Ceilings broke when the force beneath them exceeded what they could bear.

He finished and stood with his eyes closed, his breathing even. He was as disciplined about recovery as about the exercise itself.

He opened his eyes, tipped his head back, and grinned.

The grin of someone who knew he was being watched. Who knew I was here. Always here.

"How was it?" he called up.

"Your sixth-form exit is rushing."

"It's supposed to be fast."

"Fast and rushing are different."

He considered this. He heard the difference between velocity-as-design and velocity-as-impatience.

"I'll fix it."

He would. He always did.

The sun broke through the leaves, warm and green. Filtered, dappled, gentle morning light that made the clearing feel contained and private, separate from whatever lay past the tree line. A good place. A safe place. A place for training, birds, cold tea on branches, and the persistent trickle of an unnamed stream.

It was the last morning like this.

I didn't know that yet. Or I suspected it; my eyes kept going past him, into the gaps between the trees.

Something was coming.

Wei went back to training, repeating the sixth-form. Slower this time. The correction a gift I gave without moving.

I drank my cold tea. The sun moved through the leaves.

Peace.
