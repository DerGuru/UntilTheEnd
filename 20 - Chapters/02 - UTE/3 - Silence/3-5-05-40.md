I stood outside in the afternoon when the air tasted of metal. A flash of iron on the tongue, a fraction of a heartbeat, then gone, like the memory of blood, a flavor too brief for confirmation and too present for doubt.

The sun was warm and normal, but under the normal ran a tremor deep in my bones. The ground was still. I knew this resonance from the passage of immensely powerful beings through a region, the disturbance reaching even this far.

An Immortal-stage cultivator was moving into the valley.

At this distance I got only the secondary effects. The displacement. The wake of a distant ship: a metal taste, a bone-tremor, and air compressed by proximity to something vastly beyond anything the local environment contained.

Before the mountains, I hadn't felt one in centuries. Immortal-and-beyond: cultivators whose power arrived as weather. They moved rarely. They moved for reasons, and their reasons were not small. Now it was the second time since the mountains.

I looked west. Mountains, forest, and sky. The cultivator was out of sight.

But the slow swell was unmistakable, like an ocean rising, the level climbing steadily.

One sentence of evidence. The metal taste. Then gone.

I went inside and said nothing.

Wei sat at the table for dinner. He was relaxed after training, calm and requesting fuel.

He held his chopsticks in his left hand: left-handed for eating, right-handed for fighting. He reached for the rice bowl with his right.

His hand went through the bowl.

Through the ceramic. For a fraction of a heartbeat, his fingers passed through solid material, flesh present where the substance did not register. His hand came out the other side empty.

His hand caught the rim on the way back. The bowl tipped. Rice hit the floor. Ceramic on wood, then the scatter of grains.

Silence.

Wei stared at his hand. Open. He closed it, opened it, and closed it again. He tested the mechanism. It was operational now, which didn't explain non-operational then.

His breathing sped up, the rhythm of a body that had just done something his mind couldn't process.

"That's not—" He stopped, looked at the rice on the floor, and looked at his hand. "That's not possible."

No. It wasn't.

"What was that?"

I put down my chopsticks. The careful placement of someone buying heartbeats.

"A phase-slip. Your qi destabilized your physical coherence for a moment."

Clinical. A healer's words, delivered by the person responsible for him. He had to hear them from me.

"Is this—" He swallowed. "Is this the next thing? After the tremors?"

Yes.

"It might not happen again," I said.

His face told me he heard the *might*. He reached for the bowl.

I continued eating with stable chopsticks and stable hands. Hands that could hold mountains. The contrast sat across from me with rice on the floor and a hand he could no longer rely on.

He picked up the bowl and scooped the recoverable rice back in. He didn't waste food, because food had been scarce.

He ate with his left hand, the right hidden in his lap.

I ate across from him. With stable hands, full information, and silence.

The taste of metal was gone, but the bone-tremor persisted. The Immortal-stage presence settled into the region like weather, altering conditions without announcement.

Two threats were converging: the sect closing in, with signatures multiplying at the tree line, and his deteriorating core. Grip-failure was the new entry in the vocabulary of Stage 3.

Stage 3.

Warmth. Tremor. Voice-shift. Grip-failure. Each stage produced a new symptom for the catalog I maintained. The catalog was filling up.

What comes after Stage 3?

After grip-failure, core-breach. I stopped there.

I stopped eating and set my chopsticks down, aligned.

"More rice?" I asked.

He glanced up, his right hand still in his lap.

"Yeah."

I served him from the pot, into the bowl his hand had dropped. The rice was warm and fragrant. Normal. The act was ordinary, and the ordinariness was the performance I could manage when everything else was converging. That, and the silence.

He ate. Right hand still in his lap. The hand was fine now. It would be fine tomorrow, and it would be fine until it wasn't.

I sat across from him and held my chopsticks. I held the silence. I held the metal taste, the bone-tremor, and the catalog's final entries. I held the sound of rice on wood.
