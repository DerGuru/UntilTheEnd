The market was Wei's territory now. Every three days he went back to the third stall from the left, where the dried-fruit vendor had discovered that this fourteen-year-old boy was a superior negotiator and an inferior judge of quality.

He'd claimed the place through repetition: going, coming back, going again. His feet had places in the dust now, and people knew what he bought. Regular presence established ownership without paperwork. He would have laughed if I'd called it that.

He went alone and brought back things. Some were correct: rice, oil, and the variety of dried mushroom that worked in soup. Some were wrong: a spice that turned out to be decorative bark, a vegetable already half-consumed by mold, and a piece of fish that had stopped being fresh yesterday.

But the wrongness was decreasing: each trip produced fewer errors, corrected by experience rather than instruction.

He haggled. I knew this because Auntie Huang loudly discussed Wei's bargaining with another neighbor while standing close enough to my window that the discussion was either coincidental or a communication strategy. She addressed every word to the neighbor. I heard every word.

"The boy got the rice vendor down by two coppers! TWO! That man hasn't budged on price since the drought. The boy just stood there and said 'that's fair for yesterday's rice.' The man looked at the rice, looked at the boy and—"

She laughed, delighted by competence and entertained by incompetence. Her range between the two was wide enough to contain an entire community's worth of opinions.

"He'll be fine, that one," she said. Nominally to the neighbor. Actually to me.

I didn't answer. Answers would have required participation, which was available but not sought. That was how I constructed my existence in a settlement that was not my settlement, among people who were not my people but who were Wei's.

Her son appeared. Deaf Boy, as I called him. His hearing was excellent; he simply selected which of his mother's words to acknowledge, with a thoroughness that suggested long practice.

He was eleven. Small, with enough energy for someone twice his size, which he spent on motion. He waited outside our house for Wei, sitting on the step, drawing patterns in the dirt, with the patient impermanence of a boy whose schedule was "until Wei comes back."

Two children were at the well. Younger, eight or nine. They saw me and paused, uncertain where to place me. One whispered. The other laughed. They ran off, still laughing, with the energy children brought to encounters with anyone who was not a child and was not explaining herself.

I let them go.

"SHEN! COME HOME!"

He didn't look up. The not-looking was practiced. Refined.

"That boy," Auntie Huang said, hands on hips, the posture of defeat disguised as management. "He hears when it's too late."

The sentence landed, passed through me, and settled somewhere I would find it later. I can still see the patterns he'd drawn in the dirt below the step. It had been such a casual thing to say.

I let the thought go.

Wei returned and they went to the garden. They moved pots, debated the identity of the nettle-cousin, and argued about whether the blade fragment should be repositioned. They were loud, emphatic, plainly entertained by disagreement and bored whenever they found themselves agreeing.

I sat in the doorway with my tea. The afternoon's assignment.

"Auntie Huang wants to know if you need anything," Wei said at one point, passing through the house to get water.

"No."

"She says you never need anything."

"She's observant."

"She says you should come to dinner sometime."

"Tell her maybe."

"I told her maybe last time. She said 'maybe' is the polite way of saying 'no' and she wants an actual answer."

"Tell her maybe."

He grinned. The exchange was a routine, and routines were a form of affection.

He went back outside. To Deaf Boy. To the garden. To the wrong plants, the right stone and the fragment of metal. The afternoon continued in a settlement where we had neighbors who asked questions. Where a boy went to the market and came back.

The sun went down. Deaf Boy went home, called, finally, by a volume that exceeded even his filter's capacity. Wei waved. Deaf Boy didn't wave back, which was either rudeness or the eleven-year-old equivalent of emotional reserve.

I drank my tea.

Cold, as always.

I drank it anyway.

As always.
