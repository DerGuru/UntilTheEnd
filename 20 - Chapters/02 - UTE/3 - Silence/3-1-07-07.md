Wei cooked dinner with the windows open, laying out his ingredients. Properly. With ambition.

Rice, washed three times, because Auntie Huang had explained that twice was laziness but three times was respect for the grain. Vegetables, bias-cut, because of course. A piece of pork that he'd bought at the market for a price he announced with the pride of a winning negotiator.

"Too much," I said, meaning the pork. The quantity.

"It's fine."

"We don't need—"

"It's fine, Yun."

The tone. The flat finality. He had closed the conversation simply by refusing to continue it. The verbal equivalent of turning your back. I did it with silence. He did it with repetition. Different instrument, same wall.

He cooked. I sat.

Outside, evening came. Light became less. Shadows became more. The settlement's daytime population became its evening population: inside instead of outside, seated instead of standing, and quieter instead of louder.

Auntie Huang's voice carried across the yards and fences.

"SHEN! COME HOME! NOW!"

Deaf Boy, somewhere between our yard and the next, did not respond.

Wei set the table. Two bowls. Two sets of chopsticks. The rice was steaming and correctly washed, with the texture that meant he'd learned the water ratio and the heat timing.

"Are we staying here?" he asked.

The question arrived between the rice and the pork, in the conversational space that dinner provided.

"Perhaps."

"That's not a yes."

"It's not a no."

"You said that before."

"And I'll say it again."

He ate, chewing slowly. The response was insufficient but acceptable. We had long-established rules about the things I said when I meant yes but couldn't commit to yes, and he knew them well enough to finish his rice.

"I like it here," he said.

"I know."

"The market's good. Lao Chen is—" He searched. "Lao Chen is real."

Real. The word he chose. Kind, wise, or skilled would have been easy enough to say. He meant that Lao Chen was himself in everything he did, as open in conversation as he was with a blade in his hands. Wei had spent more than a year with a being whose every word was chosen for what it could conceal.

"The food is good," I said. His rice. His vegetables. His overpriced pork. A small compliment, deliberately concrete, because concrete compliments were the ones Wei trusted.

He tried not to smile and failed. The failure was small, bright, and entirely adequate.

I did the dishes after dinner. Wei worked on a technique, the third sequence, the one he'd modified. He practiced the modification while I washed, his hands forming the positions in miniature.

"Why won't you just tell me if the angle is right?" he asked.

I rinsed a bowl and set it down. "Find it yourself."

"Why do you always—"

"If I solve everything, he never learns."

I'd spoken the thought aloud. My hands were still wet from the bowl.

And if he never learns, he dies the first time I'm not there.

Wei found the angle. Himself.

I sat in the doorway with cold tea in my cup. I drank it anyway. Stars in the sky. The settlement sleeping around us.

He'd found it himself. That was the goal. Always.
