The north slope gave way.

All at once, the instant of failure when qi-destabilized soil lets go. Rocks, earth, ancient root systems torn from their moorings, collapse expressed in mass and momentum.

Three buildings near the slope took the hit. A storage barn collapsed inward, walls folding with the finality of wood asked to bear more than it could. The half-built extension crumpled. The kitchen wall of someone's house cracked and leaned, held together only by stubbornness and mortar.

Screaming filled the air, the sound of crisis, universal, unchanged across every era I had witnessed. That sound never changes.

Wei ran to the people. The correct hierarchy. People first, structures second.

"You, get water. You, support the roof. You, get the children away."

Fourteen, and saying these things in a register of command that emerged because the situation demanded it. Natural, organic, what he was rather than what he'd learned.

The settlers obeyed. In the chaos, in the dust, the screaming, and the debris, he was the one person giving instruction that made sense.

He directed. He moved through the rubble, lifting debris with qi-enhanced strength, supporting a beam while three settlers pulled a man from underneath, compressing his qi into reinforcement for a wall that was about to fall on someone's children.

He showed competence. He inspired. Crisis energized him, his capacity expanding under pressure. His element.

I helped. Quietly. In the margins, lifting things from angles no one watched, reinforcing walls from outside while they reinforced from inside. Invisible, because invisible was my register and the foreground was his.

Auntie Huang appeared at my elbow. Her son was running somewhere, in every direction at once.

"Yun."

I turned to her.

"When he's with Wei, when they play in the garden or whatever, just keep an eye on him? You know how they are at that age. You turn around and they're gone."

You turn around and they're gone.

She said it casually. Domestic. Child management, the arrangement of mothers who shared a settlement.

I nodded.

Said nothing.

You turn around and they're gone.

The sentence sat heavy in my chest. It felt like prophecy, the forward-echo of words that would acquire meaning later, a meaning I could neither know nor prevent yet.

Wei emerged from the rubble, dusty and tired, his arms scored with small cuts from jagged debris. He'd pulled people from damaged structures, stabilized walls, and directed a team of adults twice his age.

His eyes found me across the wreckage, through the dust.

The grin. Dimmer, with exhaustion, the physical cost of being fourteen and saving things. But present.

I held the nod. The promise I'd made, casually, implicitly, to a loud woman about her son.

In that moment I believed I would keep it. But deep in my stomach I knew the turning-around would happen. That he would be gone permanently.

That was later, though. Not now. Not here. Not in this moment.

Everything that counted now was the buildings. The rubble. The people. Wei in the dust, grinning, having saved everyone he could reach.

You turn around.

And they're gone.
