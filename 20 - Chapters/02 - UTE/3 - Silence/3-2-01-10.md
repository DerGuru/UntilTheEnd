On market day we walked east to the larger settlement, a watch away. Bigger streets, actual buildings with second floors and the kind of infrastructure that said people had settled in long enough to invest in vertical expansion.

Wei walked ahead of me, three steps out, the distance that used to be one.

The stalls spread along the main road in the comfortable chaos of rural commerce. Spices: cinnamon, star anise, and something pungent enough to sting my nose. Fabrics: silk, linen, and the rough-weave cotton Wei had worn until twenty-one days ago, when he'd traded for a marginally less rough shirt. At the far end, the weapon sellers had laid out swords and polearms alongside farming tools that could become weapons with sufficient intention.

The crowd was loud, pressing, and wonderfully normal. People stood shoulder to shoulder, and every touch was a transaction.

Wei haggled. He was good at it; months of practice had turned instinct into technique. But the blade stone had caught his eye from six stalls away and desire was the enemy of leverage. He negotiated like someone who'd already decided to buy: going through the motions, cutting the price by a tenth when he could have managed three tenths and accepting the number the moment it approached reasonable.

"You paid too much," I said.

"It's a good blade stone." He held it up, turning it in the light. "Feel the grain."

"I can see the grain from here. You still paid too much."

"Then next time you haggle."

I wouldn't. He knew I wouldn't. The exchange was a draw.

He tried fruit. He paid first, then tried. The order of operations that separated transaction from theft.

"These are good." He held up something round and green. "Want one?"

"No."

He bought two anyway, understanding that "no" was sometimes just politeness and not a real answer.

A cart pushed through the aisle, too wide for the gap between stalls, driven by an old man who didn't care. Crates shifted. A stack of ceramic bowls on the nearest table wobbled, and the vendor lunged for them, swearing. One bowl escaped and hit the ground. The crack was clean and final.

Wei caught the second one. His reflexes were instinctive and fast enough to draw attention. He set the bowl down gently.

"Thanks," the vendor said, still swearing at the cart driver's retreating back.

Wei grinned at me, plainly wanting credit.

I gave him nothing. He didn't need it.

Then came the problem. His qi.

He dampened it. He knew how, had known for months: the technique I'd shown him compressed his signature so every qi-sensitive being within two li wouldn't feel it. But dampening required concentration. The sword vendor had a blade with a wave pattern; at the herb stall Wei recognized lemongrass and pointed it out as though I hadn't identified it from twelve paces away. The old woman selling dumplings had steam rising around her in a fragrant cloud. A boy his age, maybe younger, was demonstrating a basic form with a practice sword. He was doing it wrong in ways that Wei catalogued and almost corrected. The almost-correction was visible in the twitch of his hands.

Gradually, he forgot. The dampening thinned and his qi expanded like a candle whose shade has been lifted. A slow, warm brightening, invisible to normal perception and unmistakable to anyone with cultivation.

A middle-aged woman, around forty, stood at the weapon stall. Her sect robes had silver thread at the collar: Iron Lotus or adjacent. Inner Disciple level, maybe higher. My neck tightened as I turned toward her. Her eyes shifted directly to Wei, drawn to the qi spreading through the crowd. She looked, calculated, and looked away.

There was a man at the fabric stall, young, about twenty-five, his cultivation concealed but present. Foundation stage, a practitioner who moved through markets to watch people rather than shop. He had no obvious marks of any affiliation. His attention caught on Wei and held before he deliberately looked away. He'd noted him.

Wei was at the spice stall when the man brushed past him, close, closer than the crowd required. His shoulder clipped Wei's arm and Wei stepped sideways in surprise, but the man walked on without turning. Foundation stage. The brush had been intentional, a reading taken at close quarters, checking Wei's qi as you'd test a melon by pressing it.

I memorized his face, his gait, and the scar on his left hand.

The last presence was harder to place. It sat at the periphery, its source out of sight. Professional attention.

Three qi-sensitive beings who had noticed my boy between the sword stall and the dumpling vendor.

"The dumplings here are terrible," Wei announced, mouth full.

"Then why are you eating your fourth?"

"Terrible doesn't mean bad. It means they could be better." He chewed thoughtfully. "Different category."

"That is not what terrible means."

He ate a fifth and moved on. Oblivious to the people around him. He could identify a quality blade at ten paces but not a man watching him at two. Priorities.

Wei walked through the stalls and a spice vendor stepped back as he passed. A small, involuntary step, a body retreating from a presence too bright for casual proximity.

Wei didn't notice.

I did.

"Can we come back in seven days?" he asked, holding two bags with herbs, a blade-polishing stone and something wrapped in paper that he wouldn't show me.

"Maybe."

"That means yes."

It didn't. But he was right that my maybes had been trending toward yes.

We walked home with him three steps ahead. Both pieces of fruit were in my hand, because he'd put them there when I wasn't paying attention. My not-paying-attention was a lie. His putting was care, and care was the thing he did now that he didn't need to receive it anymore.
