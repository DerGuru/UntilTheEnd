It was evening when we arrived in our kitchen, with its clay walls, a wooden table, two cups, and a pot. The table had a gouge near the edge where Wei had dropped a blade during an indoor demonstration I had not sanctioned and that he had conducted anyway.

He stood while I sat.

"The Iron Lotus guy."

The opening move of a conversation he'd been assembling on the walk home, a watch spent mostly in silence.

I looked up. He knew I'd heard everything. And he knew that I knew he knew. The pretense of informing had been skipped, which meant this was something else. A request for permission to discuss it, to take it seriously.

"His word was 'wasted,'" I said.

"It's the word he used."

Silence. A conversation happening in two registers: the audible one, in which information was being exchanged, and the one under it, in which something much larger was moving between us. Tectonic and slow.

"They offer real training. Real masters. Not just—"

He stopped.

The air changed. His mouth stayed open a beat before he closed it on the rest of the sentence.

Guidance by you.

Three words. One pronoun. The pronoun that was me: the acquaintance, the teacher, and the whatever-I-was that had no title. Titles were for relationships that had definitions, and ours had none. The absence was intentional. The intention was mine. And I was now paying for it.

"What," I said. Flat.

He looked at the table. The gouge.

"They offer training," he said. Quietly. "That's all I'm saying."

I nodded without saying anything.

He looked at me, fourteen, brown-eyed, reading me as he read everything: quickly, accurately, and with instinct learned over months spent with someone whose emotions came in minute adjustments rather than statements.

"Are you angry?"

"No."

"You're something."

True. I was something. Unnamed. Drinking tea in this kitchen.

"I'm drinking tea," I said.

He almost smiled. He knew I was deflecting and seemed to know why. He was going to let me.

"Okay," he said, sitting down to pour himself tea. He drank, then looked from the table to me and back to the table again.

"I'm not going," he said. "I'm just—I'm telling you."

"I know."

"You always know."

"Not always."

He stood, stretched, and went to his room.

I sat in the kitchen with the gouged table, my tea, and the chrysanthemum, unused on the counter in a paper bag. The tea was cold. Because... well.

Not just you.

I heard it. In the quiet. In the kitchen at night where nothing happened and everything was about to.

I drank my tea. The cold version. The only version.
