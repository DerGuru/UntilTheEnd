On our next visit, Wei found the sword stall before I had finished looking at the herbs. Metal had drawn him since his hands were large enough to hold a blade. This stall had thirty of them.

I stood at the herb stall across the aisle, close enough. Five paces with a clear line of sight: supervision that pretended to be coincidence. I was shopping, of course, and could cross the distance in less than a blink if necessary.

He was examining a jian, holding it wrong deliberately, testing the balance from an unconventional grip. The vendor watched patiently; he had seen many boys hold swords and made increasingly few sales to them.

"Good blade."

The voice came from behind Wei. Young and warm, with a friendliness too even to be spontaneous. It reminded me of a carefully tended fire: pleasant enough, so long as you didn't think about who had built it for you.

He was a young man in his early twenties, carefully clean. His clothes were simple but fitted, and his smile was constant, as carefully arranged as the fit of his sleeves.

"Too heavy for your frame, though," he continued. Conversational. Light.

Wei sized him up fast, having learned early that evaluation preceded interaction. The young man seemed nonthreatening, knowledgeable, and interesting. Wei talked on.

"I know," Wei said. "That's why I'm testing the off-balance point."

The young man's smile shifted a hairbreadth. For the first time, his interest seemed genuine; the practiced expression had given way to something engaged.

"You test off-balance points?"

"How else do you know where the sword fails?"

A pause. The young man studied Wei's posture, his feet, and his hips. He read the body as a physician reads symptoms, his expression pleasant throughout.

"I'm Fang Liang."

"Wei."

"Just Wei?"

"Just Wei."

Fang Liang picked up a blade from the display, a short, light dao. He demonstrated a grip that was casual, but the casualness was performance. His technique was excellent, Foundation-level cultivation expressed through muscle memory, through the economy of motion that years of competent instruction produced.

"Iron Lotus," he said. "We're up the valley. You've heard of us?"

"Lao Chen mentions you sometimes."

"The old swordsmith? Good blades. Better conversation." His smile widened to a grin, warmer than before. Also calculated, but the calculation was better hidden.

"Are you a recruiter?" Wei asked with a directness I had tried to train out of him. He said what he meant.

Fang Liang laughed, caught doing what he was doing and pleased to discuss it.

"I'm a senior disciple. I notice talent. It's a hobby of mine. And if said talents are interested in us, then I can help them find their way to the sect."

"That's recruitment."

"It's appreciation."

They talked. I listened from the herb stall, examining dried chrysanthemum without giving it a shred of attention. All my perception was trained on the conversation four paces away.

Fang Liang was good. He reflected Wei back at himself, amplified, allowing the boy to see his own accomplishments in another person's interest. He asked the right questions and listened to the answers. The hearing was the technique.

"You train alone?" Fang Liang asked.

"Mostly."

"Who taught you?"

A pause. Wei was choosing what to share with a stranger, a category that required caution.

"An acquaintance."

The word landed, deliberately imprecise.

Fang Liang accepted it. No follow-up. He recognized a boundary and had the patience to wait.

"Your acquaintance taught you well."

"She's adequate."

I examined the chrysanthemum more closely. Adequate. From Wei it was high praise, with a diminishment just as clear. The word you used for things you'd outgrown. I suspected Fang Liang heard it too.

They talked for another quarter watch. Fang Liang being interested. Wei being fourteen and talking to someone who was not me.

I bought the chrysanthemum. I didn't need chrysanthemum. I didn't drink chrysanthemum tea. I bought chrysanthemum because purchasing was the cover.
