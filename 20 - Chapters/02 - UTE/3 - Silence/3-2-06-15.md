For two days, Wei came back from Lao Chen's with metal dust in his hair. He didn't mention the Iron Lotus or Fang Liang. He trained, cooked, and asked questions about tempering techniques that I answered with the minimum required.

On the third day, he returned to the topic.

He came in from his morning's training and stood in the kitchen. His shoulders were set, his hands hanging useless at his sides: the posture of a boy assembling a speech.

"I've been thinking about it."

I knew what it was. It was the only *it* in the room.

"About the sect."

I put down my tea, giving him my attention in a form he could see. He seldom asked, so it deserved the cup on the table.

"I want to know what you think," he said.

The request had been there two days ago, under his questions about training. My response, the seventh-form correction, had been an obvious deflection. He was fourteen. Old enough to hear one and young enough to insist on trying again.

I held his gaze in the morning light.

"If you want to go," I said, "I won't stop you."

A few words containing enough emotional material to sustain a lesser being for a month, delivered flat because the alternative was the tremor.

"That's not advice."

"No."

"What is it?"

I paused, genuinely, searching for the honest answer and finding it had contradictory layers.

"Respect."

He blinked.

"Respect?"

"For your judgment. You're fourteen. You've survived more than most adults. You're capable of making this decision."

He studied me. Parsing the response for subtext and finding more of it than he'd expected.

"You don't want me to go."

A statement, not a question.

I said nothing.

"But you're not going to say that," he continued.

"No."

"Because it would be... what? An attempt to control me?"

He understood better than I'd expected. His question showed what my silence had told him: that telling him what to do was the thing I feared most.

I feared his death more. But close behind it was the fear of him living as someone whose life was my construction.

"It would be unfair," I said.

"To whom?"

"To you."

He stood there in the kitchen, in the morning light, with the gouge in the table and the chrysanthemum still in its paper bag, unused.

He went outside, back to training.

I sat in the kitchen with my cold tea.

I drank it anyway.
