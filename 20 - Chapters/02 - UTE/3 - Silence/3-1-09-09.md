Sun came through the window, reaching across the floor to Wei's blanket. It was the kind of morning that made me think a whole day was available to us, with nothing asked in return.

Birds were calling outside. Three species, all identifiable by call. I hadn't slept, which was not unusual.

Wei woke, which started with a stretch. Full-body, total commitment, the extension of every limb to its maximum reach as though sleep were a container he was climbing out of hand over foot. Then a yawn. Then he opened his eyes, brown, alert, and bright with the particular brightness of a healthy fourteen-year-old after a full night's rest.

He sat up, pushed hair from his face, and noticed me in the doorway.

"How long have you been up?"

"A while."

"You should sleep." He said it as an observation.

"Tea?" he asked.

"Already made."

He grinned. The grin that said I had already done the thing he was offering and redundancy was funny because it proved we were synchronized.

He dressed fast, with the speed of purpose. Pulling on his clothes was already part of what he meant to do today.

"I'm going to Lao Chen's."

He stated it. The grammar of someone whose asking phase was over.

"Tell him the second-form transition still drifts."

"I know. I felt it yesterday." He was already moving, already at the door, already halfway into the day while I stood in the doorway holding tea. "I'll fix it."

I'll fix it. Once, from that doorway, he would have asked me to show him, to help him. Now he gave me a promise. The pronoun shift.

He stepped outside. Barefoot by preference now; his shoes sat by the door. Wei wanted to feel the thing he walked on because walking was an act and it deserved sensation.

The sun hit his hair, brown with the gold that sun produced on dark hair when the angle was correct.

He ran.

Full speed, rather than the easy jog he could have used. He took the distance between here and there as an invitation. Speed was joy, and joy was available.

He turned the corner. Past Auntie Huang's house, past the garden wall, past the point where the path bent toward Lao Chen's workshop.

He didn't look back.

I stood in the doorway with the tea in my hand, the cold version, the one that was consistently mine. Warm tea required timing, which required presence, and presence was the thing I was spending on him.

The dust his feet had lifted settled. The air was still disturbed by his passage: warmer, displaced, and faintly vibrating.

I stepped into our garden, with its three pots, its blade fragment catching morning light, and its stone.

Morning. This morning. The kind I wanted to believe in.

It's going to be fine this time. Maybe.

He was around the corner now. Gone.

He didn't look back.
