I sat by the fire that night with the words still echoing under my ribs. He wanted to touch the sky. The thought had left an ache like a bruise in muscle: invisible, persistent, and sore when I moved the wrong way.

Wei was in the village, helping repair a wall that had cracked in the latest tremor. The third in seven days, each one stronger than the last, each one met with less surprise and more resignation. The fire was low. I hadn't bothered building it up. The light was barely enough to see my own hands, which was exactly the right amount.

A voice snapped a name. Then the village noise settled back into its careful, measured rhythm, one people adopt when fear is too expensive to indulge.

He wanted to touch the sky.

I knew someone like that. Once.

The wind tonight carried dust and a faint metallic tang that sat on the back of the tongue. The same register of wrongness. A different hilltop. A different valley. A different millennium.

But the same hunger.

A man. Barely. Twenty-two, maybe twenty-three. I had to reach for his name, sifting through layers of faces, places, and names that had accumulated like sediment, each one pressing the ones beneath it deeper into the inaccessible geology of memory. The rest was intact. Brilliant. Young. Consuming. Certain. A cultivator at the edge of something vast, pulling everything around him into the gravity of his ambition.

I'd watched him for three years, keeping my distance, making observations, and leaving him to his work. He'd been interesting as natural disasters are interesting. Beautiful, terrible, and capable of occupying all your attention until you remembered the people in his path.

The civilization.

A whole civilization. A culture that had taken a thousand years to build, layer upon layer of art, architecture, philosophy, and music, assembled by generations of people who believed that what they made would outlast them.

I'd walked those streets. I remembered the sandstone glowing golden in late afternoon light, a quality of gold that I've never seen anywhere else, warm enough to make me stop at a wall with my hand against it. The libraries smelled of cedar and old ink; it clung to the inside of the nose long after you left. There were gardens on the rooftops. Fruit trees in ceramic pots. Flowering vines climbing trellises that hummed in the wind.

Children chased each other between the planters. Old women drank tea and watched the sun go down behind the mountains. Ordinary life, built on the assumption that the walls would stay where walls belonged.

The fire crackled at my feet. For a moment the two places overlapped, this hilltop and that one. The distance I had kept while watching. The cost of it. Then a coal fell inward, settling me back at the low fire.

The cultivator, the prodigy, had built his cultivation in a cave fourteen li from the city walls. Away from people, which was responsible. Not far enough away, which was mathematics.

I remembered his breakthrough at dawn, the humming in my bones. A frequency that started low and climbed past the range of hearing into the range of feeling. A vibration that turned solid objects into liquid and liquid into air. The qi-wave expanded from his cave in a perfect sphere, invisible and absolute. The boundary between before and after moved outward through everything in its path.

Ninety li. The wave extended ninety li.

The sandstone walls cracked first. Clean, precise fractures ran through the golden stone from foundation to roof. The walls held for a moment, trembling, then settled into their new geometry: broken but standing.

The libraries burned from saturation. The scrolls, dense with centuries of ink and thought, absorbed the wave's energy until their structure couldn't hold the charge. It turned to heat. Spontaneous combustion. Scrolls that had survived floods and wars turned to ash in heartbeats, the words on them briefly visible as the paper darkened. A moment of absolute clarity, every character sharp and legible. Then gone.

The gardens died in the time it takes to blink. The fruit trees curled inward, their leaves browning and falling as the qi pulled every particle of moisture from their cells. The flowering vines turned black. The trellises, suddenly unburdened, swayed in the shockwave's wind and then stood still.

The wave didn't stop at one impact. It rolled outward, flattened, and reflected off the mountains. It returned weaker but persistent, oscillating through the city until the structures that had only cracked began to fail.

The children ran. Most of them. Some didn't. I don't want to describe those. Restraint isn't mercy; it's triage.

The survivors had crawled from the wreckage. They rebuilt. They always rebuild. That's the terrible, resilient, infuriating thing about people. I remembered them stacking broken sandstone into lower, uglier walls. They cleared the library ruins and didn't replace them because the knowledge was gone. You can't replace what you can't remember. Nobody planted new rooftop gardens.

The cultivator, radiant, transcendent, rose from his cave and flew. He lifted above the valley, above the mountains, and above the smoke.

He didn't look down.

They never look down. You'd think that would stop being surprising. It doesn't.

A tremor ran through the ground under me. Here, now. Firelight jumped. Around my own fire, the baked earth gave off its dry smell.

I'd been on a hilltop like this one. The wind had been the same, warm, dry, and full of dust. Below me, the ruins smoked. I remembered a child, five, maybe six, standing in what had been a library, holding a burned scroll. I doubted she could have read it. She held it against her chest anyway, crying as the edges crumbled against her clothes. Something enormous had been taken from her world. She held what was left.

I could have stopped it. The breakthrough, the wave, and the burning. All of it. I could have contained the expansion with a thought. Less than a thought: a reflex, as you catch a cup before it hits the floor.

But then I would have been part of it.

Observing was easier. It was always easier.

So I sat on the hill until the smoke thinned.

And then I walked away.

Wei came back late. He paused by the fire as if he meant to speak, saw my face, and decided not to. He set a cracked tile down among the stones like an offering to a god he didn't believe in and went to sleep.

I fed the fire once. I wanted the light.
