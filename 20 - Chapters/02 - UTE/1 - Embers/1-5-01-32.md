Dawn light lay across the clearing when the ground moved. A tremor. Two heartbeats, maybe three. A vibration traveled up through the rock, the soil, and the roots to shake the surface under our feet. So much for the solid ground everyone relied on.

Wei was mid-step. Third count, right foot transitioning to left, his qi settling into the pattern I'd been hammering into him for many days. He stopped. The cup on the flat stone beside us rattled against its surface, a tiny clatter of ceramic. Somewhere in the village, a child cried out. Then silence.

Wei looked at me.

I hadn't moved.

"Earthquake?"

"Qi-shift."

He stood very still, processing. Then he went back to his stance. Right foot forward. He inhaled on the count. I didn't tell him to continue; he simply did. The tremor was over. The training wasn't.

Good.

By noon, the well was wrong.

Mrs. Liu noticed first. She noticed everything first, because she woke before dawn and treated the village's infrastructure as a personal responsibility. She hauled up the bucket, looked inside, and set it down without drinking.

I was passing the well when she turned to the Elder. "The water's off."

That was an understatement. The bucket was full of something that was technically water as mud is technically earth: yellowish, cloudy, and covered with a metallic sheen that caught the light wrong. I didn't need to taste it to know. The qi-saturation in the groundwater had crossed a threshold. Minerals that had been stable for decades were dissolving into the aquifer. The clean water was becoming something that would cramp your stomach and dry your mouth.

Elder Li leaned over the bucket. He'd been chewing the inside of his cheek all morning, a habit he retreated to when worry outpaced his vocabulary.

"Hasn't been like this since my grandfather's time."

His grandfather's time. Sixty years, give or take. Which meant the last qi disturbance in this region had been minor enough to resolve itself. A traveling cultivator, probably. Someone passing through, their breakthrough ripple touching the valley and moving on.

This wasn't passing through.

I walked the perimeter that afternoon. The herbs I'd been monitoring, the ones Wei collected, the ones I directed him toward, were worse. They had wilted before. Now some had turned gray at the edges, their structure collapsing as the ambient qi pulled moisture and vitality out of anything that wasn't strong enough to resist. Drained.

The trees on the eastern ridge were dead. Their bark was splitting, their wood had bleached gray, and branches snapped under their own weight in the still air. Trees that had weathered decades of frost were crumbling. I knew what a dormant tree looked like. These would never leaf again.

I counted, mapped, and calculated.

The qi-drainage radius had expanded by a third in seven days. Xu Ran was cycling faster, pulling energy from the environment to fuel his consolidation, compressing it, and preparing. The physics of it were simple: a cultivator approaching breakthrough was a siphon. Everything within range fed the process. Water. Soil. Plants. Air. If the cultivator was weak, the effect was negligible: a few wilted flowers, a strange taste in the well for a season. A strong cultivator could do far more.

Xu Ran was strong.

Late Foundation Establishment, reaching for Core Formation. There were tiers far above that, tiers that reshaped continents or split mountains. But this was a village without cultivation, without wards, without a single person above the first step of cultivation. His strength was enough. Enough to poison the well permanently. Enough to kill the harvest. Enough to turn a livable valley into a place where survival became a calculation rather than an assumption.

"Fourteen days," I said to no one. "Maybe less."

Wei was behind me. His qi-signature had grown more distinct as his training progressed, less background noise now than a specific frequency I could track without trying.

"Fourteen days until what?"

"Until the water gets worse."

He looked at the dead trees on the ridge. At the gray herbs. At the cracks forming in the frozen earth between the roots, hairline fractures, barely visible, but there. The valley was dying from the inside out.

"And the earthquake this morning?"

"Same cause."

"The man in the forest."

It wasn't a question. He'd put it together from the direction of the qi-pressure, the timing, and the figure he'd never been close enough to see clearly. The disturbances came from where the man was hunting. He was twelve, not stupid.

"What do we do?"

I looked at the village below. Twenty-three buildings. Smoke from cooking fires. Mrs. Liu hauling water from the stream now, since the well was unusable. Gao mending nets he'd barely used in seven days because the fish were gone too, downstream, where the water was still clean.

"You go help Mrs. Liu carry water."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know."

I didn't answer the question he was actually asking. The answer was still forming from pieces I'd been collecting for many days. I recognized the shape from a hundred other valleys, a hundred other villages, and a hundred other slow collapses I'd watched from hilltops before walking away.

Except.

The boy. The tea. The congee.

It may not have been a decision, or a conscious choice, or anything at all. But I stayed.

"Help Mrs. Liu," I said again. "The stream water needs to be filtered. Three layers: sand, gravel, and charcoal. I'll show you tonight."

He went. He didn't argue. He'd learned that when I repeated an instruction, the conversation was over.

I stayed on the ridge. Light pulsed along the horizon, faint, rhythmic, and steady. Like a heartbeat growing louder.

Fourteen days.

The valley had survived worse. Probably. Statistically. But statistics are cold comfort when the well turns yellow, the leaves fall wrong, and the ground shifts under your feet. None of that stability was owed to us.

I started around the valley, mapping the qi-flows, the fault lines, and the pressure points, the village always in sight. Knowledge first. Then action.

The congee had been terrible. But I could still taste it. You'll understand why that mattered, later.
