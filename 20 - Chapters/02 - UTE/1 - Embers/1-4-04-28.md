Wei's mother brought me congee in the late morning, when the sun was high enough to warm the clearing but not high enough to bleach it. She walked carefully, carrying something that weighed next to nothing and mattered enormously. The bowl was ceramic, the same one Wei had used, with the chip on the rim.

The congee was thin. Rice, water, and a few pieces of ginger sliced so thin they were translucent. Steam rose from the surface in delicate spirals that the morning air tore apart and reassembled. It was, by any objective measure, the poorest kind of food that a poor household could produce, the culinary equivalent of a prayer offered with no expectation of being heard.

She set the bowl on the rock next to me. The same rock. The flat one where Wei sorted his herbs, where the Caltharia mimic had sat before I'd told him it was poison. A thousand small negotiations between life and death had been conducted there in the language of leaves and stems.

She lowered her eyes to the bowl as she set it down. I'd seen the choreography of gratitude in a hundred thousand variations across every culture the world had produced, and usually I knew which response was expected of me. She nodded once, a movement I would have missed if I'd looked away, and walked back toward the village.

Her hands, at her sides, were cracked and swollen from washing other people's clothes, labor that destroyed her body one joint at a time and without which her family couldn't feed itself.

She had taken her portion of rice and given it to me, and she would carry that hunger into tomorrow's labor.

I looked at the bowl.

The pale ginger slices floated in the congee. The steam thinned as the morning warmed, until the surface was barely misting. The rice had cooked long enough to dissolve into the water, a porridge that was more texture than substance, more hope than nutrition.

I picked up the bowl.

The ceramic was warm, cooled just enough on the walk from the village to rest comfortably against my palms. She had carried it all that distance, a thin woman with her own supper. The weight was negligible, the contents negligible. Nothing about the object mattered except that it had been meant for me.

The congee was unnecessary. The gesture was not.

I drank.

I searched for a word for the taste. Simple was too cold. Warm was too physical. The taste was a handshake. An introduction between my body and a world I had been observing from the other side of a window for so long that I had forgotten windows could be opened.

The ginger was sharp. The rice was soft. The water was warm.

She had gone before I could give her an answer.

Stay.

I sat with the empty bowl for a long time after. The clearing was quiet, the quiet that follows a decision. Leaves shifted overhead, and the distant knock of a repair drifted up from the village. My palms cooled against the ceramic. Everything was where it had been when she arrived. I sat among the same things in a different position.

The old position: I am here because I have not left.

The new position: I am here.

The difference began with a single word: *because*. I had removed it with everything that followed: the escape hatch, the conditional, and the deniability.

I am here.

For the boy who ran like a disaster, fell like a punchline, and got up as though proving a point.

For the mother with the cracked hands and the rice she couldn't spare.

For the father who smiled through blood because his son was watching.

For the fragile, stubborn, and temporary village, *right there*. A short walk from a forest dying from the inside, in the shadow of a cultivator who was climbing toward a power that would scatter them like ash.

You probably saw this coming before I did. I stayed.

I set the bowl down. Ceramic against stone. A small, definite sound.

The ginger aftertaste lingered. So did something else: a warmth that had nothing to do with temperature, settling into a place I had left empty on purpose for longer than this village had existed.
