I did not follow him home. I happened to walk in the same direction. The path led toward the village and the village happened to be where I was going. Past it, to be precise. Through the area. In the general vicinity. Any resemblance to following was coincidental and should not be interpreted as interest.

The boy limped ahead of me. At some point, he'd stopped trying to talk. Each step on the swollen ankle cost him a wince, which he disguised by finding something interesting at the side of the path. He was bad at nonchalance. Most twelve-year-olds were.

The village appeared gradually: first the smell of cooking fires, then the sound of a dog barking, and finally the buildings beyond the tree line. Small. Wooden. They tilted in directions that suggested either poor carpentry or optimism about the wind. The roofs were thatched with river grass that needed replacing three seasons ago. A few scraggly fields bordered the western edge, their furrows muddy and sparse with the dregs of a harvest that hadn't been worth the planting.

But the smoke smelled good. Woodsmoke always did. Through centuries of changing languages, that small pleasure had survived. Something about fire and evening air. One of the few combinations I could still enjoy as readily as I remembered enjoying it.

Children played in the dirt near the well. A woman carried water on a yoke. An old man sat on a stump, mending a fishing net without looking at the knots. A girl, maybe six, chased a chicken between two houses, shrieking whenever it escaped her. At six, the chase could be enough. None of them looked up as the boy passed. He was a fixture. Part of the scenery.

I stopped at the tree line. Far enough to be unremarkable. Close enough to hear everything, because hearing happened to me whether I chose it or not, as reliably as gravity acts on rocks. The air carried smoke, sweat, fermented grain, and the sour undertone of poverty that smelled the same in every century.

His house was at the edge, close enough for me to peek inside. Calling it modest would have required more generosity than calling the village modest. One room, or two if you were charitable with the word "room." Walls of mud-packed wood. A door that had warped in its frame and needed to be lifted before it could be pushed. The roof sagged in the middle, its thatch dark above the uneven door.

He went inside.

The door didn't quite close. It never did. A thin slice of firelight leaked out and made the dirt outside glow a sickly orange.

The cough reached me before anything else.

It was a deep, liquid sound, with fluid rattling where breath should have moved freely. A cold would have passed more easily through that chest. Each attempted breath set off another cough, building until the man could scarcely draw air between them. Then came choking, worse than the coughs, as he tried to clear his throat of what his lungs were bringing up. What they were bringing up was blood.

Silence. A thin, wavering silence that was really just the space between coughs.

Then the boy's voice came close to the sick man, gentler than before. He waited for a gap in the breathing before speaking. Each word was careful, practiced. He had done this before. Many times.

"I brought something, Baba. From the forest. New herbs. Different from last time."

A rustling. Fabric. The creak of a pallet that was too thin to be called a bed.

A man's voice, weak, threaded with the residue of coughing: "Good boy."

I didn't need to see the chest to know what it held. I had lived through plagues that consumed empires. A man with failing lungs in a village without a healer who could treat them: I knew how that ended. His family heard the same cough each day. Even the boy probably knew, somewhere underneath the stubbornness.

The fire crackled. They were rationing wood, or couldn't gather enough. The boy moved inside the house. The hollow scrape of a lid being lifted: the provision chest. Then came the dull sound of wood against wood as the lid closed again, without a rustle of grain or the shifting of dried goods. The chest was empty, or close enough that the difference didn't matter.

The boy crouched by the fire. His knees scuffed the packed earth floor, and the quiet sounds of preparation followed. A clay bowl. Water from a gourd. Then the herbs from the forest, the ones he had picked that morning. These were harmless, at least. They were also useless for what was happening in his father's lungs.

He didn't know they were wrong. He worked with painstaking focus, separating stems from leaves, discarding the parts that looked questionable, and saving the parts that looked least questionable. He crushed them between two stones with a determination that substituted for knowledge. He dropped the fragments into the water and stirred.

The liquid would have been clear if the herbs had been right. It was cloudy.

A woman moved in the background. She was thin, her silhouette angular against the firelight, her hands red and rough from labor. She watched the boy prepare the medicine. Her gaze followed each movement without the attention he gave it himself. I had seen that tired watching before, in people who knew what a remedy could no longer do. The boy kept stirring.

"Here, Baba."

The sound of liquid against lips. A sip. A swallow that took effort. A quieter cough followed it, interrupting the next sip. Then came a sound worse than any cough.

A laugh. Small, warm, grateful.

"It tastes bad. It must be working."

The father's joke. The small kindness of a dying man pretending his son's efforts mattered. They held the lie up between them, one swallow at a time, because giving it up would leave the boy with nothing to do at that bed.

I stood outside. The evening air carried the bitter, vegetal smell of the failed remedy. I recognized it from a thousand villages across a thousand years: different herbs, different bowls, and different fathers. Children measured out the same small quantities, trying again each evening despite what the previous one had brought.

I turned around and walked back toward the forest.

The boy would be fine. The father would not. Winter would come, the herbs would run out, and the cough would deepen. The provision chest would stay empty.

I told myself it didn't concern me. Concern was the word I used, a comfortable claim to professional distance. Behind me, the child's voice was still clear when he said Baba. There was no expectation of failure in it.

I stopped in the dark between the village and the trees, listening to the silence that followed the coughing. Somewhere behind me, inside the house with the warped door, the fire popped. The girl with the chicken had gone inside. The old man's net was folded on the stump.

I had seen this before. A dying man among the millions I had watched come and go. A boy standing over a sick parent with the wrong medicine and the right intentions. I knew the pattern well enough to mistake that knowledge for a reason to leave.

I believed that for about thirty steps.
