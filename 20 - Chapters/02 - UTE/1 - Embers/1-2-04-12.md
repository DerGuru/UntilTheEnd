I knew the village as a cartographer knows a coastline, from the outside, with precision and without getting wet. From the forest edge I counted twenty-three structures, generously. Fourteen if your definition of "structure" required a roof that didn't leak. The fields to the west produced a harvest that could charitably be called meager and honestly be called insufficient. The central well was deep enough for the dry months, with little to spare. One road led south toward the market town, half a watch by cart or a full watch on foot. Most villagers made the journey when they had something to sell and nothing left to eat.

I catalogued these details as readily as the mineral content of soil, the movements of animals, and the shift in wind before a three-day rain. Information accumulated with every return to the tree line. I had learned the village without ever deciding to study it, simply by standing close enough for its habits to sink in.

The fisherman watched from his doorway whenever I stood near the tree line. His name was Gao, though I had learned it against my will. He stared at me like a man waiting for proof that his suspicions were justified.

He crossed his yard this time and stood at the boundary with his arms folded, blocking the narrow path from his house to the trees.

"You're here again," he said.

"I'm standing near trees. Trees are public."

"You watch the houses."

"I watch everything. The houses happen to be there."

He held the stare for three breaths before returning to his doorway. Suspicion was sensible. I might have been a bandit, a sect recruiter, or a woman carrying a husband's debts into somebody else's village. The truth would scarcely have occurred to him. It rarely occurred to anyone.

I stood at the edge in plain sight. I had stopped bothering with that sort of concealment several dynasties ago. The villagers could see me whenever they came to the boundary; so far, none had decided what to do about finding me there.

Mrs. Zhao lived three houses from the eastern end. She walked with her stiff hips ahead of her shoulders, each step short enough to accommodate the mismatch. She had offered me tea on four separate occasions. I had declined on three. The fourth time, she set the cup on the stump near the tree line and turned to leave.

"You'll catch cold standing out here," she said, not turning around.

"I don't catch cold."

"Everyone catches cold." She was already walking away. "Drink the tea."

The cup was ceramic, chipped at the rim, filled with tea that smelled of chrysanthemum and barley. Common. Warm.

I drank it when no one was looking.

I watched Wei's house.

Looking away from it took more effort than looking. The eastern wall had settled unevenly, one side sinking several fingerbreadths deeper into the soft ground. The house leaned far enough that the tilt showed each time I returned. The thatch needed replacing; the oldest patches had gone gray-green with mold. Daylight showed through gaps where the rain would come in.

Wei's mother was at the washing stone. Her hands moved with practiced efficiency, too used to the work to watch each movement. Skin split across her knuckles, raw from lye and cold water. Those cracks were part of what feeding her family cost. She worked in the same quiet as the women nearby. Conversation took effort they couldn't spare while more clothes waited at the stone.

Near the house, a figure too tall to be Wei and too thin to be healthy was splitting kindling. Wei's older brother, I assumed, from the resemblance between their faces. He was about twenty-five, with arms that showed more tendon than muscle. His axe technique relied heavily on persistence. Each swing was wrong, the angle too steep, the follow-through too short. Rot had softened the wood enough that it split anyway.

Another sibling worked on the roof, a young woman closer to the brother's age than to Wei's. She pulled away thatch that had turned to mulch, replacing it with fresh river grass spread too thinly. The repair would hold through autumn. Winter would bring her back onto that roof with snow and ice underfoot. By then the grass would be frozen, brittle, and difficult to work. It was all they had. She fitted it as carefully as she could.

"Hand me the long ones," she called down.

"These?" Wei held up a bundle of river grass.

"The long ones, Wei. Those are short."

"They're the same."

"They are not the same." She leaned over the edge to point. "Those. There."

Wei picked up the correct bundle. He had known which ones she meant all along; he had simply chosen rebellion over efficiency.

His father coughed.

It had worsened. Even from the tree line, without extending my senses past what a mortal woman could hear, the sound was unmistakable. Wet. Deep. Fluid caught in lungs that could scarcely move air past it. A forest remedy might ease that cough, but it couldn't give him back the breathing he'd already lost.

Wei came out of the house with a bowl. He carried it carefully, both hands wrapped around the clay, steam rising between his fingers. His father sat in a blanket against the house, in the strip of sunlight that lasted from mid-morning to noon. The walk from the door took seven steps. Wei counted each one. I knew because his lips moved.

The father took the bowl and smiled. It cost him enough effort that Wei waited until the expression was in place before releasing his hold on the clay. He drank. The liquid was cloudy. The herbs were wrong.

"Good," the father said. His voice was so thin the air escaped between the words. "Better than yesterday."

It was the same as yesterday. Yesterday had been the same as the day before. The cough had deepened with each, and the remedies were as weak as the cloudy liquid in his bowl.

Wei smiled back and held it while his father took another sip. People continued such exchanges long after a remedy failed, finding enough in them to prepare the next day's bowl.

Blood appeared on the father's lip. He wiped it with the back of his hand, low against the bowl.

Wei saw.

He walked toward the tree line, arriving in my general vicinity without quite looking at me. I recognized the distinction; I'd made frequent use of it myself. He stopped three paces away, facing the forest.

"His cough is better," Wei said.

I said nothing.

"It is," he said. Quieter. As if repetition could make it true.

"The herbs need to steep longer," I said. It was the most useless advice I had ever given and I had given advice to emperors. "And the water should be hotter."

He nodded before turning back. His shoulders were stiff as he crossed the space to his father, already preparing to make the next bowl.

I turned away from the village. Bright morning light fell on the broken thatch, the blanket, and the bowl Wei had carried. I walked into the forest, where the leaves softened the light and I could keep my eyes on the ground.

The herbs would help. For a while.
