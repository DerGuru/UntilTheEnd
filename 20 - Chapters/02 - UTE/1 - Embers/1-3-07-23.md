The fire popped between us while a cricket called past the stones. Wei sat with his knees drawn to his chest, eyes on me across the flames.

"You could stop it, couldn't you?"

I'd been expecting the question since he showed me the basket of dead herbs.

I said nothing.

Orange, yellow, and blue ran along wood that had burned long enough for the flames to settle around it.

"The thing out there." He tightened his arms around his shins. Firelight made his face orange against the dark. "Whatever's draining the forest, scaring the animals, and killing the herbs—" He stopped, then began again more quietly. "You could stop it."

Flames moved between us, lengthening and shortening the shadows over his feet. He watched me through the shifting light.

"I've seen what you can do. One finger. One touch. That boar could have killed me, or everyone in the village. You turned it to dust before I could blink."

A log settled. Flame caught along the new edge, bringing another small breath of heat across my face.

"So this thing? Whatever's out there doing this? You could stop it." He paused. "You could stop it and it wouldn't even be hard."

The cricket called again, one chirp followed by a long pause. I listened until the next came from the same place in the grass.

I stared into the fire. Wood broke down in the heat, giving off light as it was consumed. I understood the process in more detail than I could have explained in several lifetimes. So much of the world was made of things breaking down. Warmth came from it, then faded when the change was done. After all this time, I had found little that improved on that arrangement.

I said nothing.

Wei waited. He had learned at least that much from the training, sitting quietly while a question remained unanswered. His breathing was slow. His eyes were steady on mine.

He waited long enough that the answer became clear.

Then he nodded once. He had needed to ask me directly, whatever he'd already guessed. Now the question sat between us, acknowledged in the quiet. I had left him little room to doubt the answer.

He did not ask why. I couldn't tell whether wisdom or exhaustion stopped him. I had the power to stop the cultivator with less effort than drawing a breath. I had also spent thousands of years choosing to leave such things alone. Saying both aloud to a child whose forest was dying would have made them difficult to defend.

Every intervention I'd made had left me with more names I couldn't forget. I remembered them far more clearly than any improvement I had hoped to bring.

The fire burned. He went to sleep. I sat awake, which was redundant, because I was always awake.

The next morning, I went into the village.

They knew me as the herb woman, the quiet one from the forest edge. Gao distrusted me. Mrs. Zhao offered me tea. The children mostly ignored a woman who smiled too seldom to interest them. I walked between the houses, past the washing stone, and toward the chicken coop Chun was rebuilding. He worked on one good leg with a vocabulary of profanity that spanned four regional dialects.

The village elder sat outside the community hall.

His name was Li, a thin man whose eyes had been shrewd before they were old and were now both. He sat on a wooden stool too tall for the step under it, which lent his authority a precarious look that suited his position. He was shaping softwood with a knife sharpened so often that its blade had become concave. His eyes stayed on the wood when I approached, but his carving slowed, which was attention enough.

I stopped in front of him. Morning light crossed the village square, a patch of packed earth between the hall and the well. It served as marketplace, gathering point, and an arena for disputes that couldn't be resolved inside a house.

"The harvest won't be enough," I said.

He carved. A curl of wood fell to the ground.

"Store grain. Preserve what you have. The winter will be long."

This time he looked up, his amber eyes clear in the morning light. He knew as much about me as the rest of the village: a woman often seen at the tree line, with opinions about herbs and little to say about herself.

"How would you know that?" he asked.

"Experience."

A reasonable answer, although herbal experience scarcely explained such confidence about a winter still a month away. He studied my face. I let him judge what he'd heard without offering a qualification. Neither of us was inclined to make the exchange larger.

He carved another curl. "Experience," he repeated, handling the word as carefully as Wei handled an unfamiliar mushroom. He looked down at the wood, taking his time over whether the information was useful.

He looked toward the thin crops in the fields, then at the clear sky above them. There was little sign of rain.

"Thank you for the advice," he said.

I nodded, turned, and walked back toward the tree line.

Behind me, the knife resumed, slower than before. The pauses between strokes were long enough to consider a grain store or a winter's demands. I had given him something practical to think about, whether he believed the stranger offering it or not.

I thought he would listen. Calm could make advice worth considering when everybody else was frightened. In a small village facing a bad winter, that counted for more than a stranger's claim to knowledge.

I walked back to the forest. The herb woman. The nobody. The thing that had once stood on a hill while a city burned and was now giving grain advice to a man on a stool.

From this distance, I could almost find it amusing.
