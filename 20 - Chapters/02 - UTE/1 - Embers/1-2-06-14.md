The fire was Wei's idea. He built it with practiced efficiency, having made fires since his hands were large enough to hold kindling, which, given his hands, was recently. Small flames burned inside a ring of stones he'd carried from the creek. He had used the wood sparingly, placing each piece where it would catch. It gave enough heat to dry the herbs on the flat rock and enough light to see each other's faces in the gathering dark.

I hadn't sat by someone else's fire in centuries. Maybe longer. The warmth was different from qi-warmth, external, imprecise, smelling of wood and resin. It belonged to the pieces he had gathered, to the wind passing over them, and to this particular evening. I could produce heat easily enough. I couldn't reproduce the fact that he had made it here.

I sat by his fire while the warmth touched my skin. I let it.

The herbs dried on the stone between us, actual Mingwort this time, correctly identified and properly harvested. His hands were still dirty from the collection, dark earth in the creases of his knuckles. Over the last days, his success rate had climbed from one in six to something approaching one in three. He had a long way to go. He also turned up with his basket every day, making progress too small to impress me unless I compared it with where he'd begun.

His father's cough had eased. The real Mingwort, brewed correctly, reduced inflammation without curing anything. It gave him easier breaths for a while. His illness was still progressing, more slowly now, toward the same end.

Wei looked at the drying herbs with his brow furrowed. He knew what the small improvement had bought. He had done the work correctly, then watched how little it changed at home. He studied the plants as though another answer ought to be available if he examined them closely enough.

The crickets sang. The fire spat. Leaves stirred outside its light.

"I want to learn what you can do."

He spoke lower than usual, letting the sentence end firmly instead of lifting his voice into his usual half-question.

I stared at the fire. A log shifted, sending sparks upward into the dark. They died before they reached the canopy.

"Cultivation," he said. "I want to learn cultivation."

"No."

The sharpness of my answer was unusual. I generally had time to consider what I said, more time than most beings in this world could imagine fitting between a question and a reply. This answer was an old one, worn into readiness by repetition. By loss. By the students whose faces a fire like this could bring back to me. I studied the burning wood.

"Why not?"

"Because it would be pointless."

"Why?"

The fire crackled. The flames divided a piece of kindling into two halves, each burning at its own rate, joined by a bridge of glowing embers too thin to last.

"You have no talent."

The words were accurate. I had known his limits since I first extended my awareness toward him, as casually as glancing at somebody's shoes to estimate their means. His meridians were narrow. His dantian was shallow. The pathways a cultivator needed to carry qi were, in his case, a creek bed with a river's work ahead of it. He could cultivate. Technically. As a man with one hand could attempt the guqin, finding a way to reach each note at enormous effort. The result might satisfy him. It would give him little to stand against somebody more gifted.

Wei sat still on the other side of the fire. During our mornings, he usually occupied a silence by sorting something or finding a reed to strip. Now his hands rested on his knees. He took in what I'd said while the flames moved between us.

His mouth tightened. He looked younger, as plainly hurt as a child who had never learned to hide it. Then his jaw set and he lifted his chin, a small movement, steady in the firelight.

"Then show me something else."

"There is nothing else."

"You're lying."

The fire popped. Bark trapped under a log released its last pocket of sap in a small, sharp report that silenced the crickets for two heartbeats.

Twelve years old. Dirty. Thin. He sat across the flames with his legs crossed and his hands on his knees, his face lit from below by a fire he'd built himself. He held my gaze. I could unmake a mountain. I had told him no. He was prepared to sit there until I supplied an answer he could believe.

"You're lying," he said again, more quietly. He had watched long enough to be sure of what he saw.

I turned away. The crickets resumed their song and I looked past him into the dark. I had refused him in words I had used for a very long time. He was still there, waiting, with a reason to doubt them.

Wei sat cross-legged, hands on his knees. He did not ask again. I had heard him clearly enough, and looking away did nothing to remove the question from the evening.

The log collapsed. Sparks rose into the dark, leaving only the smaller light of the embers between us.

Neither of us moved.
