I saw him from an unremarkable hilltop, a bald crown of grass and exposed stone above the canopy. The valley stretched northwest toward the old growth, with mountains behind it. I had climbed for the view as a person rearranges furniture: a small occupation while I thought about something else.

It proved a poor choice of distraction.

Six li away, above old growth too dense to admit much sunlight, the air shimmered. From a distance it could have passed for heat above summer stone. The qi displacing it was drawn toward one point so rapidly that the air distorted around the concentration. The shimmer was quiet above the trees and gave little sense of the force under it.

Leaves spiraled upward from the canopy in a slow vortex. Dust followed, then seedpods, insects, and fragments of bark torn loose by the pull. From the hill, the motion was elegant, like a whirlpool seen from shore. Beautiful, if you didn't know what a whirlpool did to things caught inside it.

At the center, a figure.

A young man stood in a clearing scoured out by the force of his cultivation. The ground around his feet was dry and gray for ten paces in every direction. Past that boundary the forest still lived, with bare soil between him and the first surviving undergrowth.

Small arcs of qi discharge passed between his fingers, excess energy sparking from meridians strained by the power he was drawing through them. The sparks were blue-white, sharp, and controlled. He pulled qi from the forest, filtered it through his core, compressed it, and drew more. Each completed cycle gave him the capacity to begin another.

He stood rigidly upright, spine straight, shoulders back, chin tilted up, eyes fixed above the tree line. I knew that posture, and the certainty in it. He was looking toward the next stage of his ascent. The future was up.

Young. Brilliant. Blind.

I had worn that posture once, when I was smaller, more certain, and less tired. On the warm hilltop stone, I remembered believing that climbing was the only direction worth taking. Higher was better. The view from the top would justify whatever the climb destroyed. I had climbed higher than anyone. The view was not worth it. But you couldn't tell that to someone already climbing, eyes fixed on what lay above.

And you couldn't stop them. I had tried that, too. For a very long time. The stopping was what broke me, not the climbing.

He never looked at the dead earth around his feet. Perhaps he hadn't noticed it. Perhaps he considered the damage acceptable. The ground would be just as dead either way.

I watched him for a while. The vortex spiraled. The lightning crackled. The forest died in a circle that would be wider tomorrow.

Then I felt it.

A middle-tier spirit beast was moving south, fast, directly toward the village. It was a deer-type displaced by the depletion. Normally it would have posed little threat, but hungry animals driven out of their territory behaved unpredictably. Its ragged qi came through the ground as it ran, changing course whenever another exhausted patch of forest lay ahead.

I released a thread of qi.

It took less energy than making a candle flicker. I laid a false trail through the ground, giving it the qi texture of dense forest where this species could feed. The thread led northeast, away from the village, toward a section of valley that still carried enough energy to sustain the animal.

The beast turned. Its weight shifted through the earth as it found the trail, and its course steadied along the line I'd laid.

It ran northeast. Away from the village. Away from Wei.

I dispersed the thread until it was indistinguishable from the ambient qi.

It wasn't about the village. It wasn't about the boy.

On the hill, six li away, the vortex stuttered.

A hiccup. The sparks between the cultivator's fingers dimmed, flickered, and resumed. His head turned toward the space my thread had crossed. Qi had moved there against the ambient current, leaving a disturbance that should have been too small for him to notice. He went still.

Then the vortex gathered speed again. The sparks brightened. He resumed drawing qi in the same rhythm, eyes lifting to the sky as if the interruption were too small to matter.

But he had paused. And for one moment, he had looked down.

I noted it among the things that should not have happened and left it there. A beast had changed direction. Animals did that.

I stood on the hilltop, watching the young cultivator draw the life from the forest. Burned grass scented the breeze rising from his clearing. I found myself thinking about a city.

Burned grass had smelled like this on another hilltop, at much the same distance from what I watched. The ground under me was undisturbed there too, high enough to see everything below.

Four thousand years ago. Perhaps five. At that distance, whole generations could slip between the dates I thought I remembered.

A city of twenty thousand souls, or thirty, modest by the standards of the empire it belonged to. Clay walls reinforced with timber stood on river stone. Its market smelled of turmeric, cured leather, and a fermented bean paste made nowhere else. Afterward, nobody would prepare it again. Children played in chalk circles in the square, moving stones in a game I had seen in fourteen civilizations, each believing it had invented it. At the city's center stood a temple with a curved roof patched in copper. Age had turned it green. Afternoon light caught the verdigris in a shade I still saw when I closed my eyes.

A cultivator ascended.

He made his breakthrough in the fields north of the city, at dusk, with the last light on the temple roof. Too much power moved through too little wisdom. The release was violent.

Qi pressure radiated outward. The walls cracked first, clay splitting along weaknesses that had been invisible until then. Timber broke next. The temple's copper roof bent inward, folded, and collapsed. The sound carried up the hill as one long tearing of wood under buckling metal.

The market caught fire. Turmeric burned with a yellow flame that was almost beautiful. I tried to keep the memory there, away from the children who'd been playing with stones. Each time it returned, I tried to stop at the same place. The chalk circles came back with it anyway.

I stood on a hill like this one. I could have stopped it. A small intervention would have steadied the ascending man long enough for his power to settle. The walls would have held. The copper roof would have stayed green.

I watched. The city burned. The turmeric smelled like summer.

Three days later, I walked through the ruins. The copper roof lay warped in the courtyard, its verdigris burned to black. I stood over it, then walked on.

I remember the smoke. Not the faces.

Burned grass reached me on the breeze again. Six li away, the young man reached for his next stage while the circle of dead earth widened around his feet. I saw both the ground he had exhausted and the sky he kept looking at.

The memory receded. Its details lingered: turmeric, verdigris, and the silence I had walked through after the city fell. It took time before the smell on the wind was only grass again.

The young cultivator continued his work. The vortex spun. The lightning crackled. The forest shrank.

I stood on the hill until sunset, then went back to Wei. He waited by the creek with an almost empty basket; even the herbs near the village were thinning.

"The animals are running the other way now. They used to run south, but today they went northeast."

He noticed everything now, which was partly my fault and partly his nature.

"Wind," I said.

He looked at me long enough to make clear what he thought of that explanation.

"Sure. Wind."

I sat beside him. Apparently that was what I did now. I went back.
