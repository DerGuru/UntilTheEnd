I looked up from Wei and found them staring. Every one of them. It was the stare humans produce when something exceeds their framework before it has time to collapse and rebuild around the new evidence. Somewhere between fear and understanding: witnesses who cannot categorize what they saw, waiting for the category to arrive.

Elder Li was three paces to my left. His face was open. The careful, constructed face that he wore for the village was gone. The Elder's face, the face of authority, certainty, and calm that leaders manufacture from inadequate materials. What remained was the face underneath: old, tired, bewildered, and entirely honest.

He studied me as you study a landscape that has suddenly revealed a mountain you hadn't seen before, wondering how you'd missed something that large for that long.

He didn't understand what I was. He understood that I was more. That was enough.

Mrs. Liu stood behind him, arms crossed, the posture she'd held through every crisis. But her arms were loose, the tension gone. She held herself with the strange ease of someone whose defenses had become irrelevant. Her gaze moved over my hands, then back to my face.

Chun. Gao. Mrs. Zhao, her dislocated shoulder held against her body by the sling Wei had fashioned from a torn shirt. She was flexing her fingers, testing the arm in small, tentative circles that shouldn't have been possible with a joint displaced less than two days ago, though each rotation ended with a wince, the arc cut short by whatever damage remained. The ambient qi had touched her, the residual energy from the stabilization, the small amount that had leaked outward during the process. Enough for healing to begin.

She noticed. She glanced at her shoulder, then at me, and said nothing.

The children were quiet. All of them. The quiet of children absorbing the mood of the adults around them, a mood they can feel but not name. Xiao Mei gripped one of her game stones without playing.

I looked at each of them. Briefly, systematically, as I always did. They needed to be seen by me as I had been seen by them.

Wei's mother stood two steps away. Precisely two. One would have been intrusion. Three would have been distance. Two. The exact distance to be close enough to reach her son and far enough to leave the person saving him room.

She was rigidly, deliberately upright, spine straight, chin level, shoulders set by will rather than strength. Her eyes were wet, but the tears weren't falling. She had decided that breaking down cost more than holding together, and she paid for that decision without complaint.

Her lips moved. One word, formed but not voiced, the sound either too quiet to hear or too private to release into shared air. I understood anyway.

She held my gaze. I held hers. The exchange lasted three heartbeats. Five.

Then I looked down. At Wei.

He was sleeping, the deep, reconstructive sleep that follows trauma and precedes recovery. His breathing was steady. The glow under his skin was fading. Still present, visible in the thin skin at his temples, his wrists, and the backs of his hands, but dimmer. The body was doing what bodies do when given sufficient crisis and sufficient rest: adapting. Rebuilding. Becoming something it hadn't been before.

I folded my hands in my lap and watched the boy.

The village had its eyes on me.

I had nothing to say to them. No explanation would serve, no revelation help, no cover story about qi-resonance or herbal remedies, none of the comfortable fictions that might have bridged the gap between what they'd seen and what they could accept.

So I said nothing. I sat next to the boy and watched his face. I waited for his breathing to deepen, his features to soften, and the twelve-year-old to return from wherever the older version had gone.

The village watched.

Eventually, one by one, they looked away. Whatever they had witnessed had concluded. The woman on the ground with her hands in her lap and the boy sleeping next to her needed the small privacy the others could give by turning their heads.

Mrs. Liu was first. "Come," she said to the others. She picked up a bundle and began organizing the return to the village. Elder Li followed. Then Chun, though not before Gao caught his arm.

"More than herbs," Gao murmured.

Chun eyed him, then me, then the others, one by one, returning to the work of survival. The bundles, the water, the path home, and the small, persistent, irreplaceable work of continuing to exist.

Wei's mother held her place. Two steps away. Standing. Watching her son breathe.

We waited as the sun moved and the shadows shifted. The breathing held.
