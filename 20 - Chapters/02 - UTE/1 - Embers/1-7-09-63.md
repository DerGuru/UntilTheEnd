It reached me three li away, a stone still in my hand. Sound couldn't have carried that far through the landscape's shifting vibration and air pressure. It reached me as everything about Wei reached me: through the channels I'd opened, through the pathways I'd carved, and through the architecture I'd built inside him. Months of mornings, corrections, and the patient, iterative process of teaching a body to do something it wasn't born knowing how to do.

His channels filled, the slow, accelerating intake of ambient qi flowing into pathways too new, too open, and too unregulated to reject it. The primary meridian first, the central channel that runs from the dantian to the crown, then the secondary branches extending along his limbs into the fine network of tertiary channels that mapped his nerves. Each one taking in energy like a dry sponge taking in water: indiscriminately, rapidly, and past its capacity.

Full. Then overfull. Then approaching the threshold where organic tissue stops absorbing and starts failing.

Then: shutdown. The channels closed, the body's last defensive mechanism cutting the intake before the structure ruptured. His consciousness went with it. The perception went with it. Everything I'd carefully built over months of incremental, conservative development was suddenly flooded, sealed, and inert.

He was unconscious. Alive. His heart was beating, fast, stressed, but beating. His lungs were working, shallow, too rapid, but working. The channels were closed but loaded, carrying a charge they weren't designed to hold. The residual qi dissipated slowly through tissues that were adapting as I sensed them, restructuring down to the smallest fibers to accommodate an energy density they'd never been exposed to.

He might stay alive. His body was young, resilient, and adaptive. The channels I'd built were sound, designed for gradual expansion rather than crisis intake, but structurally robust. They would hold. Probably.

Probably was not the word I wanted.

"No."

I moved.

Three li. The distance between the river and the southern slope. The distance I'd have walked in a third of a watch. I covered it in the time it takes to inhale.

I was here. At the river, with stones in my hand, the smell of boiled water, and the fading signature of a cultivator who'd run from what he couldn't measure.

The three li between the river and the slope were irrelevant to a will operating on a scale where distance was a suggestion rather than a constraint. Then I was there. At the southern slope. Three steps from Wei.

The camp. The bundles. The people, clustered, afraid, some kneeling, some standing, all of them focused on the center of a small circle. An old man held a boy. A mother held the boy's face and called a name he couldn't hear.

No one saw me arrive. One moment there was a space: empty ground, air, and the absence of a person. The next moment: me. As though I'd been standing there all along and they'd simply failed to notice. Plausible for the woman I'd been pretending to be. Impossible for the woman I actually was.

Wei lay on the ground, Elder Li's arm under his head, his mother's hands on his cheeks. His eyes were closed, his face empty. His mind had retreated somewhere deep, dark, and inaccessible while his body sorted out the wreckage.

Under his skin: light. The faint, pulsing luminescence of overcharged qi-channels, glowing through his skin like veins of phosphorescence in cave rock. Blue-white. Irregular. He had absorbed more than he could process. The excess leaked through the only outlets available: warmth and light.

His hands were glowing. A soft radiance, visible in daylight only because his skin was thin at the knuckles and along his fingers. The qi-channels there ran close to the surface. Each finger had a line of light running through it, following the meridian path I'd mapped, pulsing in a rhythm close to the third-step pattern. Close, but broken: the qi repeatedly approached the baseline it had held before the flood pushed everything out of alignment.

I knelt beside him.

"Give me space."
