The underbrush shook at the forest's edge. The thing that came out was not a spirit beast. Spirit beasts had structure: tiers, patterns, and rules I'd been reading for millennia. This was something else. Something wrong. A forest cat caught in the displacement of the cultivator's hunting, soaked in ambient energy it couldn't metabolize. Its muscles were fibrous with unprocessed qi, and its eyes had the flat, yellow blankness of an animal that had gone past instinct into something that didn't have a name.

It was too big, too fast, too present. A forest cat small enough to carry under one arm had swollen to nearly three times its natural weight. The extra mass jittered with energy that its nerves couldn't distribute.

Wei was at the forest's edge.

He'd been collecting herbs, the good ones, from the stretch near the village that still held enough ambient qi to keep plants alive. Basket on his back, he crouched at the base of a birch, fingers separating leaves with the practiced care I'd trained into him. Serrated: life. Smooth: death. He checked every time. Every single time.

The cat came from the underbrush at a flat run.

Wei's training saved him. His senses, sharpened through many days of stillness and listening, registered the pressure shift of a large body displacing air, the wrong-frequency vibration of qi-distorted muscle contracting. He threw himself sideways before the cat cleared the leaves, his shoulder scraping the birch.

The cat's forepaw caught his left arm.

The sound was a dry, compressed *crack* that was too loud for the size of the bone it came from. My stomach tightened at the sound. Wei cried out, short and sharp, a breath driven out of him by pain. He hit the ground, rolled, and came up clutching his arm to his chest with his good hand.

The cat turned. Its mass carried it past him, claws gouging the earth as it braked. It swung around, tail lashing, and came back for a second pass.

And it stopped.

I was there.

I stood at the edge of the clearing, off to one side of them. I had occupied that spot a moment ago, or possibly not a moment ago. The distinction didn't matter.

I held still, my hands at my sides, my qi drawn in close. A mortal woman standing at the edge of a clearing could have done as much. I was just *present*, as a mountain is present, occupying space with a density of being that pressed against everything nearby.

The cat's forelegs stiffened, and its ears flattened against its skull. Whatever the qi had damaged, that much instinct was intact. The animal dropped lower, its eyes fixed on me, Wei forgotten.

It fled, faster than it had come, qi-enhanced muscles firing in a panicked, uncoordinated sequence. It crashed through the underbrush, snapping branches and scattering ghosts of birds. Then it was gone. The sound of its retreat thinned until there was silence.

Wei was on the ground. His arm bent at an angle that arms don't bend at. The forearm twisted outward below the elbow, the joint visibly displaced, and the skin was already swelling in a purple bloom that kept spreading. His face had the specific pallor that occurs when the body diverts all available blood flow away from the surface and toward the core. The core is where the important things are. Everything else is negotiable.

His teeth were clenched. His breathing was fast, shallow, and controlled, the breathing of someone who is managing pain rather than surrendering to it. His eyes were open, fixed on his arm, processing what he was seeing with the clinical detachment that shock provides for free before the bill arrives.

"Can you stand?"

"I think my arm is broken."

"It is. Can you stand?"

His eyes found me. The color was coming back slowly, in patches across his cheeks. He unclenched his jaw long enough to nod, then clenched it again, because opening his mouth risked sounds he preferred to keep internal.

I pulled him up. He swayed, then steadied. His broken arm hung at his side, immobilized by the protective muscle clenching around the damaged limb. He walked, and I walked next to him. The village was six hundred heartbeats away at an ordinary pace. Ours was the pace of a twelve-year-old with a broken arm, each step measured against his hold on consciousness.

The village healer, a man named Wen, examined the arm in his one-room practice that smelled of dried herbs and anxiety. He was old, experienced, and competent up to the borders of his competence. He palpated the joint, then prodded the swelling. He moved the forearm a fraction and stopped when Wei made a sound that wasn't a word.

"The joint is shattered," he said. "I can splint it. Set the bone. But the damage—" His glance went to Wei, then to Wei's mother, who had arrived before the messenger had finished catching his breath. "He'll lose full use of the arm. Rotation, grip strength. It won't heal right."

Wei's mother stood in the doorway. Her hands hung at her sides, shaking with a fine, constant tremor she pressed against her skirt.

"I need the arm," Wei said. Through his teeth. Through the sweat that had collected on his forehead and was running into his eyes.

The healer began constructing the splint. Bamboo strips. Cotton wrapping. A paste that smelled of camphor and old promises.

I waited outside the door. The evening light was wrong, warm across my hands while that crack still rang in my ears. The forest was silent at the edge of the village. From here I could see the birch.

Not my problem. The words assembled from habit, a four-thousand-year reflex. They didn't finish forming. You've heard me say that before. You'll hear me say it again. Each time, it means less.

Inside, Wei clenched his teeth and didn't cry. His mother shook and didn't cry. The healer wrapped the splint and didn't look at either of them.

I stood at the door.
