The village slept, its cooking fires burned out. The word makes it sound gentle. It wasn't. The village slept like exhausted people sleep: deeply, gratefully, without the luxury of choosing comfort over collapse. The last conversations had trailed off once everything useful was said. The windows were dark. The doors were closed.

I was on the hilltop.

My hilltop. The one with the flat stone, the view, and the grass pressed into my shape. The wind was warm. Still warm, always warm; the drought reached the air itself. It carried dust, always dust now. The valley was drying to its smallest root, the soil releasing its last moisture into a cloudless sky.

Somewhere below, a dog barked once, a single dry warning, and stopped. Nothing answered.

Above me, the stars.

I had stopped counting the millennia I'd spent under them. The constellations were different now. Shifted. Stellar drift, the slow migration of bodies across distances so vast that the movement was imperceptible on any human timescale. On mine, it was a calendar. Stars that had been fixed points in my early years had migrated, dimmed, and vanished. New ones had appeared: young, bright, and burning with an intensity that would diminish in its turn.

Stars died slowly, across centuries: a dimming, a reddening, and a fading to nothing. It was less dramatic than the death of a sandstone library, a rooftop garden, or a child holding a burned scroll.

Footsteps in the grass. Light ones. The third-step pattern, bare feet, the rightward drift I'd failed to correct.

"You should be sleeping," I said without turning.

"So should you." He sat on the stone, the distance exactly right by now: close enough to share the view, far enough to share the silence.

"I don't sleep."

"I know, but you could try."

"I don't want to."

He pulled his knees up and looked at the sky. Stars shone in his eyes, small and bright, the same ones I'd watched for millennia, reduced to twin points in a twelve-year-old's gaze.

"Something's wrong," he said. Not a question.

"Something is always wrong."

"No." He tilted his head toward the eastern horizon, where the qi-pulse had settled into its final rhythm. "Something is wrong *there*."

I said nothing. The rightness of it sat between us like a stone neither of us wanted to pick up.

"Go to sleep, Wei."

He sat another sixty heartbeats. Then he stood, brushed grass from his legs, and walked down the hill. At the bottom, he turned.

"You're not leaving, are you?"

The question landed in my chest.

"Go to sleep," I said again.

He went.

The horizon pulsed.

Xu Ran. His qi-signature was dense, compressed, and regular. The consolidation was nearly complete. The energy he'd gathered from the spirit beast cores, from the environment, and from the valley itself was coiling inward. It tightened toward the expansion that would come when the structure reached its limit. The boundary between what he was and what he was becoming would dissolve.

Days.

The math was simple. Five li, possibly six. The village: two li from his position. Inside the zone.

My barriers would take the edge off: stone walls, trenches, and redirected flow. Then the rest would arrive anyway. The well water would turn into something you drank only if you had no choice. The well Mrs. Liu had dug deeper with such practical determination would be ruined for good. Roof beams that had held through drought would split. Children would wake with headaches and bile. They'd call it sickness because that was the only word they'd have.

The village would survive the breakthrough.

It would not survive the winter.

I could leave.

The thought arrived comfortable and worn, a coat I'd been putting on and taking off for what feels like forever now. I could leave. I could walk down the hill, cross the valley, and follow the river south until the pulsing faded. The air would clear. The village would become another place I'd been. Another valley. Another collection of warm lights in the dark, growing smaller with distance, growing easier to forget with each step.

I'd done it before. Hundreds of times. The mechanics were simple: I stood up and walked. I didn't turn around.

I could leave.

The thought sat in my chest. I held it there, examining it like an artifact that used to be functional and was now merely historical. It still fit. It still had its old weight, its old logic, and its old promise of unburdened existence.

But it didn't produce the old certainty.

The almost-laugh. The rooster. The boy asking the fire what I was, because the fire was safer to ask than me.

"Still counts," he'd said. He'd meant it.

I stood up. The grass lay flattened, a negative of my body, an absence with boundaries. The wind pushed at me from the east, carrying Xu Ran's qi, the dust, and the smell of a valley that was dying slowly. Soon it would die faster.

I didn't leave.

Staying wasn't noble. Nobility was another coat people put on when they wanted to feel warmer than they were.

I didn't leave because leaving was the one thing I'd always been good at and I was tired of being good at it.

You know how this ends. But I didn't know, then. Not yet.

I didn't leave because Mrs. Liu was deepening the well with bare hands. Mrs. Zhao's rooster was testing the fence again; Elder Li was chewing his cheek. The village was surviving, stubbornly, impossibly, in the face of forces it couldn't see, fight, or understand.

I didn't leave because soon something was going to happen. The barriers weren't going to be enough. I was going to have to choose between the woman I'd been for thousands of years and the woman who drank congee. Who replaced roof tiles, chased roosters, and almost laughed when a twelve-year-old boy told her she was in a bad mood.

I stood on the hilltop. The stars turned overhead, slowly, imperceptibly. Those ancient cycles had continued through every decision I'd ever made on a hill.

Soon.

The wind blew. From the east, carrying ozone, dust, and the future.

*You're not leaving, are you?*

I did not leave.
