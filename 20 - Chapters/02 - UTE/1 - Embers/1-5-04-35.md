Wei found the barriers by mid-morning, following the altered currents along the hillside. I'd underestimated him or, more accurately, I'd underestimated what second-step sensitivity looked like in a boy who'd spent his entire life navigating a world that didn't explain itself. He walked the perimeter I'd walked and felt for the places where the qi-flow changed direction.

"Here," he said, standing on the ridge by the first stone wall. "It goes that way now." He pointed south. "It used to go—" He turned, frowning. "Toward the village?"

"Yes."

"You moved the river."

"I moved the qi. The river follows."

He crouched at the wall, touching the stones. He ran his fingers along the seams where I'd fitted granite against granite, each piece chosen for density and mineral composition, each angle calculated to deflect rather than absorb. His fingers stopped at a spot where two stones met imperfectly, a gap I'd known about and accepted because perfection in fieldwork was the enemy of completion.

"Can I practice here?"

I hadn't anticipated that. I'd built the barriers to protect the village; he saw something to run along. The question wasn't unreasonable. The walls were low enough, the terrain was uneven enough to challenge balance, and the qi-flow patterns I'd created made the area a natural obstacle course for anyone sensitive enough to feel the currents.

"Don't break anything."

He grinned. The grin of a twelve-year-old who had just been given permission to climb on something adults had built.

He started with the walls, using qi-steps along the narrow stone surface. The same pattern as the river exercise, but harder. The stones were uneven, each one a different height and angle. The qi flowing around them created eddies that pushed at his feet like invisible hands. He fell off within ten steps. He stood up, then restarted. He fell at twelve. He restarted, then fell at eight. Regression, frustration, and the temporary worsening that comes before adaptation.

I sat on the hillside and watched.

He found his rhythm on the fourth attempt. He was beginning to respond to the terrain, although each new stretch of stone still caught him out. His feet angled toward the flatter surfaces before landing, the pressure through his soles changing as he sensed the next stone. His breathing steadied. The count held.

By noon, he was running.

He crashed through the qi-eddies with the subtlety of a thrown brick. His footwork was still raw, and he nearly broke his ankle on the second wall's corner. But he was running on a surface that wasn't designed for running, through qi-currents that pushed against him. He was keeping the count while he did it.

"Left foot," I called.

"I know!"

"Then fix it."

He fixed it. Mostly.

He spent the afternoon on the trenches, jumping across, landing on the far side, turning, and jumping back. The trench was narrow enough that the jump was easy in physical terms but tricky in qi terms: the flow through the channel acted as a barrier he had to push through mid-air, a resistance that slowed him just enough to make the landing unpredictable.

"This is harder than the river."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because the river's flow is natural. This is redirected. The qi is forced against its old currents. It resists."

He thought about that, jumped again, and landed cleaner.

"Like training someone who doesn't want to learn?"

"I wouldn't know anything about that."

He missed the landing on the next jump. I was almost certain it was deliberate.

The evening brought the villagers together.

Elder Li had called a gathering. They discussed the problem as small communities do: standing in someone's yard, talking over each other, and reaching consensus through volume rather than vote.

The well was still producing yellow water. The stream filter was Wei's design, built to my instructions. It worked, but the output was slow, a trickle where the village needed a flow. The dried fish stocks were holding, thanks to Gao's paranoid preparation habits. The grain reserves were adequate for a month, maybe forty-two days if they rationed.

But the water.

I watched from the edge of the gathering, close enough to hear, distant enough to not participate. The drought had cracked the soil in patterns that followed the qi-drainage lines, deep fissures running northeast to southwest, pulling moisture out of the earth faster than the melting snow could replace it. There hadn't been snow in nine days. The sky was clear and blue. Farmers kept looking up at it, shading their eyes against the light.

"We dig the well deeper," Mrs. Liu said. She had survived three bad harvests and learned that sentiment was a luxury. "The water's still there, below the cracks. We just have to go further down."

Elder Li nodded, slowly. He knew the suggestion was correct and hated that it was necessary.

"And if it's yellow down there too?"

"Then we boil it, strain it, and complain about the taste."

There was a laugh. Thin, tired, but real. The village's survival mechanism: the pragmatic humor of places where bad years are measured in frequency rather than possibility.

I watched them assign tasks. The young men would dig the well deeper by hand, as their fathers had, as people had dug wells since before cultivation was a word. The women would build more filters. The children would help carry water from the stream.

They didn't ask me. They didn't look at me. I was the odd woman who lived at the edge of the forest and took care of someone's boy. My contribution was the water filter and the occasional herb identification. That was the extent of my relevance.

Which was exactly right.

The village needed deeper wells, cleaner water, and the stubborn, collective refusal to lie down because the ground was shaking. They had yellow water, a dry sky, and one another. A god had no place in the discussion.

"Odd," I said to no one. "The weakest survive the longest."

Wei stood next to me. He'd been helping Mrs. Liu stack the filtered water barrels, and he had come back with wet sleeves, worn out from doing the work of three.

"That's not odd," he said. "That's the point."

I turned to him.

"We don't have anything to fall back on," he said. "No power. No shortcuts. So we just keep going. What else are we going to do?"

I didn't answer. There was nothing to add.
