At the creek fork, Wei turned uphill toward the deep forest without telling me. I knew, of course. I heard his steps diverge from the village path, taking the right branch toward the third ridge. Past it grew older trees in ground that still held enough qi to sustain dangerous wildlife. A twelve-year-old should have had an escort before going there. Or a very poor opinion of his chances anywhere else.

I could have stopped him with a word, a glance, or a correction to his route. Such small interventions had become so natural during our mornings together that I scarcely noticed making them. This time, I let him go.

I told myself it was because he needed to learn. That was true. It was also insufficient.

He was gone for most of the day.

The morning dragged, as time does when you wait for something you refuse to admit you're waiting for. I sat by the river, its murmur clearer in the depleted forest's quiet. Every small noise from the path pulled my attention before I returned to the water.

He returned in the late afternoon. I heard him crashing through the undergrowth, then slow to a walk before he came into view. His clothes were torn at the shoulder, a horizontal rip suggesting close proximity to claws. Dried blood marked a shallow scratch on his left cheek. It looked more dramatic than the wound itself.

And in his hand: a tooth.

A spirit beast tooth, almost as long as his hand. It curved like a sickle, yellowed with age and stained at the root where it had sat in a jaw powerful enough to crush stone. It pulsed from ten paces away. Dense, hot qi radiated through its enamel like heat from a coal. A beacon. Every qi-sensitive creature within three li could find it.

He held it up with a grin, as proud as a hunter with his kill. He'd gone into the deep forest alone. He'd come back carrying something he thought made the journey worthwhile.

"Look what I found!"

I looked at the tooth. Then at him. Then at the tooth.

"Put it down."

"What? No, listen. I found a den. Abandoned. The beasts had already fled, but this was left behind. It was wedged in a tree trunk, like it got stuck during a fight. It's still warm. Can you feel it? It's like holding a small fire."

"Put. It. Down."

His grin faltered at my tone. He had grown accustomed to my short commands, usually answering them with another question. This time he studied my face. I had spoken with a flatness even he could distinguish from ordinary irritation.

"Why? This is valuable. Old Chen says spirit beast parts can be sold at the city market for—"

"I am aware of the market value of spirit beast components. Put it down."

He looked from the tooth to me and back. He was weighing my order against what he'd found. The qi in that tooth could be sold for rice, medicine, and a better chance of bringing his father through winter.

He clutched it tighter.

I turned around and walked away.

His footsteps followed, quicker than usual. He carried the tooth against his chest like something he expected to have taken away. He'd chosen to keep it despite what I'd said, and he stayed close, waiting to see whether I would object again.

I should have explained that the tooth was a qi beacon. In a forest emptied by an ascending cultivator, displaced predators would follow such a concentration for li. I should have described what they would find when they reached it in his house: slow, fragile people who had never cultivated.

I said nothing. Explaining would have meant teaching. Teaching meant caring, a door I was still pretending to keep locked. The tooth's warmth reached me through his clothes while I walked. The last time I'd opened that door, a province had burned to make room for my good intentions. I remembered that warmth too.

I know how that sounds. But at the time, it was the only way to keep the distance I thought I needed.

The night was warm. The moon was a crescent. The tooth sat in Wei's house, on the shelf by the window where his mother kept the ceramic jar with the dried flowers and the chipped cup that had belonged to Wei's grandmother.

It pulsed.
