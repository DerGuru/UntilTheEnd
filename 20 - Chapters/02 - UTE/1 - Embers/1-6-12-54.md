Wei sat across the fire from me on the last night. I didn't know it was the last night. That's something you only know afterward, when the shape of events has solidified and the urgency has drained away. You can look back at one evening and say: *that one. That was the last one.* But in this moment, it was just an evening. Fire, silence, and a boy whose face had aged three years since the drought began. His eyes were too clear for someone who hadn't slept properly since the well turned yellow.

The fire was small. Still small; the drought hadn't broken yet, not fully. The wood was still dry, still scarce, rationed with the same careful arithmetic that governed the rice, the water, and the watches of daylight. But the fire was warm and warm was enough.

Wei sat, doing nothing. The frantic, burning exhaustion of crisis had settled into the slow, heavy exhaustion of aftermath. The emergency was over. The bill had come due.

He was quiet. Unusually quiet, even for our fire. Our silences had many textures: the comfortable silence of routine, the charged silence of unasked questions, and the productive silence of two people thinking parallel thoughts without needing to verbalize them. Tonight's silence was different. It had weight. It pointed at something neither of us was saying.

"I prepared the village," Wei said.

"Yes."

"You prepared everything else."

I said nothing. The fire popped. An owl called, the first in many days, returned now that the qi-pressure had eased and the forest was habitable again.

"You'll do something tomorrow. Won't you?"

Tomorrow. He didn't specify what tomorrow contained; the word itself carried enough. The qi-column had dimmed, the pressure withdrawn into that final, dense coil before detonation. Xu Ran was still out there. Close to breakthrough. Still within range of a village that had barely survived his preparation and would not survive what came next.

"Yes," I said.

"Can I help?"

"No."

The word landed between us. He'd learned to receive my refusals. His frustrated protest of the early days had become resigned acceptance, then understanding. The protection in the "no" was as specific and deliberate as the stones I'd placed on the hillside and the trenches I'd dug in the riverbed.

He looked at the fire. His face was orange in the light, shadowed in the hollows, older than it should have been, and exactly as old as it needed to be.

"Okay."

One word. The same word he'd used on the hilltop when I'd told him I was old. He'd thanked me for nothing and almost made me laugh. Almost. The absolute, simple, devastating acceptance of a child who trusted a person he didn't understand. Trust wasn't built on understanding. It was built on mornings, corrections, and the outer robe I'd given him. On the trench I'd dug in the dark with hands that came out the other side too clean to explain.

Okay.

The owl called again. The stars were out, cleaner tonight, the sky less dusty, the atmosphere beginning to recover. Constellations I hadn't seen in a while showed again, old friends rearranged by stellar drift but recognizable.

Wei's breathing slowed. The third-step pattern asserted itself, his body's default, the resting rhythm I'd woven into him through many days of repetition. Inhale. One, two, three. Exhale. One, two, three. His shoulders dropped. His hands uncurled. His head nearly dipped.

"Yun?"

"What."

A pause. Long enough that I thought he'd fallen asleep mid-question.

"Whatever you do tomorrow, come back."

I turned toward him. His eyes were closed. His face was soft with impending sleep, the jaw relaxed, the forehead smooth. A twelve-year-old's face, finally, briefly, looking its age.

I didn't answer. Answering would have required a promise. Promises were a currency I'd avoided because the exchange rate between intention and outcome was unreliable. The default rate was catastrophic.

But I looked at him for a long time.

"Go to sleep," I said.

He was asleep within sixty heartbeats. The deep, immediate slumber of someone who has spent everything and has nothing left to spend.

I sat by the fire. The fire burned. The stars turned.

Tomorrow.

The word sat in my chest like a stone. "Good" had been smooth, warm; "old" had been heavy with accumulated years. This was a sharp one, with edges, and it left a mark when pressed.

Tomorrow I would walk to the place where a young cultivator, brilliant, powerful, and blind, had reached a level that made him dangerous by default. I would do something. I didn't know what yet. The plan had stopped at the trench, the river, and the physics of absorption. Everything beyond that was a space I hadn't filled because filling it required a decision I hadn't made.

How much to reveal. How deep to reach. How far to step out of the margin I'd occupied for a very long time and into a place where the distance no longer protected me.

Tomorrow.

It was the last night I could pretend to be a spectator. The last night the distance was intact. The last night the fire was just a fire, the silence was just silence, and the boy was just a boy. I was just an old woman who lived at the edge of the forest and knew things about herbs.

Tomorrow, I would become something else. Or remember something I'd been. The distinction was less clear than I wanted it to be.

The fire burned low. I fed it one more time. The wood caught. The heat spread. The boy slept.

Tomorrow.
