One morning I sat by the sorting rock while Wei unpacked his basket and realized this had become the shape of my days. The routine had settled so gradually that I had accepted each small change before noticing what they added up to.

Mornings were for herbs. Wei arrived with his basket, his questions, and his stubborn refusal to be intimidated by a corrected mistake. He'd graduated from catastrophic to mediocre, which in the economy of forest pharmacology represented genuine progress. He found the right Mingwort three times out of five now. He could distinguish Silverthorn from False Silverthorn without touch, which saved time and, potentially, fingers. He had stopped bringing Blushing Cap lookalikes entirely, which I chose to interpret as learning rather than coincidence.

Afternoons were for stillness. He sat while I waited nearby. He listened intently enough to distinguish an occasional qi current under the forest's ordinary sounds. Sometimes he felt the pressure shift before a spirit beast moved through the underbrush. This was the awareness the second step required. Cultivation would come later, once he could reliably perceive what he hoped to use.

His father's cough stabilized. The real Mingwort, properly prepared, had reduced the inflammation enough that the bad days were less frequent. The good days were the same. The trajectory had not changed, only the speed at which it traveled.

Evening arrived with the uneven warmth of autumn in a valley. Light clung high in the trees while cold gathered near the ground. Wei had struggled through a difficult session, spending longer than a quarter watch hearing only his heartbeat, his breathing, and the small sounds he made whenever his concentration slipped. Keeping still left him a great deal of himself to listen past.

He'd left frustrated. Quiet. His footsteps on the path back to the village carried the rhythm of disappointment, which sounded like regular footsteps except slower.

I sat at my usual place by the birch, with the sorting rock next to me and the village visible through the trees. Smoke rose from cooking fires. The dog barked as persistently at evening as it had at dawn, for reasons I could seldom identify. I envied its undiminished enthusiasm more than I wanted to admit.

Half a watch passed. Perhaps a full one. Stars appeared in the gaps between clouds, their cold light crossing distances that even I found impressive.

Then footsteps came, lighter than his usual ones. He picked his way over the uneven ground, slow as someone carrying something he couldn't afford to spill.

He emerged from the tree line with a small ceramic bowl, steaming, in both hands.

He set the bowl on the flat part of the rock and held his hands around it until it stood steady. He kept his eyes on the bowl, giving me time to see what he'd brought without asking anything in return.

He turned around and walked back toward the village.

I looked at the bowl.

Rice. Plain. White. It was half of what a growing boy needed, possibly his entire evening share. Steam rose in thin curls that caught the starlight and dissolved into cold air. It smelled of warm grain, the faint sweetness of starch, the cooking water, and the mineral note of the clay itself.

I had not eaten in centuries.

I had simply stopped at some point, as I'd stopped needing sleep or noticing birthdays. When I tried to remember the last meal, my guesses wandered hopelessly. Eating belonged to bodies that needed food to continue. I continued regardless. A phenomenon, I sometimes thought, rather than a person. Phenomena did not eat rice.

The steam rose. The rice cooled.

I stared at the bowl for a long time. Wei had left it within reach, quietly enough that I could have let it cool without answering him. He was poor. He had brought me food anyway. Both facts sat in that small portion, however long I considered it.

A thought crossed my mind that did not belong there.

It arrived cold, analytical, with a distance from what I was seeing that made it feel foreign. As though I had stepped far enough away to see myself beside the rock, reduced to a shape in a scene whose meaning lay elsewhere.

A pattern, repeating. Like a loop closing.

I blinked. The thought was gone before the next curl of steam faded above the bowl. Only its cold interest lingered, as though an ordinary moment could be studied for its structure while everything that made it matter was set aside.

I shook my head. The night was cold. The stars were bright. The rice was cooling.

I did not touch the bowl.

Morning arrived. The fog. The birch. The dog.

Wei came from the village with his basket. He walked to the rock and saw the bowl where he'd left it the night before.

It was empty.

He didn't smile. Smiling would have drawn attention to the bowl and required us to acknowledge what had happened. We had carefully maintained that this was an exchange, that I didn't care, and that a boy bringing rice to a woman like me was an ordinary event in an ordinary forest in an ordinary world.

He picked up the bowl, put it in his basket, and sat down.

"I found something with spotted leaves near the creek. Purple spots. Is that—"

"Poisonous."

"Right."

The morning continued with herbs to sort by the murmuring river. Wei asked the next question as readily as he had the day before. I had eaten rice for the first time in five hundred years because a child had thought of me. He made it as ordinary as the bowl in his basket.

He reminded me of something I wanted to forget. With the bowl empty, the weight of it was back. I had set that weight down so long ago that picking it up should have been impossible.

The bowl was empty. I would deny it if asked.

Nobody asked.
