Mrs. Zhao's rooster ran across the square with its neck stretched out, barely a feather missing after its fall from the roof. Of everything we had to put back where it belonged, it was the hardest part. It dodged. It feinted. It achieved a sustained velocity that contradicted everything I understood about the aerodynamics of overweight poultry.

Wei chased it through the village square, around Elder Li's cracked house, behind the water barrels, and into the gap by Mrs. Liu's house. There, against the grain shed, it turned to face him with its head cocked. Wei was panting. The rooster stood still. If this was a contest, I knew which of them was winning.

"Come here," Wei said.

The rooster did not.

Wei lunged. The rooster launched itself vertically, cleared his outstretched hands by a fingerbreadth, and landed behind him. A remarkable performance for a bird of that shape. It strutted away with its feet lifted high, while Wei was still turning around.

"Use your feet," I said from the wall where I was watching. "Circle left. It will go right."

"How do you know—"

"Roosters always go right."

He circled left. The rooster went right. He was ready. It ran straight into his reach. Wei scooped it up with both hands, pinning its wings against its body. He held it at arm's length while it kicked, screamed, and beat its feathers against his sleeve. Wei grimaced. Apparently he had expected the noise to stop once he'd caught it.

He handed it to Mrs. Zhao, who tucked it under her arm without ceremony and carried it back to the coop. The rooster went silent. Wei watched this with an expression I found considerably more entertaining than the chase.

Wei shook feathers from his sleeve and picked up a hammer instead.

The fence took half a watch. Wei worked with Chun. They didn't talk about the explosion. They talked about the wood: which pieces were salvageable, which needed replacing, and how the joints should be angled to resist the next impact. Practical things. The vocabulary of survival, which has no words for fear because fear doesn't fix fences.

I replaced the roof tiles. Two clay tiles, standard provincial design, fired in the kiln that the village maintained communally and jealously. Finding the replacements, climbing the ladder, seating the tiles in their brackets, and climbing back down took less than half the time Wei spent on the fence. The work required no thought and no qi.

By late afternoon, the village was repaired. The distinction mattered. Restoration implies return; repair implies continuation. The fence was standing but weaker. The roof was complete but the tiles were mismatched. The well was still yellow. The fields were still cracked. The air still tasted like ozone and stress.

But the village was standing. The people were standing. The children had stopped crying. The rooster was in its coop, pecking at the gap under the door.

Near the well, Wei's brother was filling jars with careful hands, rationing each pour, measuring the water against the marks on the clay. The arithmetic of drought. His sister sat on the roof she'd patched in autumn, watching the tree line for nothing in particular.

As we worked, I heard the first version of the story being assembled.

"She was already there," someone said near the well. A woman I didn't recognize by voice alone. "Before the sound finished."

"The herb woman," another voice answered, lower. The words came carefully, with space after them. I waited for the rest. There wasn't any.

Elder Li said nothing. But when I climbed down from his roof, his eyes followed me as they did when he was measuring grain.

Wei found me at dusk.

I was at the barrier wall, the one that had shifted. I'd spent the afternoon resetting the fallen stones, adjusting the angle, and packing clay into the gaps where the shockwave had loosened the foundation. My hands were dirty again. The blisters from last time had barely healed before new ones formed over them, layers of manual labor that my body wasn't accustomed to and my pride wouldn't let me heal.

"You moved faster than me today," he said.

I fitted a stone into the wall, tapping it with my palm until it seated.

"When you stopped me, you were behind me. Then you were in front of me. I didn't see you move."

"You were running," I said.

"I was running and you were not."

His voice had tightened.

"Tell me the trick."

"There is no trick."

"Yun." His voice cracked on my name. He tried again, rougher. "I need to know what I'm learning."

I picked up another stone. The wall was nearly complete. One more row and the reflection angle would be restored. Better, actually, because I'd reinforced the weak points that hadn't survived the first test.

"You're stronger than you look," he said.

I didn't answer.

"You hardly eat. You don't sleep. You don't get tired. And today you—" He stopped, swallowed, and forced the words out without flinching away from them. "Today you were *impossible.*"

Silence held between us.

He waited.

It was a new kind of waiting. He sat down where I had to look past him to reach the pile of stones, refusing to be moved.

"It matters," he said finally, quiet. "To me."

I took the stone he handed me. The right stone: dense granite, the correct weight for the position in the wall. He'd been watching me work. He'd been learning the pattern.

We rebuilt the wall in silence. Stone by stone. Hand to hand. The sun went down.

The wall held.

The stars came out. From the east, Xu Ran's signature pulsed, rhythmic, steady, and tightening.

Soon.
