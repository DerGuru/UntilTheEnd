The sky glowed above the forest, too bright for sunset, too high for the lanterns of any distant city. This was qi, raw, dense, and compressed, rising like a column of invisible fire. The air above the treeline shimmered with pressure rather than heat. The distinction mattered to no one in the village except me, yet I was not the one vomiting.

Three children retched. Within a few hundred heartbeats. The qi-pressure hit the unprotected human body like a deep note hitting a wineglass: finding the resonant frequency, amplifying it, and shaking loose whatever wasn't firmly attached. Nausea was first. Then the headache, a band of pressure across the forehead that tightened as the column intensified.

The animals that were left screamed: Mrs. Zhao's rooster, two goats, and a cat that had survived the qi-mutated phase by being the most cautious mammal in the province. Each in its own register, each with its own particular sound of terror. The rooster's contribution was the loudest. Here, finally, was a situation that warranted the volume it had maintained its entire life.

The ground shook.

The modest tremors that had continued for so many days had deepened into something structural, originating from a point northeast of the village where the qi-compression had reached a density that the bedrock could no longer absorb passively. The vibration traveled through the earth at a frequency that humans felt in their teeth, their joints, and the soles of their feet.

A wall fell. Elder Li's house, the one I'd already repaired the roof tiles on, lost its eastern wall. The mud brick simply separated from the corner posts and toppled outward. The sheet hit the ground and broke into a cloud of red-brown dust. The dust hung in the air. Everything was dust now; the drought had turned the valley into a substrate that converted every impact into a cloud.

Mrs. Zhao was thrown to the ground by the old beam in her doorway, the one that had been cracking for years and finally broke. It caught her shoulder. She cried out, a short, sharp sound, more surprise than pain. Wei was there in heartbeats. He lifted the beam. He couldn't lift the beam; he was twelve, it was oak, and it weighed more than he did. But he lifted it anyway, because he'd given his body an instruction and his body followed instructions. Chun pulled her free.

Her arm hung wrong. Dislocated. The joint popped and swollen, the shoulder at an angle the tendons couldn't sustain. She was conscious. Coherent. Angry, because she'd survived four decades in this village without being felled by her own architecture and she resented the irony.

Wei turned to me. His face was asking a question his voice hadn't formed yet, the question I'd been waiting for since the first tremor. It had been building behind every day of water runs, rationing, fence repair, and earthquake cleanup.

*What do we do now?*

"Move them," I said. "South. Now."

He didn't hesitate. He'd prepared for this: the evacuation plan he'd built, the bundles at every door, and the route he'd chosen. The southern path I'd cleared.

He went. Running. His voice cracked through the dust, too high, too fast, the words tumbling out as they did when he was scared and pretending not to be:

"Get the bundles. The bundles by the doors. We're going south, the slope, come *on*—"

The village moved. Awkwardly; smoothly was a fantasy that belonged to a different world. But it moved. People grabbed their packs. They lifted children and supported each other. Mrs. Liu had Elder Li by the arm, half-dragging him because his leg had cramped from the tremor. His dignity wouldn't let him lean and her patience wouldn't let him fall. Gao carried Mrs. Zhao, arm hanging, shoulder swelling, her face set in an expression of concentrated displeasure that worked better than any pain draft.

Wei led from the front, south, finding the path I'd marked, the route I'd cleared, and the passage through the boulder field that I'd made safe with three days of manual labor. Split hands. No qi.

They moved. South. Away from the forest. Away from the column of pressure that was rising like a prayer no one had asked for and no one wanted answered.

But I didn't go south.

I went east.
