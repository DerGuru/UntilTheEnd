Wei struck the river with his open palm, hard enough to soak his trousers to the knee. The flat slap carried across the bank. Ripples spread from his hand, breaking against the stones before the current smoothed them away.

He hit it again.

He hit harder this time. His reflection scattered under the splash, then reassembled as the water settled. It still showed a boy standing in the same place, with wet trousers and nothing else to show for the effort.

The breathing exercise he had practiced so persistently had produced nothing today, after failing him yesterday too. On good days he could sense the qi beneath the topsoil. Now it was so thin that even experienced cultivators would struggle to distinguish it. Wei had spent the morning sitting on his rock, using everything I'd taught him to search for something that grew fainter each day.

"What's the *point*?" He stood ankle-deep in the river with water dripping from his hand. His voice cracked as it rose. "Sensing qi. Great. Super useful. I can sense that the forest is dying. Amazing. Really helping. When the thing that's *killing* it gets here, what do I do? Just... breathe at it?"

He kicked a stone. It skipped twice across the surface and sank.

"Sit still. Listen. Feel the qi. That's it? That's what you've been teaching me? There's a *thing* out there. I can feel it, you know. It presses on the air. And it's getting closer and everyone in the village is scared and Chun can barely walk and my father is worse again and all I can do is SIT ON A ROCK AND BREATHE."

The river murmured under the trees. A fish darted out from a submerged root, leaving a small wake as it moved upstream. It settled past the circles spreading from the stone he'd kicked.

I knew he was right.

The training was necessary, a foundation for everything that would come after. He was also right about how little it gave him against the threat six li away. Each day brought that danger closer while he made his small, slow advances. He was learning to hear. He needed to be able to fight. Between those two abilities lay more work than determination could accomplish in the time he had.

His ceiling was low. That was the kind thing to call it. His meridians were narrow, his capacity small, and the best he could achieve after years of perfect training might be the lowest stage of Qi Gathering. Against someone on the brink of Core Formation, that was a candle held against a furnace.

But candles were what people used. I had learned this. Slowly, reluctantly, over more lifetimes than the metaphor deserved.

"You're not ready," I said.

"When will I be ready?"

The honest answer was a cruelty I almost committed.

"Maybe never," I said.

Wei stood in the water, hands lowered and dripping. He faced me. His mouth opened, then closed without another protest. He had given the work everything he had. Each mistake had left him something to try: a different footing, another breath, or a better grip on the next stone. He had treated effort as a route he could keep walking until he arrived. I had just told him that years of such work might leave the distance just as wide.

The current pushed around his ankles. The sun behind him made his outline sharp, dark, and small.

Then his jaw set. I had seen that small movement before: his chin lifting, his shoulders squaring, and his gaze leaving me long enough for him to decide what to do next. He stepped onto the bank, sat down, and began wringing water from his trousers.

"Then show me how to not die anyway."

He spoke quietly, eyes on the wet fabric in his hands. Water dripped onto the bank. Looking at me took more than asking had.

The river moved. The fish returned to its root.

"That's what I'm doing," I said.

He looked up and studied my face, wary after so many answers that had turned out to be evasions. He had heard adults make a dismissal sound kind. I let him take his time with this one. The words were true. I'd spent each morning helping him survive, however poorly I admitted it. Neither of us understood yet how much more those words would eventually mean.

He nodded.

"Okay." He stood, brushed his knees, and walked back to his stone. He sat cross-legged, closed his eyes, and let his breathing slow.

He listened.
