Mrs. Zhao's rooster was pressed into the corner of its coop, silent. That was the first thing. The absence of that stubborn, chronically inaccurate timekeeper's call. Its dawn performance had been the village's most reliable, if least appreciated, alarm for as long as anyone could remember. Its head was tucked under its wing, its body motionless. Its usual combative posture had shrunk to something small and tight.

The birds were gone. The trees were empty. The bushes were empty. The eaves, the rooftops, and the fence posts where sparrows gathered every morning to compete for territory were bare. The sky was bare, a blue so absolute and uninterrupted that it looked manufactured, like a ceiling painted by someone who'd never seen weather.

The wind had stopped.

The leaves on the few remaining trees hung motionless, each one suspended at the exact angle where the last gust had left it. The flags on Elder Li's house, two faded cloth strips that usually showed the wind direction to anyone who cared, drooped. The dust, which had been the valley's constant companion for so many days, settled and lay still. The air was heavy. Still warm but thick now. It sat in the lungs like something solid, requiring effort to breathe, as though the atmosphere itself had gained weight overnight.

The silence was the wrong kind.

Quiet is the absence of loud sounds. This was the absence of all sound, including the ones you don't notice until they're gone: the wind in the grass, the creak of warm wood, and the constant, subliminal hum of insects that forms the background on which every other sound is painted.

That background was gone. The world was a room with the door closed and the air stopped. Every surface padded, every creature holding its breath. Something, somewhere, was about to happen. Even my own breathing sounded intrusive.

I stood in the clearing. The qi-pressure was absolute.

The pressure grinding through the valley for so many days had been incremental, except for yesterday's short relief. This was final. Complete. The qi-density in the air had reached a level where sound waves couldn't propagate normally, where the medium itself was too saturated to carry vibration. The silence was physical. The qi suppressed sound as water suppresses fire, filling the space it needed to exist in.

Xu Ran was ready.

He was five hundred paces northeast, a single point of impossible density, compressed past the limits that physics had set and cultivation had overridden. He held himself at the threshold, the final breath before the final step, the last moment of being what he was before he became what he would be.

Wei was awake. He stood in the doorway of the hut, barefoot, rigid, his shoulders tight under the pressure. His qi-sensitivity, second-step, refined through months of training, was registering a sensation he had no framework for: weight without weight, loudness without sound, and threat without source.

He looked at me.

I looked at him.

"Today?" His voice was thin in the pressurized air. The word barely carried.

"Yes."

"How long?"

"The village needs to go now."

He swallowed. His hands balled at his sides, a boy holding himself steady when the air pressed down.

"The bundles," he said. Not a question.

"The bundles."

He turned and walked into the village. Within a hundred and twenty heartbeats, his voice cut through the space between the houses, thin, pitched too high, breaking through the silence like a stick breaking through ice.

"Bundles. South slope. Now."

No arguments. No discussions. The village had been living in the shadow of this moment for many days. The people had learned something that arguments couldn't teach them: preparation is the dividend that fear pays when it's invested correctly.

They moved. Practiced, smooth, the choreography of a community that had rehearsed its own salvation. The bundles were at the doors. The children were gathered.

"Mei, hold your brother's hand. Don't let go." Mrs. Liu's voice was steady. The kind of steady that costs something.

"The chickens—" someone started.

"Leave them," Elder Li said. He was already moving, Mrs. Liu at his arm. His stubbornness and her patience, locked in a partnership neither acknowledged but both relied on.

Six hundred heartbeats. The village was empty.

They went south, down the path Wei had chosen, along the route I'd cleared, and over the terrain I'd secured. Fifty-four people moving away again from a danger they couldn't see or fight. They understood it only as weight, silence, and the wrongness of a sky without birds.

Wei was at the front. Leading.

"Stay together," he called back. "Watch your step past the rocks."

"We know the rocks, boy," Gao muttered. But he watched his step.

Wei's back was straight and his hands were steady. When he turned once to make sure everyone was following, his face was the one I'd seen in firelight, moonlight, and the dawn of a day when the well went dry. Older than twelve, younger than any age that would justify what he was doing, and exactly as old as he needed to be.

He turned again. He looked for me.

I wasn't at the gathering point anymore. I was at the tree line, east, moving toward the forest, toward the pressure, and toward the point where the silence was thickest. There the air was heaviest and the force of what was about to happen was concentrated into a space the size of a clearing.

"YUN!" His voice. Distant. Thin in the suppressed air. "Yun, where are you going?"

I vanished into the woods. The answer would have been too long, the truth too heavy, and the air too thick for either.

"YUN!"

I kept walking. Among the trees. Into the pressure. Toward the thing that was about to happen, because someone had to be there when it did.
