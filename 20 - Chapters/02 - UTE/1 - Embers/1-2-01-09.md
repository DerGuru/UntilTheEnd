Birds called before daylight showed between the trees. The air shifted as dawn approached, then gray began to replace the dark. I sat against the birch as each sound sharpened.

He woke and sat up with leaves in his hair, moss on his cheek. He blinked at the trees, still trying to place where he'd slept. He looked around until he found me ten paces away, against the birch, in the same position. He studied me without speaking. I couldn't tell whether he understood I'd been there all night or assumed I had just arrived.

He stood, stretched, and winced when he put weight on the ankle he'd been able to forget while sleeping.

"Morning," he said.

I said nothing.

The houses were hidden, but thin gray smoke from breakfast fires rose through the canopy gap to the south. He eyed it, then turned back to me.

He didn't leave.

He sat back down, pulled his basket toward him, and began sorting yesterday's herbs as if it had been decided, without discussion, that this was where sorting happened now.

The morning passed. Then the afternoon. He talked about the herbs, the village, and the dog that had eaten his father's shoe. I said nothing. He didn't mind. At some point he took cloth-wrapped bread from his basket, stale enough that each determined bite carried over the birdsong.

The sun moved through the canopy in its reliable arc, and the light went from gray to gold to the long amber of late afternoon.

He stood up, brushed off his pants, and checked the smoke again. It was thinner now, with the evening fires still unlit.

"I should go home," he said. His father would be waiting for the medicine. He spoke reluctantly, as if returning home were an interruption to what he had chosen to do with his day.

He left through the underbrush toward the village, his footsteps doing their usual damage to the quiet. I listened until the crunching faded, then longer, until ordinary forest sounds filled the space where his voice had been.

The birch was against my back. The moss still held the impression of his feet between the roots. It would fill back in by morning. Moss always did.

He came back the next morning.

He came to the same spot: the birch, the roots, and the gap in the canopy that warmed the moss by mid-morning. He approached it like a room he'd rented, sat down without asking, and unpacked his basket.

I was already there, against the birch, where I had been and where I continued to be.

"I brought these." He swung the basket off his back and knelt in front of me, presenting its contents like an offering. Herbs. Roots. Mushrooms. All of them wrong.

I studied the collection, then him, then the collection again.

"What?" he said.

I said nothing. He shrugged, sat down three paces away, and began sorting his findings into piles. Their logic escaped me. I had no great desire to investigate it.

He told me about wolf tracks he'd found by the creek.

"Badger," I said.

He continued describing how large the wolves must have been.

That was day two.

On day three he came later, after the sun had cleared the fog and insects hummed above the moss. He had questions. Many questions. He wanted to know where spirit beasts lived, what they ate, and whether they slept. Whether they could be tamed. Whether the one I'd killed had a family.

"Do spirit beasts have families?"

I did not answer.

"Because if they do, there might be a baby boar out there. Right now. Looking for its mother. Which is dust." He paused. "You dusted its mother."

I stared past him at the damp bark. I could have been sitting against it three hundred years earlier. That was a comfortable distance from the conversation.

"I think they do have families," he continued, answering his own question with the confidence of a boy for whom silence had never been an obstacle. "Old Chen says they're animals with qi and animals have families. So there might be a Mrs. Boar. And little boars."

He was quiet for three heartbeats.

"Do you think the little boars will come looking for revenge?"

"Those were badger tracks," I said.

He blinked, connecting it with yesterday's conversation. He had talked about so many things since then that I could forgive him losing track of one. Then his face lit up.

"Ha! I *knew* you were listening yesterday! I said they were wolf tracks and you—" He pointed at me with a reed he'd pulled from the ground. "You said *badger*. Under your breath. Like you didn't want me to hear."

I had not said it under my breath. I had said it at normal volume. He had simply been talking too loudly to notice.

On day four, the herbs improved. He'd watched what I stepped around, what I glanced at, and what I left untouched. He was observant with the desperate focus of a child whose survival depended on reading the world correctly. His new collection was still mostly wrong, but less catastrophically so. He'd stopped picking Blushing Cap lookalikes. He'd started picking Silverleaf, which was useless but harmless. An improvement over poisonous.

He laid them out on a flat rock near where I sat. I did not look at them. He watched me not look at them, leaving each specimen in easy view in case I changed my mind. We had begun a small game. Neither of us had said what the rules were.

Day five.

He sat down two paces away instead of three, basket on the ground, legs folded under him. He rested his hands on his knees and stared at the same trees I was.

He was quiet for a hundred unhurried breaths. Wind moved through the canopy. A bird called repeatedly without an answer. Branches creaked as the morning warmed them. You'd think a silence with a child would be unremarkable. You haven't met this child.

"Are you always like this?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Good. Then I know what to expect."

He picked up a pinecone, turned it over in his hands, and set it down.

"My father's worse today. The cough. It's deeper."

I said nothing. The morning light fell through the canopy in sheets, lighting small golden motes of dust. Each was visible between us only until it reached the shade.

"The herbs I'm bringing. They're not working, are they?"

He had picked up the pinecone again and turned it over and over, pressing his thumb against the edges of its scales.

"No," I said.

He nodded, barely. He had known the cough was worsening longer than he'd known me. He had collected herbs anyway, because it gave him something to do for his father besides listen to it.

He put the pinecone down, stood up, and brushed off his pants.

"Same time tomorrow?"

He left before I could answer. Which was convenient, because I didn't have one.
