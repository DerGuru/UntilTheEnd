The days acquired a rhythm and I let them. Each morning found me against the birch, where smoke from the village rose through the trees. I had begun organizing my mornings around his visits. It happened without any grand decision about being presentable before the boy arrived. Mostly, I didn't wake at all. Sleep was a habit I maintained out of nostalgia, like a chair kept long after the person who made it worth sitting in had gone.

I sat at that same birch, at the same point in the morning, facing the village. First came the smoke from the cooking fires. Eventually, the sound of his footsteps followed it through the trees.

I told myself it was the tree. It was a good tree. Well-positioned. Comfortable bark.

The village hung at the edge of my attention, like a picture in a room I crossed every day. Nothing in it asked to be studied: smoke, movement, and the muted sounds of a community too small to thrive easily. The dog barked at the same point each morning, needing no more encouragement than daylight. Children chased each other near the well, their sharp, bright laughter carrying over the birdsong. A woman carried water. A man sharpened a tool he'd sharpened yesterday. The rhythms of survival repeated themselves with the dull reliability of a heartbeat.

Villages like this had passed before me for longer than any of them could comprehend. They were stubbornly identical in their essentials: the same needs, the same hierarchies, and the same quiet conflicts over resources that were never quite enough. Yet their inhabitants met each difficulty with the urgency of people discovering it for the first time: the first to struggle, the first to pray, and the first to learn how little a harvest responded to either.

He arrived each morning, as predictable as the sunrise and approximately as avoidable. His basket contained an evolving collection from the forest, each day less lethal, each day organized with more intention. He was learning through proximity, gathering information from what I did even when I hadn't technically told him anything. Refusing to give instruction was proving inadequate.

"Old Lan's goat got loose again," he said on the seventh morning, sorting roots next to me, comfortable in the decision that this arrangement was permanent. "Third time this month. She wanders into the Bai family's garden and eats the cabbages. Mrs. Bai says she'll cook the goat next time. Old Lan says Mrs. Bai couldn't cook water."

I picked a beetle off my sleeve and set it on the ground. It walked away as unhurriedly as it had crossed my arm, straight toward a fallen leaf.

"But his granddaughter follows the goat every time. She's eight. She goes into the forest, past the safe boundary. Nobody notices because everyone's busy fighting about the cabbages."

Something in his tone shifted. Under the argument about cabbages ran an eight-year-old following an animal past the tree line, out of sight of people busy keeping their households fed. Spirit beasts roamed there. For a child that young, getting home could depend on which trail the goat chose.

"I brought her back yesterday," he said, as casually as if he'd returned a borrowed cup. "She was sitting by the creek. Playing with rocks. There were tracks nearby. Not badger."

He glanced at me. Quick. Testing.

"That's a weed," I said, of the root in his hand.

He eyed the root, then me, and threw the weed over his shoulder. "So the tracks—"

"Deer."

"Deer," he repeated flatly. "Not wolf."

"Deer."

"Are you sure? Because they were big and—"

"Deer do not typically attack eight-year-olds."

"But spirit deer might, right? If they're qi-enhanced—"

"That was a normal deer."

He considered this, nodded once, and reached for another root. I hoped the distinction would be clearer to him than most of the plants in his basket.

"Also, Old Chen's wife asked about you. She wanted to know if you're the herb woman from the east that the traders mentioned. I said you were my grandmother."

I turned my head toward him, slowly, with a slowness no words could improve upon.

He grinned. "What? You look like you could be somebody's grandmother. A very... well-preserved grandmother."

I returned my gaze to the forest. The beetle I'd set down had reached the edge of a leaf. It paused there, antennae moving above empty air. I sympathized with the delay.

"What should I tell them?" His voice was quieter now. "The village. When they ask. Because they ask. People in small villages ask about everything, especially about things that aren't their business."

There were several dozen answers to this question, most of them variations on nothing. He could say I was passing through. He could deny that I existed, although the woman who'd asked had already seen me.

"Tell them nothing," I said.

"That'll make them more curious."

He was right. It would. In a village this small, curiosity traveled from house to house as reliably as water ran downhill. Refusing an explanation would give them one more thing to discuss while they invented their own.

"Tell them what you want," I said.

He was quiet, then pulled another root from his basket and held it up.

"This one?"

"Wrong."

He threw it over his shoulder and reached for the next.

The morning went on. Smoke rose behind us, the village voices faint under the trees. The forest stretched ahead. Between the life he came from and the silence I carried, we sat on opposite sides of a boundary neither of us had named.

Not yet.
