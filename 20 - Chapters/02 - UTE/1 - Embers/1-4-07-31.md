The crickets started up one at a time while Wei built the fire. There were still only a few, scattered across the clearing, their calls separated by long stretches of quiet. I placed each by its sound: one near the woodpile, another beyond the stones, and a third somewhere downhill. By the time the fire was burning steadily, five or six were calling. The gaps between them shortened until I could listen without counting. Enough sound to sit with.

Wei sat by the fire. Cross-legged. Hands on his knees. Eyes closed.

His breathing was steady, the third-step rhythm, the count I'd drilled into him until the pattern became habit. He inhaled. One, two, three. He exhaled. One, two, three. The intervals were even, the breathing of a boy who had learned to measure himself against a standard he couldn't see and trusted that the standard was real.

I sat beside him. The distinction had crept up on me, as most things about this arrangement had: each adjustment too small to notice and too significant, taken together, to ignore. A month ago, I'd sat across the fire from him. Fourteen days ago, a quarter of the way around it. Tonight: beside. Less than a pace of space between us, occupied by warmth, firelight, and the quiet between two people who have stopped needing to fill it.

The fire was good. He'd built it well, small and efficient, with the ring of stones tight enough to contain the heat. He had stopped building large fires after I'd remarked, once, that large fires attracted attention and small fires attracted warmth. He hadn't asked which kind of attention. He was learning not to ask things he already knew the answers to.

The valley lay below us. From this rise, the same one where I'd first watched Xu Ran's vortex, the village was a collection of warm points in the dark. Each one a window, a doorway, or a cooking fire, a small human light in all that darkness. From here came the murmur of final conversations, the splash of washing, and a mother singing badly, beautifully, to a child who wasn't tired.

Somewhere below, a dog barked once. Then it stopped, as if it had heard something the rest of the village couldn't.

The lights on the horizon were absent tonight. Xu Ran was cycling inward, consolidating, and preparing for the next expansion. Even from this distance, the dense knot of qi where he worked pressed against me, coiled tight, with the weight of snow banked on a mountainside.

Twenty-one days.

Wei opened his eyes a slit, just enough to let the firelight in.

"The pressure's worse tonight," he said. Quietly. To the fire, or to the valley, or to no one.

"Yes."

He closed his eyes again. The word dissolved into the cricket-song.

Wei breathed. In. Out. The rhythm was hypnotic, a pulse independent of the crickets, independent of the fire. Independent of everything except the body that produced it and the discipline that shaped it.

I listened.

That was all. I listened to the boy breathe, to the wood crackling in the fire, to the crickets calling across the clearing. I listened to the murmur of the village, the hum of the valley, and the distant, compressed heartbeat of a threat I could end with a thought. I chose to listen.

I just was. Here. On this rise, by this fire, beside this boy.

It was enough.

That was the last quiet evening.

I didn't know that then.

Wei's breathing slowed. The intervals stretched. His head dipped. He caught himself, then his head dipped again: the losing battle of a boy who had been running all day and couldn't outpace his own exhaustion. His shoulders relaxed. His hands slid from his knees to the ground beside him. His chin touched his chest.

He was asleep. Upright, unfinished, and mid-breath.

The fire was burning low. The coals glowed orange in the dark, warm but diminishing. In half a watch, they would be gray. In a full watch, the cold that already reached my back would settle around us.

I reached over.

I took a piece of wood from the pile he'd gathered. A thick, dry piece that would burn slowly and evenly for a watch or more. I set it on the coals. The fire caught it immediately, the orange brightening to yellow, the heat expanding outward in a wave that reached Wei and settled over him like a hand.

The fire would burn until morning now. He would be warm.

And I?

The crickets sang. The fire burned. The boy breathed.

I stayed.
