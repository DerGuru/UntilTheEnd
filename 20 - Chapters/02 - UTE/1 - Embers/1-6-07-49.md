The ground hummed under my feet, the air tasted like metal, and the bones in my body sang. These old, impossible bones.

I knew what was coming. I remembered.

The humming was the same. The vibration in the marrow, the frequency that traveled through structure rather than air. Through the mineral density of my bones, through a skeleton forged in the first era of cultivation that had never, in thousands of years, lost the resonance it had acquired in the violence of its making.

I knew this feeling in my bones, like a soldier hearing drums. My shoulders squared with each pulse. The vibration brought one word: *fight.*

I kept walking. East. Toward the column. Toward the pulse. Toward the river past it: the channel I'd dug, the trench I'd cut, and the place where water could do what I wouldn't. Away from the village, the boy, and the people moving south on a path I'd built with bleeding hands. Human labor, because the alternative was the thing I'd buried. The easy alternative, the correct one, the one every fiber of my being was shouting at me to take.

The river smell reached me in faint, mineral gusts. Wet stone. Silt. Water I could put to use without a prayer.

But the burying was coming undone.

The trigger was the noise, the qi-column's harmonic, low, vast, and somehow personal, as though the frequency had been tuned specifically to the instrument of me. It reached into the sediment of memory. What surfaced was old, heavy, and hot.

I was a fighter.

At the beginning, what was I? Curious. Observant. Content to exist in a large, interesting world populated by creatures that lived and died in patterns I found fascinating. The observation was enough. For a long time, it was enough.

Then it wasn't.

I didn't recall the exact moment. What mattered was the accumulation, the slow buildup of evidence that observation without action was participation. Watching a village burn and doing nothing was a choice with consequences. Cowardice was less expensive than courage and left no fingerprints.

So I fought.

One memory surfaced cleanly, as if the column had hooked it by the spine and yanked.

A stone room. A meditation array carved into the floor. Children strapped to it, their screaming spent, their developing meridians forced open because adults were impatient. A sect elder standing over them with a calm expression, explaining the necessity of it as you explain weather.

I remember my hand.

The technique escapes me. So does the qi. What remains is the motion: reaching out and ending him in the time it took dust to settle.

I was good at it. You know what that means, coming from me.

More than good. I was absolute. The gap between me and the things I fought was an absence where a contest should have been. The word "fight" was a courtesy I rarely meant.

And it repeated. Different banners, identical appetites. Every vacuum I created filled before the dust settled.

A thousand years. Nothing changed.

You can fight a wave. You cannot fight an ocean.

The ground jolted under my feet. A tremor, sharper than the last. Stones clicked against each other somewhere upslope. I steadied myself without breaking stride.

Eventually, I stopped fighting. The stopping was undramatic, too gradual to provide a final battle or a moment of revelation where the futility crystallized into a decision. I simply ran out of the thing that had made me start. The anger. The conviction that fighting mattered. The belief that ending one cultivator, or ten, or a thousand, constituted progress rather than maintenance.

I went back to what I'd been before: an observer. A presence on hilltops, watching. Tired. Older than the anger, smaller than the problem, and aware, with a clarity that felt like punishment, that the only thing my thousand years of fighting had produced was a thousand years of evidence that fighting didn't work.

The wind was warm. The ground hummed. The bones sang.

I walked east. Toward the tribulation. Toward the spot where the qi-column met the river, where water could absorb, redirect, and neutralize its pressure.

My fists were clenched. I didn't remember clenching them. My tendons had tightened and my knuckles had gone white. A fighter's stance, a fighter's readiness, and the ancient, useless, intoxicating certainty that if something needed to be ended, I could end it.

I unclenched them. Deliberately. Finger by finger.

I was not going east to fight.

I was going east to move stones, dig trenches, and redirect water. Physical work. Manual labor. The intervention of a woman with blistered hands and old bones. A commitment to keep a village alive without becoming the thing she used to be. I told myself it was no promise, only a direction I chose again with every step.

The column pulsed. The sky glowed. The bones sang.

I didn't listen.
