I was between the boar and the boy before either of them understood what had happened. I had crossed without disturbing the air, leaving them only the change in my position to notice. A moment earlier I had stood by the tree line. Now I was close enough to see the individual bristles on the boar's snout, each one thick as a needle, each vibrating with the qi that kept this animal alive.

I raised my hand toward the charging beast and flicked my index finger between its eyes.

It came apart.

Bristles, bone, muscle, and qi unraveled at once, the solid shape losing its cohesion as thoroughly as sand under the incoming tide. The beast's eyes were the last to go. They held their shape for half a heartbeat longer than the rest, still locked on me above the tusks. Then they were dust too.

The dust hung in the air, fine particles catching the morning light, turning it amber. I had seen temple interiors lit like that; this light fell on a clearing where something had just died. It settled slowly on the leaves, the moss, and the boy's hair, carrying the metallic scent of dispersed qi. Ozone, with something earthier underneath. The smell of a living thing that had stopped being a living thing so quickly it hadn't had time to rot.

No birds sang.

No insects hummed.

The small sounds of the forest had stopped with the beast. In the sudden silence, pressure built in my ears as if I stood underwater.

I counted the heartbeats. Ten. Twelve. On the fourteenth, a finch started up again from somewhere to the east. One short call, a pause, then another. By the third, the interval had shortened to something like normal.

Behind me, the boy breathed.

I turned around.

He was still on the ground, his back against the boulder, legs splayed, one ankle purple and swollen. His mouth was open. His hands were at his sides, palms flat against the dirt, fingers curled into the earth for purchase. The second rock, the one he'd picked up to throw, had fallen from his fingers. It sat in his lap, gray and ordinary.

He stared at me.

I would have expected fear. Gratitude might come later, if it came at all. For now, he was confused. He looked from my hand to the place where the animal had been, trying to reconcile what he'd just seen with everything he knew about how the world worked.

His nose was still bleeding. The thin red line had reached his chin.

We stared at each other. The dust settled between us, slow as snow.

I thought about saying something, then discarded the thought. "You're welcome" was too generous. "Be more careful" was too parental. "Those mushrooms would have killed whoever you brought them to faster than the boar would have killed you" was accurate but unhelpful. There was a fourth option: standing there without speaking, as I'd been doing for centuries. That one came easiest. It always had.

Instead, I studied the space where the beast had been. A patch of bare earth, still warm. In a watch or two, grass would begin growing back. In a few days, the warmth would fade. After twenty days or so, this spot would look much like the rest of the forest floor.

I had seen that happen so many times that it should have stopped meaning anything. It had, mostly. But a small, quiet part of me still remembered gardens. That part noticed when warmth faded from a place.

The boy moved slowly, reaching down to free his swollen ankle from where it was wedged against a root. He winced, sucking air through his teeth as he got his foot loose. Then he tried to stand. His ankle buckled and he caught himself against the boulder. He tried again. This time he held, weight on his good foot, one hand braced against the stone.

He brushed dirt off his pants. It was a small, absurd gesture, straightening his clothes after watching a spirit beast turn to powder two paces from his face. Apparently tidiness still mattered. He attended to the other knee.

He looked at me again.

I looked back.

He opened his mouth, closed it, and opened it again.

I turned around and walked away.

The last thing I killed had been larger. And more pointless.
