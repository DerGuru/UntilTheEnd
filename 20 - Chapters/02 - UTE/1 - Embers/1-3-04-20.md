The screaming started a watch and a half into the night. I had been standing at the tree line since evening, watching for a threat I'd declined to prevent. I had no investment in this village. That was difficult to reconcile with my position here, but I had other explanations available. The air was pleasant. The stars were acceptable.

Wood crashed first, splintering at a fence or coop door. Then the chickens began, their shrieks raw enough to carry between every house. Something was wrong. They made that much clear with considerably more volume than information.

Then the human screaming started.

The creature was a forest predator, scarcely developed enough to count as a spirit beast. It was something between a large cat and a dog, built for speed over long grass, with claws that could open bark. Normally rabbits and the occasional slow deer would have kept it deep in the forest. The depletion had emptied its territory. Qi from the tooth on Wei's shelf reached far enough to draw it toward the village.

It crossed the boundary at a run, cleared the first fence without breaking stride, and went straight for the chickens. Two died in less than a heartbeat. The blood was black in the moonlight.

Then it found Neighbor Chun.

Chun came out with a torch, because torches were what people used where cultivation was a rumor and practical solutions involved fire. His shoulders could fill a doorway. He had large hands used to moving heavy things and a matching confidence that he could hold his ground. He planted himself in the gap in the chicken pen's fence.

The creature ran straight past him.

Its claw caught his leg in passing, with scarcely a change in its stride. The torch went one way. Chun went another. His startled cry carried across the yard as his injured leg folded under him.

Blood spread over the dirt from a long wound above his shin. The artery was intact, but torn muscle would heal slowly. He would carry a limp into old age. He crawled quickly for a man his size, heading toward the house where somebody was already shouting his name.

Not my problem. That phrase had kept me upright for longer than this village had existed, longer than the last three empires I'd watched rise and fall. I could not bring myself to care. I had known this would happen from the moment Wei held up the tooth.

The creature bolted with its prey. It vanished into the dark in three bounds. The tooth's qi still radiated from Wei's shelf, giving it the same path to follow back whenever it grew hungry again.

The village woke. Torches multiplied while people talked over one another, calling for help or asking questions nobody could answer yet. Harvest, weather, and neighborly disputes had given them little practice with this. Someone bandaged Chun's leg. Someone else counted the chickens and announced the loss with the flatness of a person who counted survival in eggs.

Morning fog rose over the village as regularly as it had the day before. The dog began barking at dawn. Blood had dried on the dirt into a stain that would take two rains to erase.

I heard the conversation before I saw it, voices carrying sharply between the houses. Near the well, some twenty people had formed a loose semicircle, all facing the same person. From the tree line, I knew whom they had chosen before I saw his face.

Wei stood in the center.

People had positioned themselves around him until their attention left him little room to move. He stood with his arms at his sides, chin level, watching whoever spoke. He was deciding whether to argue, though giving in had never come easily to him.

"The boy brings the forest into the village," Gao said, pitching his voice to reach the people at the back. "Every day he goes deeper. Every day he comes back with something. And now—" He gestured at Chun, who sat gray-faced on a stool by the well, his bandaged leg extended. "This."

Wei's older brother stood behind him, tall enough to see over the gathering. He rested one hand lightly on Wei's shoulder, leaving the boy free to move under it. His sister waited at the edge of the crowd, her gaze returning to the gaps between people.

"I didn't—" Wei started.

"The tooth." Gao again. "You brought a spirit beast tooth into the village. Chun saw it yesterday, on your shelf. Glowing. Like a little lantern. Then, what a coincidence, something comes in the night and tears up his leg."

He was precisely right: the tooth's qi had drawn the predator. Cause, effect, a straight line. Gao had reached the conclusion without a theory to explain it. Village logic could be efficient that way, attending to what happened rather than what anybody called it.

Wei said nothing. His jaw clenched, released, and clenched again as he looked from Chun's leg to the tooth. His training had given him enough to follow the connection himself. The tooth. The qi. The signal. The animal.

His chin lowered. He stared at the bandage now, the tooth still in his hand. He had brought it home to buy medicine. Somebody else would walk badly for the rest of his life because it had spent a night on that shelf.

His mother pushed through the crowd.

She was small and thin, her hair pulled back with a strip of cloth that had once been white. She came straight through the villagers, passing people who were still speaking. Her movements had a weary economy, as if this were one more crisis in a day too full of them. Her eyes never left her son.

She passed Gao without answering him. Whatever the others had left to say would have to wait. She reached Wei, looked him in the eye, and slapped the tooth from his hand.

It tumbled from his grip and landed in the mud with a dull thuk. It lay yellow against the brown earth, still pulsing faintly with warmth.

Wei's chin trembled, so faintly that I doubt most of them saw it. He did not cry. He stood among twenty accusers with his mother silent before him, the tooth in the mud beside his foot. Blood showed through Chun's bandage. He stared at it and did not cry.

I stood at the tree line. Clear morning light fell on the gathering as plainly as on the net hung beside Gao's door.

I could have warned him. I had known what the tooth would do from the moment Wei held it up, glowing, warm, and full of exactly the energy that drew exactly the consequences now bleeding through a bandage by the village well.

I had said put it down. I had not said why.

I thought he would figure it out himself.

He had.

The tooth lay in the mud. The crowd dispersed. Chun limped home. Wei stood alone by the well, staring at the ground.

I turned away. The path back to the forest was short. The distance between what I could have said and what I had said was considerably longer.
