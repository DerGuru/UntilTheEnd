Wei's hands started shaking on the second water run, between the spring and the boulder field. The transition required precise footing and sustained grip strength, which twelve-year-old boys acquire through practice rather than biology. His right hand released the stone he was using as a handhold. The fingers simply opened, the muscles overriding his intention with a truth that intention couldn't override: he was spent.

He caught himself. He grabbed the stone again with his left hand, shifted his weight, and found his balance. He moved on. The containers on his back sloshed, half-full, at least. Chun's leg had flared up that morning, the old spirit beast wound reopening under the daily stress of hill-climbing. So Wei and Gao had gone back out a second time.

How many days of this now? They blurred, each the same in outline. He woke before dawn, ran to the spring, carried water back, and distributed it. He checked the rations, walked the perimeter, helped Mrs. Liu with the well, and helped Elder Li with the structures. Then, only then, he ate his half-ration and collapsed beside a fire that was barely a fire. He slept the dense, impenetrable sleep of exhaustion.

He wasn't eating enough. I knew because I counted portions as I counted everything: automatically, continuously. He'd been giving them away. Quietly, without inviting anyone to admire the sacrifice. A handful of rice added to Mrs. Zhao's bowl when she wasn't watching. A dried fish slipped to the youngest Chun child, whose ribs had started showing. His own portion shrinking in increments so small that no single meal looked insufficient.

The cumulative deficit was still building.

His face was thinner. The softness was gone from his cheeks. The jawline was more defined than a twelve-year-old's should be. The eye sockets were deeper, shadowed, the skin under them carrying the dark, delicate bruising of chronic sleep deprivation.

He was burning himself out. Methodically.

You'd think watching would get easier with practice. It doesn't.

I considered intervention. The calculus was simple: I could tell him to stop. To rest. To eat his own food, sleep four watches, and let the adults carry what the adults should have been carrying from the beginning. The words were available. The authority was available. He would have listened because some part of him, the exhausted part, the twelve-year-old part that was still a child, was waiting for permission to stop.

I didn't intervene.

I wanted to. But he was learning something I couldn't teach him, something that couldn't be transmitted through instruction or correction or the careful, measured curriculum I'd been building since the first day he'd followed me home with mushrooms that would have killed him.

He was learning what it cost to be responsible. Cultivators who'd never skipped a meal discussed the theoretical weight of duty in elevated terms. Here was its physical, measurable cost: choosing other people's survival over your own comfort.

It would break him if it continued. I knew that too.

So I compensated in ways that didn't require permission.

At dusk, I'd come back with a small bundle of oily nuts and dropped them by Mrs. Liu's pot as if they'd been there all along. She'd eyed them, eyed me, and said nothing. She understood shortages. She also understood windfalls.

Tonight, he hadn't eaten at all. His portion sat by the fire: rice, a sliver of fish, and water. He'd sat down, looked at it, and left it untouched. His hands were in his lap. They were shaking.

The night was warm. The shaking came from exhaustion, structural, his body announcing through involuntary muscle contraction that it was reaching the end of what it had to give.

He looked at his hands. He turned them over. He examined the tremor as he'd examine an unfamiliar herb: with curiosity and distance, identifying a symptom rather than experiencing it.

He wrinkled his eyebrows. He opened his mouth and closed it again.

Not a word. That was his whole response to his own disintegration.

He picked up the rice bowl. He ate. Slowly, mechanically, without the pleasure that food should produce and without the complaint that its absence should generate. He ate because eating was maintenance, and necessary things were done without conversation.

The rice smelled of smoke and oil.

His hands stopped shaking by the third mouthful. The fuel arrived. His body stabilized.

He finished. He set the bowl down and looked at the fire.

"Tomorrow," he said.

"Yes."

"It'll be bad, won't it?"

"Yes."

He nodded, neither comforted nor surprised.

"Okay."

He lay down. He closed his eyes. He was asleep in thirty heartbeats.

His hands were still while he slept, curled against his chest, the tremor absent, the muscles finally given permission to stop performing. Small hands. Twelve years old. Carrying more than they should and refusing to put it down.

I didn't eat my own portion. I had no portion; the village's resources were not mine to claim. I looked at his empty bowl and thought about the grandmother who boiled ginger. About cost. About the space between teaching a boy to survive and watching him learn the one lesson that couldn't be taught without damage.

His hands were still. The fire was low. The horizon pulsed.

Tomorrow.
