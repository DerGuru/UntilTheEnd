The sun began to set and I discovered that I had been walking in circles. The path led downhill through a section of forest where the trees grew close enough that their canopies formed a single unbroken ceiling. I had been here before. This morning, seven days ago, or a century ago. The geography of forests stopped mattering after the first few thousand years. You saw one beech, you saw them all. I had stopped making much effort to distinguish them.

The light changed from gold to copper, then to the deep amber that preceded gray. Songbirds finished their last phrases in the canopy. Crickets grew louder, followed by the owl that lived in the dead oak one li north. I had been hearing that owl for three years. Before it, there had been another owl. Before that, another. The tree remained. The owls were temporary.

Everything was temporary.

Behind me, footsteps.

He had been following for the better part of half a watch. Quietly, for once, by his own standards: he'd reduced the twig-snapping from one per three steps to one per seven. An improvement. He had said nothing since "are you okay," which was either sensitivity or exhaustion. Knowing him, it was probably both. I corrected that thought. I did not know him. I had spent a long time keeping that particular doorway shut.

I reached a fork in the path. The left branch led toward the village, descending through undergrowth that thinned as the forest gave way to fields. The right branch went deeper, into old growth where the trees were thick enough to block the evening sky and the only sounds belonged to things that didn't sleep at night.

I took the right.

The path narrowed. Roots crossed it under layers of leaves. The canopy screened out most of the evening light, so I judged them by the slope more than by sight. The air cooled. Somewhere, condensation dripped from the canopy to the soil in a slow, uneven rhythm.

His footsteps followed, ten paces behind. The distance had been consistent for the last quarter watch, close enough to track me, far enough to retreat if I turned around. A negotiated distance. A treaty neither of us had discussed.

"Are we going to the village?" he asked.

"No."

"Where are we going?"

"Away."

He considered this openly, with his whole face, as he considered most things. Then his footsteps resumed. The questions stopped, leaving a longer stretch of quiet between us than I had grown used to hearing.

That was different. The boy had talked through every other moment we'd shared: through fear, through wonder, and through the aftermath of a beast dying two paces from his face. He plainly had more to ask. But each time I glanced back, he watched the path, granting me a quiet I had given him very little reason to grant.

We walked.

The forest closed around us. The path narrowed to a deer track between trunks older than most civilizations I'd watched rise and fall. Moss covered everything, green so deep it looked black in the dying light. Even his footsteps quieted on its soft surface. His breathing carried, but his movement had grown hard to distinguish from the small sounds around us.

A thick oak branch hung low across the path, heavy with lichen, angled downward by its own weight. It would hit my chest but catch him square in the face.

I reached up, pushed the branch aside, and held it.

The motion was small, my fingers against the bark, the wood bending gradually under my hand. I held it for one heartbeat. Two. Enough time for him to pass underneath without breaking stride.

I had dealt with the branch as readily as I stepped over roots. A small courtesy, given while I was already looking toward the next gap between the trees. Holding it for the boy had required less attention than finding a reason not to. Reflex. Habit. Or something older than either.

He went under the branch. I let it go. It swung back into place with a rustle, then grew still.

Neither of us mentioned it.

Three steps later: "Thank you."

"The branch was in the way."

"You held it for me."

"I held it because letting it swing back would have made noise."

He said nothing, like a child who has heard a lie and decided not to argue with it.

The sky above the canopy was purple now, darkening toward the brief evening blue that never repeated exactly. The first star appeared, or the first one I bothered to notice. Stars were another thing I'd stopped counting.

I walked.

He followed.

Ten paces. The evening sounds. The moss. The dark.

"You know?"

"What."

"The owl. It sounds like it's asking something."

I listened. Two notes from the dead oak, rising at the end. It was the pattern that had made him think of a question.

"Owls don't ask," I said. "They announce."

Quiet. Then, softer: "It sounds like asking to me."

I didn't correct him. The owl called again. It did sound like asking, if you were twelve and inclined to hear questions in everything.

At some point, he would turn back. The novelty would fade and gratitude would find its limits. He would return to his village, his father, his poisonous mushrooms, and his life. I would continue in the direction I had always been going, which was no direction at all.

That was what I told myself. You've noticed the pattern by now.

The forest grew darker. The path disappeared entirely, replaced by the soft uncertainty of ground that humans didn't walk on. My feet found their way without light, as they always had, since before light was something I craved.

Behind me, ten paces back, the boy's breathing steadied. He fell gradually into the rhythm of my steps, until I could hear the two keeping time together.

He didn't turn back.

I didn't stop.

"I'm not going back," he said. The words came slurred, heavy with approaching sleep.

"Nobody asked you to."

"Good." A yawn swallowed whatever came next. "Because I'm not."

He walked behind me until he didn't.

Somewhere past midnight, where the forest was darkest and the moss deepest, his steps slowed. The ten-pace distance stretched to twelve, then fifteen. There was a stumble, a correction, and a second stumble that ended with him sinking to his knees. Then came the soft, decisive sound of a twelve-year-old settling between two roots. His breathing deepened at once. He had been running on stubbornness and had run out.

I stopped in the dark with a sleeping boy a few paces behind me. The night sounds continued around us, spreading beyond anything I could see. The forest went on quite adequately around the two of us.

I could have left. I considered it with the ease of an old solution, while the distance between his breathing and my walking could still become permanent. The simplest exit. The kindest one, probably. He would wake in a forest he knew and find his way home. I would become a strange woman who'd killed a spirit beast, then faded into a memory he could eventually file under "probably a dream." Or he wouldn't wake again at all.

I didn't leave.

I sat against a birch. Ten paces was the distance we'd been walking and ten paces was the distance I kept. The forest's night sounds filled the space between us: the owl in the dead oak, the small rustlings of things that lived in leaf litter, and the slow dripping of condensation from branch to soil. Sounds that covered other sounds. Sounds whose absence would make me pay attention.

I wasn't watching him. I was facing the right direction, that was all. The birch happened to be positioned so that the gap between two trunks framed the patch of moss where he had folded himself between the roots. Coincidence. Geography. The forest had a limited number of comfortable birches and this one was the closest.

I wasn't watching him.

The night passed. His breathing changed once, with a shift, a murmur, and something half-spoken into the moss that faded before it became a word. His body curled tighter against the cold. The temperature had dropped slowly at first, then sharply as the last of the day's warmth faded under the canopy. The damp ground would be cold against his clothes.

He was twelve, sleeping in thin clothes with his knees drawn against his chest. I could observe that. Observation was something I had practiced for a very long time.

I didn't move closer. But the birch's qi, the old, slow warmth of a tree that had been processing sunlight for ninety years, radiated outward in a circle that happened to extend, if one were measuring, to approximately the spot where a boy lay curled between two roots. Birches did that. It wasn't me.
