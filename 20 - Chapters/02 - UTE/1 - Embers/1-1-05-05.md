He followed me, his steps loud in the undergrowth behind mine. Of course he followed me. I had just disintegrated a spirit beast with my index finger and he had the survival instincts of a moth flying toward a lantern. Following the woman who could do that was probably the safest option available. Or the most interesting one. I wasn't sure which motivation was worse.

Every third step, a twig snapped. Every fifth, he stumbled over a root and barely caught himself. His breathing was heavy from exertion, from the lingering strain of almost dying, and from the swollen ankle that made every stride painful. Stealth was clearly beyond him. Talking, however, took him no effort at all.

"Hey! Wait!"

I did not wait.

"HEY! I'm talking to you!"

I walked just fast enough that he had to jog to keep up. I could have been on the other side of the mountain before his next breath, but this was the increase I chose. His basket bounced against his back with each stride, producing a hollow wooden clatter that harmonized poorly with his footsteps.

He caught up and fell into step next to me. Half a step behind and to the left, he adjusted his position whenever the path narrowed, uncertain how close I would let him come.

"What was that?"

Leaves crunched under my feet. The morning light shifted as the canopy thickened, leaving small patches of gold along the path. I stepped from one into the next while he waited for an answer.

"What did you *do*? You just—" He gestured behind us. "It was there and then it wasn't. How did you do that?"

A bird sang three notes from somewhere above us. I recognized the species. I had heard its ancestors, back when the melody had fewer notes.

"Are you a cultivator?"

There was a bend in the path ahead where the ground sloped downward toward a creek. The water would be cold. I considered walking through it, on the assumption that he wouldn't follow through cold water on a bad ankle. Then I considered the possibility that he would. He would probably slip, leaving me to listen to the splashing.

"Why are you out here alone? Nobody lives out here. Trust me, I know. I come out here all the time and there's nothing. Just trees, beasts, and mushrooms. And you, apparently."

I stepped over a fallen log. He climbed over it, badly, hooking his good leg over first and dragging the swollen ankle behind him.

"What's your name?"

"No."

He paused. For exactly one heartbeat he was silent. Then:

"'No' isn't a name. That's not how that works. I asked what your name *is*, not whether you have one. Everybody has a name."

My jaw tightened. One syllable. I'd given him one syllable and he'd turned it into a doorway. That was the danger of answering. With the smallest encouragement, he could treat my presence as permission to continue indefinitely.

"My father says that if someone won't tell you their name, it means they've done something they're ashamed of. Have you done something you're ashamed of?"

The creek appeared through the trees. Shallow. Clear. Stones on the bottom, worn smooth. A salamander sat on one of them.

"You don't look like someone who's ashamed. You look like someone who's tired. My mother looks like that sometimes. After harvest season, mostly. But that's a different kind of tired."

I crossed the creek. The water came up to my ankles and felt like nothing, because temperature stopped mattering around the same century that names did.

He crossed behind me, splashing loudly enough to send the salamander under a stone.

The silence lasted four steps. Then his tone changed.

"My father is sick."

The pitch dropped. The rhythm slowed. Still a child's voice, but one that had learned a register that no child should know. He wasn't asking anymore. He was explaining.

"He—" The boy swallowed. "He coughs. Blood, sometimes. The herbs from the market stopped working months ago and we can't afford the ones from the city. So I come to the forest. I collect what I can. I bring it home. I make the medicine myself."

He was quiet, eyes on the path, working toward the next sentence. It came more slowly than all the questions had.

"The harvest was bad. The rain came wrong. Too much, then none, then too much again. Half the village lost their fields. We had nothing saved. My mother sells baskets at the crossroad market, but that's a watch's walk each way. She leaves before sunrise. She doesn't always come back with money."

The forest opened into a small clearing. Sun fell through the gap in the canopy, warming a circle of grass that hadn't been trimmed by anything but deer. Small white wildflowers grew at the edge, the kind that closed at dusk and opened at dawn. The air was warm here, sheltered, gentle.

I stopped in the middle of the clearing. I stood with the sun on my face, listening to him breathe behind me.

He stopped too, three steps behind me, still breathing hard.

"Winter is coming. You know that. The first frost is a month out, maybe less. After that, the forest is empty. No herbs, no mushrooms, nothing. If I don't find something that works before then..."

He didn't finish the sentence.

"You killed that beast with *one finger*. You have to know about herbs. You have to know which ones are right."

I stood in the clearing. The sun was warm on my hair and meant nothing.

"I thought I saw someone. In the forest, before the boar came. You looked at my mushrooms. At my basket. You had this expression like—" He paused. "Like you already knew something was wrong with them."

Quiet. He stood behind my shoulder, close enough that I heard every breath he took while waiting for me to turn.

"They were wrong, weren't they? The mushrooms. They were the wrong ones."

The answer sat in my throat like a stone. I didn't owe him anything. I didn't owe anyone anything. I had stopped keeping accounts approximately four thousand years ago, give or take a dynasty.

But the mushrooms. The wrong ones. He would bring them home to someone who would drink the tea, smile, and say it was working.

I said nothing. I walked.

He followed.
