He found me before noon the next day. I was sitting on a rock by the creek, the same creek from before, the one with the stones and the salamander. The salamander was back. It sat on its stone, motionless next to the woman who had turned a spirit beast to dust yesterday. I could sit motionless too, although I suspected the resemblance ended there.

The water was doing what water always did, finding the lowest path, wearing the stones, and murmuring ceaselessly without somehow becoming boring. Sunlight broke across the rippling surface. I sat where it reached the rock, letting the sun warm my skin. It was good. Simple. The kind of good that required nothing from me.

The boy announced himself well before he emerged from the tree line. Branches cracked. Leaves scattered. He burst into the clearing wild and staggering, running on a bad ankle. His face changed as soon as he saw me, the tightness around his mouth giving way to a grin.

I was here.

"I knew it!" He was grinning. Limping, dirty, grinning. "I knew you'd still be around. Nobody just *leaves* after something like that. You can't turn a spirit beast into powder and then just walk off. That's not how things work!"

It was, in fact, exactly how that worked. I had been doing it for millennia.

He sat down a few paces away, leaving a cautious stretch of bank between us. His basket was on his back again. Still empty. The ankle was still swollen, but already less purple; he had wrapped it with a strip of cloth that looked like it had been torn from a shirt.

"So, okay. You don't talk. That's fine. I can talk enough for both of us. My mother says that's my problem. I talk too much. But my father says talking is just thinking out loud and thinking is free, so talking should be too."

He pulled a reed from the bank and began stripping it. His hands moved with absent-minded ease; he had done this a thousand times. Village hands. Working hands.

"I figured it out, by the way. The mushrooms. You were right. They were wrong. I showed them to Old Chen. He's the closest thing we have to a healer, which isn't very close. He went pale. He said they looked like Blushing Caps. He said they'd have—" He looked down at the reed. "Well. They were wrong."

He tossed the stripped reed into the creek and watched it spin in the current.

"So... thanks. For not telling me. Kind of."

Something twitched at the corner of my mouth, so briefly that I could pretend it hadn't happened. I turned toward the creek before he could notice. He was already talking again, looking for another reed.

He went on talking. Of course he did. He talked about the forest: which trails were safe, which the spirit beasts used, and where the good mushrooms grew. He talked about the village: who was kind, who was difficult, and who owed whom what. His mother's baskets were very good, apparently, but the merchants at the crossroad market cheated her because she came alone. He wanted to go with her to make sure they didn't. She wouldn't let him, because someone had to stay with Baba.

The sun moved. Shadows rotated. Water continued over the stones. I listened, because listening was the one thing I could not turn off and his voice filled a silence I had grown tired of filling myself.

Then he said something.

I don't remember the words themselves. Something about the creek, the stones, or how the light fell on the water. I remember his cadence, the rising inflection at the end that made a statement into half a question. He watched me as if everything in the world invited a response and he couldn't understand why anyone would decline it.

My foot hung above a patch of moss. The forest smelled of damp earth and pine resin. The creek murmured beneath the boy's voice, with that rising note still clear among his words.

I knew that voice.

A different voice had sounded like that once: the same rising note, the same half-question, and the same assumption that silence was a problem to be solved. Someone had walked beside me. Or ahead of me. Or just near enough that their voice reached me without trying.

I reached for a face, but the memory held only warmth. Something like laughter, distant and distorted, as if I were hearing it through a wall. Through years. Through a distance that isn't measured in li.

Then silence. I remembered a departure, the particular quiet of a space that used to be occupied. A chair pushed back from a table. A door left open after the person who had used it was gone. Death had its own sound; this memory held the smaller noises of somebody leaving.

He was gone.

The memory broke off so abruptly that I drew a sharp breath. The creek came back into focus: the moss under my raised foot, the boy beside his basket. Damp earth filled my nose. I was still in the forest.

The boy was still talking.

"And then he said you can tell by the underside of the cap. The pattern of the gills. If they're pink, they're good. If they're white, you should probably not—"

I set my foot down on the moss and reached for a branch at my side. I needed something to hold. Rough bark pressed into my palm, each ridge distinct enough to keep my attention here. I had buried that other place so deeply that I should have been able to stand beside a creek without finding it again.

The branch snapped in my hand. Of course it did. Everything I held broke eventually.

"Are you okay?"

I turned to him. The boy. Twelve years old. Dirty. Limping. Alive.

"Fine," I said.

That was...

I let the broken branch fall. It was nothing. I could still get up and start walking.
