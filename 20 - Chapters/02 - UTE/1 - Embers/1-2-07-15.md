The river was slow that morning. Fog lay close to its surface, moving in currents apart from the water underneath. Mud softened the banks, and dark green moss grew wherever fresh water met shade. Wei sat on a stone above it.

His posture was a child's attempt at patience: straight-backed, legs crossed, and hands resting on his knees with more effort than the position should have required. His left foot twitched. His right hand made small adjustments to his trousers. A muscle in his jaw pulsed as he held his mouth closed.

He had been waiting when I arrived. I didn't know how early he'd come. The dew on his shoulders suggested before dawn. The fact that he'd said nothing when he saw me suggested something closer to desperation.

I stood at the river's edge. Water moved over stones older than the village, older than the forest, and older than anything else in the valley except me. Fog drifted cool against my ankles.

I could walk away. I had walked away from burned cities, broken covenants, and students who had shown more promise than this boy could dream of. Better reasons to stay than a child sitting by a river. Walking away was what I did. It was the only thing I did with genuine competence.

"Sit still."

My voice came out flat over the water. He hadn't asked a question. I had given him an instruction anyway, standing there with all my excellent reasons for doing something else.

Wei blinked.

"Don't speak. Don't move. Sit still."

He froze mid-blink. Then he unclenched his jaw, settled his shoulders, and straightened his spine. He still held himself too rigidly, working at the position instead of resting in it. He closed his eyes.

"Listen."

"To what?"

"I said don't speak."

He closed his mouth.

The fog drifted over the unhurried murmur of water. Faint movement stirred in the canopy, though the nearest birds had stopped calling. I listened to his heartbeat, fast with anticipation at first. Gradually it slowed as he sat there, waiting for something more dramatic than stillness.

Ten heartbeats passed.

"How long do I—"

"Longer."

He swallowed the rest of the question. His foot twitched once, then twice before growing still. His breathing deepened as the effort of keeping quiet eased. Air moved the fog around his shoulders. Ordinary weather could produce a pleasing effect: the mist made him look more accomplished than he was.

A hundred slow breaths passed. Then another hundred. Morning light strengthened through the fog as the sun rose. His face relaxed, the furrow between his eyebrows softening. I hadn't noticed that crease forming during our mornings together, although he had plenty to worry about once he went home. His hands unclenched.

"What do you hear?" I asked.

"The water. Birds. Wind in the leaves." His voice was slower than usual, as if he had to finish speaking before he could pick out the next sound.

"What else?"

He listened with his shoulders relaxed. His head tilted, turning toward something I knew was too faint for his ordinary hearing. He kept his attention on it and didn't open his eyes for help.

"Something..." He hesitated. "In the ground. Not a sound exactly. More like... humming? Very low. Very far away."

He hadn't opened his eyes. His face was turned downward, toward the stone beneath him, as if he could hear through his knees.

I said nothing.

He was right. Old qi currents ran through the valley's bedrock, residual flows too faint for most cultivators below Foundation Establishment to detect. Their hum was felt more than heard. A boy with only his natural sensitivity had distinguished a trace of it, despite having done so little to develop his meridians.

Remarkable was the word I almost allowed myself. I had stopped using it for a reason. Remarkable led to investment. Investment led to attachment. I looked down at the water before following that thought any farther.

He sat cross-legged on the stone, head tilted, hands on his knees. His back needed straightening. His weight was unevenly distributed, with his chin too far forward. Still, I knew the shape of him against the morning fog, the sun beginning to show through behind his shoulders.

Someone else had sat like that.

The cold smell of the river was enough to bring the memory close. I had thought it buried well beyond the reach of a boy sitting badly on a stone.

A different river, wider and colder, had carried gray snowmelt from mountains that no longer existed. Herbs lay on its bank, the right ones because I had taught him which to choose. He'd listened with the same attention Wei gave me, leaning closer whenever I pointed out a difference, holding the plant until he understood.

His hands had been larger than Wei's, callused by years of grinding herbs in the stone mortar he carried everywhere. I remembered those rough places clearly enough to distinguish them from a farmer's hands. He had learned quickly through diligence rather than talent.

Occasionally, his questions had made me stop. They reached past the name of a plant into things I'd scarcely considered in years. Why does this root heal and that one kill, when they grow in the same soil? I had begun taking time over my answers. That was how it happened: I turned toward someone until I looked forward to the next question. Then I had to live with the quiet after they stopped asking.

A bird cut across the fog. Blue and fast. It vanished into the reeds, leaving the air trembling.

In the memory, the herbs were still on the bank. Brown and brittle, curling at the edges, left exactly where he'd placed them.

One day the herbs had withered and he was gone.

I blinked at brown water flowing past the stone. Wei sat small against the lifting fog, with the mountains still visible behind him. I held my eyes on the nearest ridge until its shape was clear again.

"You okay?"

The question was soft. He'd opened his eyes to look at me instead of the river. He had noticed the change in my attention, though he couldn't know what I had remembered. He waited more carefully than a twelve-year-old should have needed to.

I brushed my knees and stood up. A small human gesture that gave me something to finish before I looked at him again. I had little practical need of such gestures. I made them anyway.

"Enough for today."

He glanced at the sky. "That was only a quarter watch."

"And?"

He opened his mouth, closed it, and considered. Then he nodded, leaving the protest where it was instead of trying again.

He stood, picked up his basket, and turned toward the village path. Before leaving, he stopped to ask:

"Same time tomorrow?"

I did not answer. He left.

The river moved over its stones. The fog finished lifting. Full morning brought light, warmth, and the ordinary sounds of animals moving through the undergrowth. Nothing in those sounds had changed because of what I remembered. I listened to them until I could let the other river recede.

I stayed by the river for a long time.
