I came in the night, past the shuttered houses. The village slept lightly, uneasily, as villages sleep during hard times. People lay with one ear turned toward the forest and the other toward the crying of children who'd absorbed their parents' anxiety through walls too thin to keep anything out. A dog whimpered somewhere. A shutter creaked.

Wei's house was dark. His mother had fallen asleep in the chair by the pallet, her head tipped forward, her chin on her chest. Her hands were still folded in her lap as if she'd been praying and lost the argument with exhaustion mid-sentence. Wei lay on the pallet, his splinted arm elevated on a folded blanket, his face turned toward the ceiling. His eyes were open.

The door didn't creak when I entered. Or it did and neither of us acknowledged it.

"You," he said.

"Quiet."

He closed his mouth. His eyes tracked me across the room, wide, alert, and sharpened by pain into a focus that his training hadn't yet achieved on its own. Sweat glistened on his forehead. The splint smelled of camphor and desperation. His skin was hot with the low-grade fever of a body fighting inflammation with the only tools it had.

I knelt at the pallet.

His arm lay between us on the blanket, swollen, purple-black at the joint. The splint held the bones in approximately the right alignment. Approximately was the best a village healer could do. Approximately wasn't enough.

I placed my hand over the break.

The contact was light, my palm on the wrapping, fingers curving around the forearm without pressure. My skin was cool. It was always cool.

"What are you—"

"*Quiet.*"

He went quiet.

I closed my eyes. Closing them narrowed the world to touch and frequency. What I was about to do required precision at a scale that made surgery look like guesswork.

I released qi.

A thread. Less than a thread: a filament, thinner than a spider's silk, carrying just enough energy to join the bone fragments along their broken edges. I guided it through the wrapping, through the swelling, and into the fracture zone. Three pieces of ulna sat where the village healer had managed to place them, still short of their original alignment.

The fragments moved.

Wei's jaw clenched. His back arched involuntarily as the bones shifted inside his arm. It was not pleasant: a deep, grinding pull, accompanied by a warmth that started at the bone and radiated outward through the soft tissue. Relief mixed with the pain as each fragment returned to its place, the easing of a dislocated joint slipping into its socket.

The first fragment aligned. I held it, then fused the fracture line with a pulse of qi so precise that the bone's crystalline structure reformed along its original grain.

The second fragment. Smaller, more displaced, and lodged in soft tissue that had already begun to swell around it. I moved it. The tissue tightened against the broken edge. I eased it apart. The fragment slid home.

The third. The joint itself, with its damaged articulation surface, torn cartilage, and ligaments stretched past their elastic limit. This took longer. Cartilage was slower to rebuild than bone; it required coaxing rather than commanding. Coaxing with qi was like trying to whisper through a storm. I reduced my output to next to nothing. A breath of energy. A suggestion.

The joint reformed.

A hundred and eighty heartbeats. Two hundred forty. Under my hand, the swelling receded as the excess fluid redistributed, absorbed back into the tissue faster than any healer would call sane.

The air *shimmered*.

For one heartbeat, too brief to count and too strange to dismiss, the world hesitated. The edge of the window light looked wrong. The walls felt thin, like paper held up to a lamp. Wei's face, my hand, and the splint went flat, then deep again, as if something behind reality had blinked.

Then it was gone. The world resumed.

"What was that?" Wei whispered.

His voice was careful, held low, held close, and carrying something he wasn't sure he wanted to put down. He'd seen it. He'd felt it.

"Nothing. You're tired."

He shook his head, his eyes still on the window. He didn't believe me. But the moment was already fading, dissolving into the unreliable architecture of memory. There it would settle alongside dreams, déjà vu, and the thousand other small impossibilities that the human mind filed under *probably didn't happen*.

"Flex your hand," I said.

He flexed his hand. The fingers curled and uncurled. The wrist rotated. The elbow, the joint that the healer had declared permanently compromised, bent and straightened with the fluid, painless ease of something that had never been broken.

He stared at his hand.

I stood up.

The warmth of his skin lingered against my cool palm. A memory surfaced, uninvited, as they always did.

A girl. Five or six. Hands blistered from a forge fire, held out to me, palms up. Silent. I remembered healing her, the burns retreating under my fingers from red to pink to smooth. She cried when it stopped hurting.

Three years later, I found the forge cold. The village gone. The girl was ash.

That was when I stopped healing. It was the most rational decision I ever made. It was the only decision I could not defend.

Now I had healed the arm of a boy who would die. The arm was flexing in the moonlight, and he was staring at me with an expression I could not meet. I know. You're wondering why I did it. I'm still wondering.

"Go to sleep," I said.

"But—"

"Sleep."

He lay back. His arm, whole, sound, and functional, rested at his side. His eyes closed.

I left. The door did not creak.

Outside, the stars were cold in an empty sky. I stood in the dark for a long time, doing nothing, feeling nothing, and convincing no one.
