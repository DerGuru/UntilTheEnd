I arrived at the camp to find people covering the fire pits. Blankets rolled, packs shouldered, and children gathered. The camp was being *concluded.* They were getting ready to go home.

Wei was at the front, counting heads, checking the line. As I learned afterward, he'd made the call before I arrived. Before the shockwave. Before the tremor, before the pressure wave, and before the single tile that fell from a building none of them were inside. He'd felt it coming. The qi-sensitivity I'd built into his training, the third-step pattern, the foot-reading, and the awareness of ambient pressure had alerted him as a falling wind alerts a sailor.

"Pack up. We're going home."

He said it with the authority of a finished calculation. He wasn't interested in discussing alternatives. Elder Li had looked at him, the old man's face doing the recalculation it had been doing since the village square, and nodded. Chun had stood up. Gao had stood up. Mrs. Liu had folded blankets. The community had begun to move together. The boy at its center was simply facing the right direction and walking.

I fell in at the back of the line.

A child glanced over his shoulder, saw me, and looked away. Mrs. Liu's gaze flicked once, to my hands, to my face, and moved on. Elder Li did not turn fully. The angle of his head changed a fraction, acknowledgment without ceremony.

No one said anything.

The village was damaged. The distinction I'd been maintaining since the barriers went up, between repair and restoration, still held. Between surviving and living. Two more tiles had fallen. The fence was down again. Rebuilt twice, reinforced once, and still the source of ongoing philosophical debate about the structural integrity of salvaged lumber. A window in Elder Li's house had cracked. Dust everywhere. The ever-present, drought-amplified dust that converted every event into a cloud and every cloud into a coating.

But the walls stood. The houses stood. The grain shed held. The water containers were intact. The stream, fed by the spring that Wei had found and the channel I'd widened, was running clearer than it had in many days.

Clearer.

I paused and tested the water. The qi-contamination had dropped. Sharply. Xu Ran's consolidation had shifted; the siphon effect that had been draining the valley's qi for so many days was easing. The final compression had pulled his energy inward, away from the valley, concentrating it into the tight spiral that preceded breakthrough. The drain had slowed to a trickle.

The well would recover. The mineral contamination would take many days to flush. Maybe longer, depending on what the shock had stirred up.

But it would recover. The fields would rehydrate. The trees on the eastern ridge would grow new leaves. The valley would heal as living things do when a parasite departs and they have strength left to recover.

The village recognized this slowly, the realization passing from person to person. Mrs. Liu tasted the stream water first. Her face changed, the tension around her eyes easing after so long it had become architectural. She said nothing. Chun tasted it next. Then Elder Li. Then the children, who didn't understand water quality but understood that the adults had relaxed. The relaxation of adults is the most reliable signal of safety that children know.

Wei stood in the village center. The same spot where he'd stood before. He looked around. At the fallen tiles, the broken fence, the dust, and the cracked window. At the standing walls, the intact roof, the grain shed, and the stream.

He nodded.

A private nod, cataloging the results without judgment: what held, what fell, what needed repair, and what could wait. A future leader finishing his first campaign, and older for it.

I stood at the edge of the village, my usual position, the space between belonging and observing. My hands were in my pockets, a gesture I hadn't made in centuries. I didn't know what else to do with them.

"He's learned it," I said quietly.

The words hung in the air. Simple. True. Insufficient.

He'd learned it.

Good.

A pause.

He'd need it.
