Wei arrived the next morning with dead herbs in his basket. He swung it off his shoulder and held it open for me to see, his expression suggesting that I owed him an explanation for something the forest had done.

The Mingwort was brown at the edges. The Silverthorn had gone limp, its normally rigid stems drooping over the basket's rim. Even Whitestem, which Wei had learned was decorative at best, had blackened. It looked far older than anything gathered the day before.

"These were fresh *yesterday*," he said. "I picked them at the good spot, the one by the creek bend. They were perfect. Green. Firm. The right ones. I checked the edges twice." He pulled out a Mingwort specimen and held it up. The cap's margin was clearly serrated. He had identified it correctly, harvested it cleanly, and brought home something that should have kept its strength. It was completely dead.

"What happened to them?"

I took the Mingwort from his hand and turned it over. Its flesh was intact, with none of the marks frost or disease would leave. Hardly any qi remained in it. Energy that ordinarily circulated through the soil, the roots, and the fine web of fungi under them was being drawn away. The herb had exhausted what it needed to live as surely as a lamp with its oil supply cut off.

I set the mushroom down.

"Pick closer to the village," I said again.

"I did pick closer to the village! This IS closer. Three days ago, the good spot was by the third ridge. Now everything past the creek is dead." He gestured toward the forest with the hand that wasn't holding a dead mushroom. "The next good spot will be the village garden and Mrs. Bai's cabbages are already suspicious of me."

The village had begun to notice small changes too. Gao pulled empty nets from the river. One poor catch would have meant little. Three days of them made him inspect the water for longer before casting again. The fish had followed the spirit beasts downstream, toward water that still carried enough qi to sustain them.

Children were kept indoors. From the village the forest looked the same, paths under a clear sky, but fewer people let their children use them alone. Mrs. Bai caught her youngest by the sleeve as he tried to slip toward the path. She pulled him back to her side without looking up.

Mrs. Zhao stood outside her house each evening and stared at the tree line. She had been doing this for seven days. When Wei asked what she was looking at, she told him about the Year of the Rat, forty years earlier. A similar silence had fallen on the valley. Something came out of the deep forest one night; three goats and a woodcutter were lost before it vanished without anybody identifying it.

"She thinks it's happening again," Wei reported. "The old people remember. My mother remembers too. Her grandfather told stories about lights in the forest. Then the well went dry for a month."

I mapped the pattern. The qi depletion was expanding from a point roughly sixty li northwest at a rate that suggested continuous, aggressive cultivation. The source moved every two or three days, following the spirit beasts as they fled.

The dead herb in my hand brought back another valley. I had watched a young man make the same progression there: he hunted, consumed, expanded his range, and began again. He'd burned through everything within seven days' travel before anyone thought to object. By then there was little left with which to resist him.

Wei's dead Mingwort sat between us, its edges curling inward.

At the current rate, the depletion would reach the village in twelve days. Ten if the cultivator sped up, which he probably would. They usually did. I knew the progression as I knew rain by its smell, from too many valleys.

"What's causing it?" Wei asked, more quietly than usual. He stared at the dead mushroom. The boy who had screamed at a spirit beast on our first morning was learning to ask about what frightened him.

I could have told him. A young man with more talent than sense was hunting through the forest, taking what he needed for the next stage while everything around him withered. I could have explained how often it happened. Every generation produced people willing to climb over the broken bodies of those below them. After enough repetitions, whole societies learned to speak of that cost as something ordinary.

I could have told him a lot of things.

"Wind," I said.

He gave me a look that said, clearly and without ambiguity, that he did not believe a single syllable of that answer.

"Okay," he said. "Wind."

He gathered his dead herbs, put them back in his basket, and stood up. He didn't ask again. I suspected my lies told him more than I intended. He could pick out the subjects I avoided, even if I gave him no help understanding them.

After he left, I sat by the river and listened to how little of the forest was left. I calculated how long it would take for the remaining sounds to disappear from this bank too.
