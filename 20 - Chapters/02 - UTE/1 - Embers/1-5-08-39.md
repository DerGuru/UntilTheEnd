I was checking the barriers under a cloudless sky when thunder came from the forest. About one li to the east. A deep, concussive sound that struck through my chest, a vibration as distinct as the noise itself. Above the trees, the sky was the same empty blue it had been for twenty-two consecutive days. Around me, everything small enough to hide went quiet.

Trees snapped. The sound was different from wind-break, sharper, more violent, the crack of wood crushed through rather than bent. They fell in sequence, one after another, toppling outward from a central point. Each falling crown caught the branches of the next.

Then the scream.

A spirit beast, large, cornered, and dying. The sound was wrong as dying sounds are always wrong: too loud, too sustained, and too much like a human. You have to know that spirit beasts vocalize differently from animals. Their cry carries qi, and the qi carries an emotional signature that penetrates deeper than sound alone. This one carried terror, raw enough to tighten my fingers against the stone. The beast was facing something so far above it that there was nowhere it could run.

The scream cut off.

Silence followed. A pebble dropped from the wall beside my hand and clicked against the others.

Then a massive amount of qi exploded.

I'd been expecting something of this kind. Xu Ran had been hunting in a tightening spiral for days, each kill closer to the village than the last, each one releasing a burst of qi as the spirit beast's core destabilized and discharged its stored energy. The previous ones had been distant enough that the village felt them as pressure changes: ears popping, headaches, and children becoming irritable without knowing why.

This one was close.

The shockwave rolled out from the kill site in concentric waves.

It hit my barriers.

Stone took it, split it, and forced it down the trench toward the river. What slipped past scattered against the granite ridge, breaking into fragments that reached the village as a gust of warm wind and the brief, ugly feeling of standing up too fast.

The village felt it. Loose thatch tore free and spiraled. Ripples appeared in the water barrels. A fence, the same fence Wei had repaired three days ago, cracked at its weakest joint and leaned sideways. The old rooster on Mrs. Zhao's roof flapped, screamed, and fell off the edge, landing with more dignity than the situation warranted.

Two roof tiles slid loose from Elder Li's house and shattered on the ground. A child started crying. Another child joined. Then a dog howled and another dog answered. For thirty heartbeats the village was a chorus of panic, with no source it could identify and no target it could negotiate with.

Then it passed. The wave dissipated. The warm wind died. In the quiet that followed, I heard the village again, one sound at a time: a bird, a voice, and a door opening.

Only then did my mind do what it always did: measure.

My barriers held.

Barely. The second stone wall had shifted, two stones fallen, the angle compromised. Another explosion at the same intensity and the wall would redirect three-tenths instead of three-fifths.

That had been a hunting kill, chaotic, uncontrolled. Xu Ran's actual breakthrough would be worse. Focused. Sustained.

My barriers would not be enough.

The village would survive in the technical sense. But the well would sour. The spring planting would fail before it began. The village would face the slow arithmetic of starvation: how many mouths, how much grain, and how many days.

I was still calculating when Wei ran past me.

He'd heard the scream. He'd heard the fence crack. When a child cried his name from the square, thin and panicked, he broke into a run without a word to me.

Toward the forest.

He was fast, faster than he'd been a month ago, the qi-steps giving him speed that surprised me even though I'd taught him the technique. He moved through the clearing at the village's edge without slowing, angling toward the tree line, toward the source of the explosion, and toward the cultivator still hunting there.

I was in front of him before he reached the trees.

He didn't see me move. One moment I was behind him, thirty paces back, standing on the ridge where I'd been checking the barriers. The next moment I was here, between him and the forest, close enough that he had to stop or collide with me.

He stopped anyway.

His feet skidded in the dirt. His breath hitched. His eyes went wide with the shock of his world briefly failing to behave.

No one else was close enough to see it cleanly. Dust. Bodies moving. People looking for children, fallen tiles, and a rooster that had become a projectile. If anyone noticed a blur at the tree line, they would file it under panic and keep working.

"No."

"But there's—"

"*No.*"

I had spoken quietly, keeping my voice controlled. But something in it made him stop as a wall makes you stop. Something I hadn't authorized, hadn't intended, and couldn't recall putting there. He could see my hands at my sides. I simply stood in his path, absolutely present.

He looked at my face. I don't know what he saw. I know what I did: my hands curled once, hard, and then uncurled. My jaw locked. The cold in my chest had edges.

I heard that scream again when I looked at him.

"Go to the village," I said. "Help with the cleanup."

"Yun—"

"The fence needs fixing. Two roof tiles fell from Elder Li's house. Mrs. Zhao's rooster fell off the roof. He'll need catching."

"What about—"

"Go."

He went. He turned slowly, reluctantly, with the visible effort of a boy overriding every instinct he had, and walked toward the village. He looked back once. I was standing where he'd left me, facing the forest, and I did not move until he was far enough away that the distance was its own barrier.

Then I looked into the trees.

One li. Xu Ran's signature was settling, consolidating.

Five days. Maybe less.

I stood at the tree line for a long time. The forest smelled like ozone, broken wood, and sharp wrongness that lingers after a creature dies from qi-discharge rather than physical trauma. The trees that still stood leaned away from the kill site, their branches pointing outward, bent by a force that had already passed.

Then I walked back to the village. The fence needed fixing. The tiles needed replacing. The rooster needed catching.

I needed to not think about what would happen when five days became zero and the barriers weren't enough.

I had already decided what I would not do.
