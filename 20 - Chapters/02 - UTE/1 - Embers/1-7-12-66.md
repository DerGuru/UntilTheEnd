Night settled around the intact hut. One of six structures in the village that had survived without significant damage, a fact that was partially my doing, though the how would have been difficult to explain and I wasn't going to try. The walls stood. The roof held. The mat on the floor was the same mat, in the same position, with the same threadbare sections in the same places.

Wei was on the mat. Sleeping.

The glow was almost gone. Faint traces at his wrists. A thin line at the side of his neck, following the path of the secondary channel I'd restructured. Barely visible unless you had the vision to see qi-flow through skin, muscle, and bone.

His face was relaxed. Twelve years old. The premature lines that field work, drought, and insufficient food had carved into his features were smoothed by the unconscious honesty of sleep. He looked young. He looked his age.

His channels were wider. Stronger. The flood had reshaped the existing architecture and left something new in its place. The new architecture was better. The foundation I'd built slowly, brick by brick through all those days of basic exercises, corrected breathing, and deliberate, controlled exposure had held.

He would be different when he woke up. He would feel the world differently. He would hear things he hadn't heard and sense things he hadn't sensed. He would understand, from direct experience rather than instruction, why I had made him breathe in counts, stand in postures, and repeat exercises that had seemed pointless. Until the moment they became the only thing between him and permanent damage.

He would have questions.

I sat next to him. Legs drawn up. Hands on my knees.

The hut was quiet. The village was quiet. The quiet that follows crisis, the hush of a community that has survived something and is holding still to make sure the survival takes.

I watched him breathe.

He shifted once. His hand moved, reaching for something that wasn't there, or pushing something away. "...still here," he murmured. The words had no context. Sleep-talk. His hand settled. His breathing evened again.

Sometime later, after half a watch or a full one, I stood. The measurement didn't matter. I made no sound. I'd been moving without sound for longer than this village had existed, longer than this province had been named, and longer than the language they spoke had differentiated itself from its parent tongue.

Wei didn't stir. His breathing held. The pattern continued, stable, autonomous, and permanent.

I opened the door and stepped out.

The air was cold, properly cold, winter cold. The residual warmth from the tribulation's energy was faint, already losing to the season. The sky was clear. The stars were out.

The path to the hilltop took no thought. The flat stone was there at the top, where I sat and watched. It was cool when I settled onto it, chilled by the night air at this height. The cold seeped up through cloth and skin without urgency.

The village was below. Dark. Quiet. Intact.

The houses, the fields, and the well with its cracked wall that someone would repair tomorrow. The granary with its dwindling stores. The path to the river where the trench still ran, raw earth, exposed stone, the scar of intervention visible even in starlight. It would silt in. It would grass over. In a year there would be a shallow depression. In five, nothing.

The forest stretched to the north. Damaged. The trees nearest the epicenter were dead, stripped, bleached, their wood drained of something more fundamental than moisture. But the damage ended. There was a boundary, a rough circle, and outside it the forest was intact. Dark. Animal sounds, faint, tentative, the first returns of creatures that had fled and were venturing back between the trees.

A bird called once, then went quiet.

Xu Ran's signature was to the south. Far south. Moving fast, like a man who had met something he couldn't process and chosen distance.

He would process it eventually. He would return to the question, if not to this village, this stretch of unremarkable territory where nothing was supposed to be. He was the type that needed categories, explanations, and coherent frameworks. He would spend years building them rather than accept something that did not fit.

That was a problem for another time.

The stars turned. Slowly, imperceptibly, their rotation continuing whether I watched or not. I watched them as I had watched them for thousands of years, without expectation, without interpretation, and without the human compulsion to extract meaning from pattern.

The village was dark.

Wei was sleeping.

The air smelled like scorched wood, wet earth, and the faint, sweet residue of dispersed qi fading into the natural background.

I sat on the hilltop. I looked over the valley.

And I waited.
