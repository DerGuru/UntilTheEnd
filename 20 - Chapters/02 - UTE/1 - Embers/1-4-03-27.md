His shout reached me on the porch, where the tea was still steaming in my hands. It was the shout a prospector makes when he finds gold in his pan, or a child makes when the present under the wrapping is the thing they'd been afraid to hope for. Through the open doorway, he sat up, held his arm out, and turned it. He flexed it, made a fist, and opened the fist. He rotated the wrist, then bent the elbow, performing a systematic inventory of function with the thoroughness of someone checking cargo against a manifest.

"It WORKS."

I'd been on the porch since before dawn, which was a watch ago. I was drinking Mrs. Zhao's tea, the chrysanthemum-barley blend she left on the stump each morning, wordless and unfailing, as if she had decided something and saw no reason to discuss it. The tea was adequate. The morning was cool. The village was waking up around me with the usual sounds: dogs, chickens, and someone hammering something that had come loose in the night.

Wei burst through the door. The splint was off. He'd removed it himself, which the healer would later describe as "irresponsible," "baffling," and "actually, how is that arm—" before trailing off into a silence that had no medical precedent.

"You—" He pointed at me with the hand that should have been crippled. "You did this."

"I've been out here all night."

"My arm was *shattered*."

"You heal fast."

"It was *shattered*. The healer said—"

"The healer said many things. Drink some water. You look pale."

He did not drink water. He stood on the porch and stared at his arm like someone reunited with a future they'd given up on: the herbs he could collect, the stones he could climb, and the baskets he could carry for his mother.

The healer arrived in half a watch, summoned by Wei's mother, who had woken to find her son splintless. She had responded with the blend of terror and hope that parents reserve for situations that violate the rules they've structured their anxiety around. Healer Wen examined the arm. He pressed and probed. He asked Wei to rotate, extend, grip, push, and pull.

He looked at me.

I looked at the tea.

"This is not medically explainable," he said.

"He heals fast," I repeated.

The healer had the professional dignity not to argue with something he couldn't disprove and the human intelligence not to believe something he couldn't explain. He packed his tools, told Wei to be careful, and shot me one more look that contained approximately sixteen questions. He left without asking any of them.

Wei's mother stood in the doorway. Her eyes were wet. She didn't wipe them, letting them dry on their own as she let everything happen on its own, because intervention was a resource she'd depleted. She looked at Wei's arm, then at me. She opened her mouth, then closed it again. She nodded, the smallest motion, a dip of the chin.

She went inside to make rice.

The forest was quiet, but a different quiet than the dead silence of the past many days. The insects had begun to return. Their calls were scattered, with long gaps between them; a cricket chirped from a bush near the path. A single cricket, alone. We walked for several paces before it chirped again. Nothing answered it.

"Run," I said when we arrived at the edge of the village.

Wei ran. Badly, his arms pumping, his feet slapping the ground with flat-footed enthusiasm, as if he'd learned to run before he learned to walk efficiently and never updated the habit. He reached the forest, turned, and ran back. He was panting. The arm swung at his side, fully functional, as if it had never been introduced to the interior of a qi-mutated cat's paw.

"Now," I said. "Feel the ground before you touch it."

He blinked. "With what?"

"Your feet. The same way you hear the qi in the soil. The same sense. Route it down. Through your ankles, into your soles. Feel the root before you step on it."

He tried. The first three steps were indistinguishable from his normal running: heavy, loud, and fully committed to impact. On the fourth step, his right foot came down on a root that crossed the path at ankle height.

He tripped, caught himself, and swore.

Mrs. Liu had stopped on the path to watch. Her water bucket rested against her hip, forgotten.

"Again."

He ran again. He fell again. A different root.

Two children had appeared at the edge of the village, drawn by the sound of someone running, falling, and running again. They watched with the frank curiosity that adults train out of themselves.

"Again."

On the fourth attempt, something changed. His stride shortened. His weight shifted forward, balanced on the balls of his feet rather than his heels. His breathing settled into rhythm by the same instinct that had once helped him detect qi currents through a stone. For three steps, three smooth, quiet steps, he glided over the forest floor, clearing the roots with barely a fingerbreadth to spare.

Then he went over a stone and landed face-first in leaf litter.

"You curse better than you run," I observed.

He spat out a leaf. "*Thank you.* I've practiced that longer."

He stood up, brushing himself off. The gesture was so well worn, brushing off dirt, resetting, and trying again, that I almost recognized it. I almost saw someone else performing it, in a different forest, in a different century, with the same stubborn refusal to interpret falling as a reason to stop.

"Again?" he asked.

"Again."

He ran. He fell. He ran again, fell again, and ran again. On the ninth attempt, he cleared a stretch of thirty paces without stumbling, his feet finding the gaps between roots and stones with growing precision. Part qi-sensitivity, part the sheer bloody-minded determination that was, as far as I could determine, his primary talent.

He stopped at the end of the run, looking down at his feet as if they had done something remarkable without asking his permission. Then he turned to me.

I was already walking away.

"Was that good?" he called after me.

"It was adequate."

"That's the nicest thing you've ever said to me!"

I walked on. I did not smile.

Later, I was told my expression changed. I never confirmed.
