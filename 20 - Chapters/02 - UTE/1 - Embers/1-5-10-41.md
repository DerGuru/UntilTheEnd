The fire was smaller than usual, burning low between us while we picked through the wood Wei had brought. The drought had dried the forest floor until every branch snapped clean and the kindling crumbled to dust between your fingers. Finding wood that would burn slowly enough to last a conversation required going farther than it had a month ago, and neither of us had the energy for farther tonight.

Wei had come back with driftwood from the riverbank, pale and half-sanded, wood that burned too fast and smoked anyway. We fed it to the coals in small pieces and pretended it was a plan.

So: a small fire. Enough light to see each other's faces. Enough heat to acknowledge that warmth was desirable without fully providing it. The fire of two people rationing their resources and pretending they weren't.

Wei had dirt on his face. Specific dirt: the gray-brown clay from the irrigation channels he'd been digging since noon, mixed with the darker earth of the root vegetable beds and the chalky dust of the collapsed wall section he'd helped clear. It was a comprehensive dirt. A topographical map of his day, written on his skin in layers.

He sat across from me, cross-legged, hands on his knees, braced to say something he'd been carrying for a while.

The fire popped. A cricket gave one chirp, then stopped.

"What are you?"

He asked it to the fire, eyes fixed on the coals, voice quiet. The question dropped into the space between us, heavy and deliberate. Whatever came back would change the shape of everything. He waited.

So did I. I'd known this question was coming, just as I'd known the drought was coming. The trajectory was obvious: speed, healing, stillness, and knowledge. Wei was observant enough to collect it, intelligent enough to assemble it, and stubborn enough to stop pretending the assembly didn't form a picture.

The fire crackled. Night sounds came rarely, separated by long silences.

I could have given him a story. I gave him a word.

"Old," I said.

He didn't look up, and his hands didn't move. The word settled into the silence like another coal.

"That's not an answer."

"It is."

Pause. A log shifted in the fire, sending up a constellation of sparks that rose and vanished.

"How old?"

"Very."

"Older than the forest?"

"Yes."

He processed that. His eyes narrowed and his mouth compressed, a mind straining to fit information into categories too small for it. He was building a new one, right now, next to a small fire with dirt on his face. It was labeled with my name. It held everything he'd observed, plus the gap between what he'd observed and what he could explain.

"Older than the mountain?"

I paused, aiming for honesty. The mountain had been here for geological ages. I had not. Measuring myself against the permanent, against things that endured through sheer mass, was a habit from somewhere in my second millennium. I had never corrected it.

"Yes."

He swallowed, dry, with the canteen empty and the stream too far for a midnight trip. A twelve-year-old boy had just been told, by a woman he trusted more than he should, that she was older than the rock under him.

Then he did the maddening thing that made him Wei.

"No wonder you're in such a bad mood all the time."

Something in my chest jerked, a short, involuntary exhale. I held my mouth shut. The left corner twitched anyway, briefly enough that I could choose to disregard it. I did.

But Wei saw it. His eyes had moved from the fire to my face at exactly the wrong moment. His face warmed, a boy handed something small who knows better than to comment on it.

"Go to sleep," I said.

"Did you just—"

"Sleep."

"You did. You almost—"

"Wei."

"Fine." He stretched out by the fire, pulling his thin blanket over his shoulders. The blanket was too thin for a night that had cooled faster than the drought explained. The qi-drainage was pulling heat from the air along with everything else. He'd be cold by midnight.

I would not give him my outer robe. I'd already given it to him once, shrugging it off to drop it over his shoulders before I could think better of it. Once was a precedent I couldn't afford. Tonight, the fire would have to be enough.

"Yun?"

"What."

"Thanks for telling me."

He said it simply. He was twelve. He had asked a question and received an answer larger than his understanding. He could leave it there, cheek against his folded arm, while an adult would still be hunting for the proper words.

"I told you nothing."

"You told me you're old. That's something."

"It's a word."

"Still counts."

He closed his eyes. His breathing slowed into the third-step pattern, automatic now, woven into the rhythm of his sleep like a second heartbeat. Before the next piece of driftwood burned through, his face relaxed. His hands uncurled. The dirt on his cheek blurred in the firelight until he looked younger than twelve.

I sat by the fire. The fire was small and getting smaller. The night was large and getting larger. The horizon pulsed with the heartbeat of a young man who wanted to touch the sky and believed he deserved it.

Any morning now.

I fed the fire. One piece of wood, rationed, measured, the minimum required to keep the heat alive until dawn.

The fire would last.

It had to.
