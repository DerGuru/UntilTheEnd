The forest smelled like rain that hadn't fallen yet. I walked. That was what I did: walked through forests, across mountains, along rivers that had changed their courses three or four times since I'd first seen them. This one was old, for a forest. The trees were thick enough that two men couldn't have wrapped their arms around most of them. Under that dense canopy, morning came late and left early. Moss draped the branches in soft curtains. Ferns unfurled from rotting stumps. The air was heavy, green, and wet.

It was beautiful. I knew that as a fact I'd repeated too many times to feel much while saying it. Looking back, I remember the cool moss underfoot more clearly than that certainty. Some part of the morning still reached me. I was stubborn about admitting it.

Another day arrived.

I stepped over a fallen trunk without looking down. I judged the ground by the slope under my feet. A thousand forests, ten thousand: they all had the same bones underneath. Roots, rocks, and the slow decay of things that used to stand. The qi in the soil was dim and sluggish, moving well below the depth at which most travelers would notice it.

Birds called from somewhere above. A warbler, two sparrows chasing each other off the same branch, and something larger rustling through the upper leaves. There were plenty of other branches. The sparrows returned to that one. Insects hummed in the spaces between the trees, that low drone that filled the silence without breaking it. The sound of a forest that was alive, unremarkably, persistently alive.

I'd heard these sounds when the trees were saplings. Before that, when the land was scrub. I let the memory stop there. On mornings like this, I held my attention on the light coming through the leaves in patterns I'd memorized ages ago. It gave me something to look at.

A branch caught at my sleeve. I pulled free without looking; the fabric tore with a small, dry sound. The robe had survived enough branches for this one to finish what the others had begun. I walked on. The torn thread dangled against my wrist and I tucked it into the seam from habit. An unnecessary repair, but it gave my hands something to do.

My feet were bare. The moss was cool and damp. Dew clung to the fern tips; occasionally one brushed my ankle, leaving a cold streak on my skin. I almost enjoyed it. Almost was a comfortable word, one that committed me to very little. The temperature of water on skin, the texture of moss between toes. Small things. I still registered small things. I just didn't know what to do with them anymore.

I passed an empty spirit beast den, recently abandoned. The scratches on the entrance log were fresh, the dirt still damp where something large had dragged itself out. Low-level. Tier one, maybe two. The kind of beast that would terrify a village and bore a proper cultivator. I noted its position as I would a crack in the road, adjusting my next step without stopping to examine it.

At the base of the den lay a clutch of crushed eggshells, white against the dark soil. They were paper-thin, already half-buried. Something had hatched here and left. I stepped past. The shells crunched under my heel.

The air shifted, suddenly warmer against my skin. A cloud must have moved. Sunlight broke through the canopy in a column of gold, illuminating dust and pollen above the ground. The motes drifted through the light at different heights, visible until they crossed back into shade.

I stopped without meaning to.

The light was warm on my face. I closed my eyes.

I let it sit there. The warmth. The quiet. The smell of moss, wet wood, and the faint sweetness of wild jasmine. I could have named every flowering thing in this forest if I'd cared to. Some part of me still did, the part that had once kept a garden. I pushed that down. It went easily. It always did.

Then I opened my eyes and walked on.

The morning light caught on a spiderweb strung between two branches, each droplet of dew refracting a tiny part of the forest. I'd seen something like it before, once, on a mountain that no longer existed. The web had been bigger then. Or maybe I was remembering it wrong. Memory did that after a few thousand years. The details softened and the edges blurred until I could recall how a morning felt more clearly than what had happened in it. You'd think the forgetting gets easier. It doesn't. It just gets quieter.

A mushroom cluster grew from the base of a rotting oak: pale, fat caps in a neat row, their undersides dark with spores. Edible. Good, even. The kind a village herbalist would trade a day's work for. I lingered over them three steps longer than I needed to, then turned away. How long had it been since I ate? A month? Three? The body didn't need it. It hadn't needed it for so long that the act of eating felt like a performance. I could eat. I remembered how. I remembered enjoying it, distantly, like a story somebody had told me long ago.

My qi was still. Perfectly masked. That took no effort; it hadn't for millennia. As automatic as breathing or blinking. An ocean's worth concealed in a puddle. Anything in this forest with the sense to feel for energy signatures would register me as nothing. A mortal woman walking through the trees, unremarkable and forgettable.

That was the point.

I followed the gaps between the trees that were wide enough to walk through without turning sideways. They made a sufficient path. The forest floor dropped, following the slope of a hill I'd watched erode over the centuries. At the bottom ran a thin, clear stream, gurgling over stones that had been sharp once and were smooth now. A kingfisher sat on a stone in the middle of the current, its feathers absurdly blue against the gray water. It watched me step across. I stared back. Neither of us blinked. I liked kingfishers. That was one of the small admissions I still allowed myself: they waited patiently, struck precisely, and asked nothing of me.

The kingfisher dove. The splash was barely a sound. When it surfaced, a minnow flashed silver in its beak.

Something caught at the edge of my awareness.

A qi signature, low, animal, and pulsing with hunger. Spirit beast. Close. Maybe three hundred paces north, moving south through the undergrowth. Its energy was ragged, agitated. Hunting, or fleeing from something that hunted it. It was coarse as rough-cut stone, raw power compressed into muscle, bone, and instinct. A cultivator would have refined it. This creature carried it as it had grown.

I noted it and walked on. Spirit beasts were common enough in forests like this. The qi concentration in the soil attracted them. They fed, grew, and sometimes evolved. Sometimes they wandered into the lowlands and destroyed a village. Sometimes a cultivator came and destroyed them first. The cycle was old and unremarkable. I'd seen it turn a thousand times.

Then came the second signal.

Small, human, faint: someone who had never cultivated a day in their life, only the dim, natural glow of a living body. A child, probably, moving through the forest maybe a hundred and fifty paces east of my position, perpendicular to the beast's trajectory.

I stopped.

The beast was heading south. The human was heading west. Their paths would intersect in sixty heartbeats. I made the calculation without thinking about it, as I calculated most things by then. Maybe seventy, if the child was slow. Children usually were.

I stood very still and listened.

Later, I'd tell myself that the pause meant nothing. I was good at telling myself things.

The kingfisher called from the stream behind me. Two sharp notes, rising at the end. I could have heard a question in them if I'd been inclined to answer one.

Something in the air felt a fraction out of place.

The way I always did.
