The sparrows stopped singing while I sat by the river. Then the finches fell quiet. By the time the thrush near the dead oak one li north stopped too, I was paying attention. I had listened to forests for four thousand years. Those songs were as worn into me as the ground under my feet, each generation of birds making small changes to sounds I remembered from the last.

By mid-morning, the forest was silent.

The quiet went past the calls I knew. Nothing moved in the nearby canopy, and the rustling that usually filled the gaps between songs was gone. It was the silence of a place the animals had fled, and the river sounded unnaturally clear in it.

Wei noticed.

He sat on his stone with his eyes closed, practicing the breathing exercise I'd taught him seven days earlier. His posture had improved: a straighter back, centered weight, and hands resting easily on his knees. He was supposed to be listening to the water, separating it from the forest's background noise until he could pick out each sound.

His eyes opened.

"It is too quiet."

Four words, spoken with a care that made me turn to him. He had tilted his head to listen at the angle we'd practiced until it became habit. He was hearing the absence too.

I'd spent half a watch on the bank staring at fog and thinking about nothing, the only meditation that still worked after I had exhausted every other kind. You'd think four thousand years would produce better hobbies. You'd be wrong. The fog moved slowly over water that smelled of mud and cold stone. The air was colder than it had been seven days earlier, cold enough to settle into fingers and make breath visible at dawn. Wei's frost, right on schedule. Under it lay a metallic tang, the kind that arrived when qi shifted underground.

That metallic edge. That was the important part.

"Where are the birds?" Wei stood slowly, eyes on the trees, as if a sudden motion might draw something toward him. He checked the canopy, the underbrush, and the water. Each was still. His shoulders were tight the whole time.

A change in pressure reached me through the ground from dozens of li away. It traveled along the earth's qi currents as distinctly as a disturbance travels through water. Somebody was drawing a great deal of energy through their meridians. The surrounding soil lost warmth with each pull, its qi thinning as the distant concentration grew.

I knew what it was. I had seen it a hundred times across a hundred centuries: a cultivator hunting spirit beast cores for a breakthrough. They absorbed, consumed, and climbed, concentrating on the next stage until everything beneath it became easy to overlook. Ascension, from where they stood. Catastrophe, for the people left below.

I had stopped intervening before the first human kingdom fell. I had the power. I had also learned what happened when I used it.

Trees would weaken as the soil lost its qi. Smaller plants would follow, then the water sources would change. The animals were already fleeing what people here could scarcely sense. They had no need of a theory before leaving a place that could no longer sustain them. That was the quiet Wei was hearing.

"What's wrong with the forest?" he asked.

I stood and brushed my hands down my sides, a habit I had outlived the need for. The fog drifted above the river's murmur. Under both, the metallic edge sharpened.

"Something's hunting," I said.

"Spirit beast?"

"They're what's being hunted."

His face lost a shade of color.

He waited for elaboration. I gave him none. He was learning to tell when I had reached the end of what I intended to say, which usually left him with a great deal more he wanted to ask.

"Should we—"

"Collect your herbs closer to the village."

He opened his mouth, closed it, and glanced back at the emptied forest. Then he turned toward the path to the village, where smoke still rose above the houses. There would be people there, making ordinary noise. For now, that was enough.

"Okay," he said.

He picked up his basket, paused, and turned back.

"You're not coming?"

"No."

He waited a beat. Two. Then he walked. His footsteps were as loud as ever through the undergrowth, but his pace was steady. He had heard the forest fall silent. He went through it anyway, carrying his basket toward the people who needed what he gathered.

I stood by the river and listened to the silence. Far northwest, deep in the old-growth forest where the trees predated human memory, the pressure pulsed again. Another pull followed it, stronger than the last. The intervals were regular.

Fourteen days. Perhaps less.

The animals were right to run.
