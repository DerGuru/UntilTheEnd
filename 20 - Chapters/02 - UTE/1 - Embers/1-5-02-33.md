Wei and I were training at the river, or doing what passed for training when the water was half its usual depth. The current ran sluggish and brown. Rocks along the bank were dry-cracked and warm to the touch. During winter. Xu Ran was closer than I'd calculated.

I'd moved the exercises here because the flow added instability, which added difficulty, which added competence. Walk on solid ground and your body learns balance. Walk on shifting ground and your body learns adaptation. The difference matters when the ground stops being reliable.

Wei was ankle-deep in the current, working through the next step pattern. He inhaled, stepped, exhaled, and stepped again. His right foot was still sloppy. He overcompensated on the transition, loading weight before his qi had settled into the new position. I'd corrected it seven times already today. He'd fixed it each time and then reverted within thirty heartbeats. Either stubbornness or muscle memory resisting a new habit. In Wei's case, probably both.

"Right foot."

"I know."

"Then fix it."

He adjusted. Better. His ankle still wobbled. He made it three steps before the current caught him and he sat down hard in the water, sending up a splash that reached the bank. He stood up, shook water from his hair, and started over.

Then I felt it.

For many days, the background pulse had hummed in the qi around us like a bass note played too quietly to identify but too persistently to ignore. Now a sharper presence cut through it. Closer. A presence with edges, with direction, with the density of a living cultivator. He was easy to place.

Three hundred paces. Forest. East.

I turned my head slowly, a woman checking the weather, and let my gaze travel along the bank before it reached the trees.

He stood at the tree line.

Xu Ran.

Seventeen, maybe eighteen. Thin as young cultivators get when they burn through food faster than they can replace it. Every bit of spare flesh had been stripped away by a body channeling more qi than it could safely hold. His hair was long, unwashed, and tangled enough to suggest he hadn't thought about it in many days. His robes, once fine, probably, were torn at the sleeves and stained with something dark. Qi-saturated fabric. The threads themselves hummed faintly with residual energy he had shed into them.

In his right hand: a spirit beast core. Fresh. Still pulsing with a faint blue light, the last heartbeat of whatever creature it had belonged to fading in irregular bursts. He held it as a farmer holds an apple, with his fingers loosely curved around it. Useful, ordinary, and already categorized as fuel rather than trophy.

His face.

I studied it. He looked occupied, already attending to something beyond the core in his hand. That was the thing the villagers wouldn't understand, the thing I needed Wei to eventually learn. They would look for cruelty. They would need a villain to explain the suffering, someone corrupted by power or twisted by some dark force. Xu Ran was hunting because the breakthrough demanded resources and spirit beast cores were resources. The math was simple and the conclusion was obvious. Morality had nothing to do with his equation.

I'd worn that face. Millennia ago. Before I'd seen what the equations produced when you followed them long enough.

He looked in our direction. Through us. Two figures in a river, a woman and a boy, peasants, as unremarkable to him as the river. Present, acknowledged, and irrelevant. His eyes moved on. He turned and walked back into the forest. The trees hid him from view, and the pressure receded as he moved farther away.

The whole encounter lasted not even ten heartbeats.

Wei had stopped practicing. He stood in the water, dripping, staring at the place where the figure had been.

"Who was that?"

"Nobody you want to know."

"That wasn't nobody. I felt him. Like—" He pressed a fist against his chest. "Here. Like something heavy."

He'd felt the qi-pressure. Second-step sensitivity, working as intended. He couldn't identify the level or the nature or the intent; he was years from that kind of nuance. But he'd registered the weight of it, the physical reality of a cultivator operating at a tier so far above him that the gap between them was a canyon.

"Was that him? The one causing the earthquakes?"

"Yes."

"He's young."

"Yes."

Wei turned to me. His face was doing the thing it did when he was incorporating new information that didn't fit his existing model: a narrowing of the eyes, a compression of the mouth, and the visible effort of a mind rebuilding itself around an unwelcome truth.

"You could stop him."

I didn't answer. The river murmured between us, noise that served as punctuation rather than content.

"Couldn't you?"

"Right foot," I said. "Fix the transition."

He held my gaze for three heartbeats. Then he turned, faced upstream, and resumed the pattern. He inhaled. He stepped. He exhaled. He stepped again. His right foot was better this time.

He didn't ask again that afternoon.
