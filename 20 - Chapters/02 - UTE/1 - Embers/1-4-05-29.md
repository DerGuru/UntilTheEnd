The run was going well, which was unusual enough to be suspicious. Wei moved through the forest with a fluidity that had not been there seven days ago. His feet found the gaps between roots, the stable ground between stones, and the path of least resistance through the terrain, available to anyone willing to listen for it. He listened. His breathing was synchronized, an inhale on the third step, an exhale on the sixth. The rhythm carried him forward with a momentum that wasn't quite grace but was no longer its opposite.

He cleared a log by going around it, feet shifting without stopping, weight transferring from one side to the other. He pivoted as the qi-sensitivity in his soles registered the solid wood ahead. By the time he looked down, he was past it, his stride unbroken.

Three steps. Five. Ten without falling.

He stopped at the far end of the clearing, then turned. He looked down at his feet with the baffled pride of a man who'd taught his dog a trick and was now unsure which of them had learned something.

He looked at me.

He grinned.

"Thank you, Shifu."

The grin died.

It died because the word landed between us with a weight that its two syllables had no right to carry. The clearing had been pleasantly warm and acceptably alive with insect noise. Now the noise was gone.

"Don't call me that."

My voice was flat. Anger implied investment. This was older than anger, the smooth, featureless terrain left behind when every version of this conversation has already been had.

"What? Shifu?" He cocked his head. A little of the grin returned, faltering at one corner of his mouth. "That's what you are, though. You teach me. You—"

"I teach you nothing. You stand there and I talk. That is not instruction."

"You fixed my arm."

"Your arm healed."

"You showed me the herbs."

"I corrected your mistakes."

"You showed me the qi-steps."

"I prevented you from injuring yourself."

He counted on his fingers. The fingers of the arm I'd healed. "Teaching, healing, and preventing injury. You're right. That's completely different from being a Shifu."

I looked at the tree line. I might as well have asked the trees for support. They stood in their usual positions, being wooden, contributing nothing.

"I am not your master."

He stopped smiling. What replaced the humor on his face was something older than twelve. The expression of a boy who had learned to read people as he'd learned to read the forest, because reading things correctly was the difference between eating and starving.

"What are you, then?"

The question hung in the clearing. It expanded, touching the edges of the conversation, then the edges of myself, then edges I hadn't known existed, because I'd spent thousands of years believing I had none.

I looked at the forest. At the trees that had been here for centuries and would be here for centuries more, if the boy on the distant hilltop didn't burn them out of existence. Then up, at the blue sky. I was running out of places to look.

"Someone who is here."

The words arrived without planning. Thousands of years of accumulated wisdom, all that careful weighing of language, had brought me to this. I scraped away the refusal, the deflection, and the excuses I'd used to keep out anyone who had ever needed something from me. This was what was left.

Someone who is here. I could manage that much. A title would have carried obligations I could recite from memory; presence required me to occupy the same location as this boy, at this moment, in this forest. I promised proximity. I had already broken my own rules by healing an arm I should have left shattered. Apparently I was still negotiating how much that meant.

Wei considered this. His face moved through several expressions as he processed, weighed, and filed the answer in a mind whose logic I couldn't predict. Then he nodded.

"Okay."

He walked back to the starting position, stretching his arms.

"Again?"

I nodded.

"Now," I said. "Breathe on the third step. Inhale. Always on the third."

"I already do that."

"You do it when you remember. I want you to do it when you don't."

He ran, fell, and got up.

"Again."

He ran. He didn't fall. For twelve steps, he moved through the forest with the qi-sensitivity I'd built into him, breathing and stepping in rhythm. Half-learned, half-something he carried in his bones, as some people carry music.

He stopped, then turned.

"Is this something I'll need to be able to do alone?"

The question was casual, thrown over his shoulder without ceremony. He was already stretching one leg, unaware of what he'd asked. The question held something larger than the exercise, something shaped like the future: an assumption that I might not always be standing at the edge of the clearing while he ran.

He had to learn to do this alone.

The thought passed through me like a blade through water, without resistance, without friction, without leaving a wound I could point to. A rational assessment of a practical reality.

He was mortal. I was not. At some point, these two facts would produce a conclusion that no amount of training could prevent.

"Yes," I said.

He nodded. He didn't ask why. He ran again.

He ran. He fell, then rose. He fell, then rose again, a creature learning to move through a world fundamentally hostile to its existence. He had to learn to do this alone, I thought. The thought was correct. The thought was cold. I filed it where I wouldn't have to look at it again.

It would find me later. It always did.
