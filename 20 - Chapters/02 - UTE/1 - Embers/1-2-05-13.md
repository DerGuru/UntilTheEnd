Wei held a mushroom up in the afternoon light. It was a Caltharia mimic, with a cap the right color, the right size, and the same habitat as Mingwort under ash trees where the soil was damp. From two paces away, most people would have mistaken it for the real thing. A boy who had learned botany by trial and error, supplemented by a village elder whose expertise peaked at "don't eat the red ones," had little chance.

He held it high, as proudly as a hunter returning with a kill. This particular hunter was twelve years old, with dirt under his fingernails and a grin out of proportion to the fungus in his hand.

"This one," he said, "is for the fever. I remember. You said the ones near ash trees are the good ones."

I had never said that. I had paused by an ash to examine Mingwort growing at its base. He had interpreted the pause as instruction, which I supposed it was. Unintentional instruction was still instruction, just as an unintentional fire was still a fire.

"That's poison."

His hand stopped turning the mushroom. In the quiet after that word, the water at the clearing's edge was the only sound.

I should tell you that I said it calmly. That would be true. I used the tone I'd have used to mention rain. Calm can mean that the speaker has forgotten how much a word matters to somebody else.

Wei's grin froze in place while he stared at me. His hand hung raised, the mushroom between thumb and forefinger.

"What?"

"Caltharia. Not Mingwort. The resemblance is strong. Same color, same growth pattern, and the same soil. The difference is the margin." I pointed from where I sat, moving only my finger. "Smooth. Mingwort is serrated."

He turned the mushroom to examine the edge. It was smooth, a gentle curve where serrations should have been. He followed the whole rim with his eyes.

"Your father would have stopped coughing within a watch and a half."

Pause.

"Because he would have stopped breathing."

The mushroom descended slowly, his eyes on me. He placed it delicately on the rock, withdrawing his fingers as soon as the cap rested there.

He turned to the rest of his collection, arranged across the flat stone in piles he'd spent half a watch building. He went over each specimen, finding reasons for caution in things he'd brought here so proudly. His hands didn't go near them.

"Have I—" His voice was thin. "Has my father already—"

"No. This variety grows only in the deep forest, past the third ridge. The specimens near the village are the harmless variant. Different soil acidity." I paused. "Harmless and useless are not the same thing, but in your case, they have been the same thing."

He breathed out. He had been holding the breath through my answer, and his shoulders lowered with it. He glanced once more at the harmless plants, then at the mushroom on the rock.

"Show me the difference."

The request was bare and direct. He had no attention to spare for politeness. He needed to know what he could take home without making his father's illness worse.

I stood up.

All my usual objections were still available: indifference, detachment, and several thousand years of reasons to keep both. None had much force while he waited next to something he'd nearly taken home to his father. I could at least show him an edge. A small concession.

I walked to the stone, picked up the Caltharia, and turned it over.

"Margin. Smooth means death. Serrated means life. It's that simple."

I set it down and picked up a genuine Mingwort from his collection, the one correct specimen among twenty wrong ones. I suspected he'd chosen it by accident.

"This is Mingwort. The real one. Feel the edge."

He touched it, running his finger along the cap's rim. His brow furrowed.

"Okay. That's—" He touched the rim again. "Yeah. Bumpy."

"Serrated."

"Serrated. Right."

I picked up another specimen. "This is Whitestem. You've been collecting it for fourteen days. It does nothing. It's a garnish. Your father has been drinking garnish tea."

"He said it tasted better than the last batch."

"It would. It tastes like very mild lettuce."

I set the Whitestem down and moved to the next. "Bitter Root. Medicinal, but not for lungs. For stomach parasites. Unless your father has stomach parasites, this is irrelevant."

"Does he have stomach parasites?"

"I don't know. Does he?"

"I don't think so?"

"Then this is irrelevant."

The corrections went on for half a watch. I insisted on thinking of them as corrections; a lesson would have implied a different sort of arrangement. I stood at the rock, dividing his collection with the systematic disdain of a librarian given a box of dubious books. Useful, useless, dangerous, and how did you even find this.

He questioned everything until he understood it. He compared one specimen with the next, rearranging the piles whenever I explained another difference. Once the plants were sorted to his satisfaction, he began asking about those he'd left behind.

"What about that purple one? The one with the spotted cap?"

"Decorative. You could dry it and sell it at the market. You cannot use it for medicine."

"And the long one? The root?"

"That's a root."

"I know it's a root. What kind of root?"

"The kind you pull out of the ground and bring to me as if it means something."

He grinned again, smaller this time, with a sharpness that hadn't been there when he held up the mushroom. I could be as discouraging as I liked; he was still leaving with something he hadn't known before.

The sun moved and the shadows rotated. The pile of discarded specimens grew; the useful ones were five or six out of thirty. He asked, I corrected him, he asked again. The pattern settled in with no regard for what I called it.

I was not teaching him. I was preventing him from killing someone through ignorance. There was a difference, vast enough that I could articulate it clearly and defend it logically. I did not believe it for a single moment.

"Same time tomorrow?" he asked, when the light had gone orange and the shadows had merged into a single, undifferentiated dusk.

I said nothing. I walked away.

He took this as yes.

He was not wrong.
