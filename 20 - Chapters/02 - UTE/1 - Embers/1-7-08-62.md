The qi reached the southern slope as a breeze. I wasn't there for what happened next. I was at the river, picking up stones. What I know, I know because they told me. Wei in fragments. Mrs. Liu, in her steady voice. Elder Li, with the care old men take describing things they wish they hadn't seen.

For the villagers, huddled behind the ridge with their bundles, their fear, and their careful, practiced patience, it was a warm gust. Nothing more. A puff of air smelling of ozone, tasting faintly of metal, passing through their bodies like wind through clothes: felt, noted, and forgotten. Mrs. Liu adjusted her collar. Elder Li blinked. Xiao Mei looked up from the game she'd been playing with stones in the dirt and said, "That smells funny." She went back to her stones.

For Wei, it was an ocean.

His body was different. Three months of training had opened channels that normal bodies never used, qi-pathways running along his meridians, carved through breath, discipline, and the third-step pattern that had become his heartbeat's echo. Second-step perception. Third-step qi-steps. A circulatory network carefully and conservatively expanded by a teacher who understood that opening a door was only safe when you controlled what came through it.

I had not controlled this.

The dispersed qi, harmonized, gentle, and distributed evenly across the upper atmosphere by a thought that had been intended to minimize harm, was harmless at its ambient concentration. For a normal body, it was nothing.

For a twelve-year-old boy with open qi-channels and sensitivity trained to register ambient energy as an ear registers sound, it was everything.

His channels caught the qi. Automatically. The pathways I had opened weren't selective; they absorbed, processed, and circulated ambient energy, because that was what qi-channels did. They had no means of cutting off the flow or filtering it. They opened, and the environment flowed in.

The environment was flowing in at a rate his body was not designed to handle.

He felt it first as warmth. A spreading heat that started in his chest, where the primary meridian ran, and moved outward along the channels. Down his arms, up his neck, into his fingertips, his toes, and the base of his skull where perception lived.

"I feel—" He looked at his hands and turned them over. "Something's happening."

"Wei?" Elder Li reached for him. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong. It's warm. It's—" He stopped. His eyes widened.

Then the warmth became pressure. The channels were full. The qi poured in; the ambient concentration wasn't decreasing, because the dispersal was still settling, the energy still spreading through the volume of atmosphere it had been distributed into. The channels absorbed, and the pressure built as pressure builds in any sealed container: steadily, mechanically, without any means by which Wei could say *stop*.

No one had taught him how to say stop because no one had expected him to need to.

His pupils dilated. The qi flooding his channels reached his optical meridians and spread into his sight. For one brilliant, overwhelming, impossible moment, he saw.

Qi-streams in the air. Rivers of energy flowing above the ground like transparent currents, each one a different density, a different color, and a different texture. The air was full of them. It had always been full of them. He was seeing them for the first time, all at once, without the gradual acclimatization of a sight that had developed with him.

Qi in the ground. Flowing through channels under the soil, following the paths that I had redirected, running south along the trenches I'd dug, and pooling at the nodes I'd modified. The earth was alive with it. Luminous. A map drawn in light, under the surface, visible through the thin skin of dirt, grass, and clay.

Qi in the people around him. Each person a network, dim, barely flickering, the minimal circulation of unawakened meridians operating at a level too low to register except to eyes that were suddenly, catastrophically open. Mrs. Liu: a steady, warm glow. Elder Li: dimmer, concentrated around his joints and his heart. The children: bright, scattered, and chaotic, the undirected energy of bodies still growing.

He saw everything. He held it all: the sky, the ground, the people, the village, the valley, and the distant, fading signature of something massive moving northeast, away from them. Then the river. Where the light stopped. Where his new perception reached and found nothing his eyes were built to see. A hole in the world made of light. A shape where a person should have been. Where I was, invisible to the sight I'd given him.

"It's—" His voice cracked. "I can see it. I can see everything. The light, it's everywhere, it's—"

"Wei!" His mother was on her feet. "Wei, look at me."

He looked at her. Through her. Past the skin, the worry, and the worn hands into the dim, warm glow that was her life. Her qi. The quiet fire that kept her moving through days that gave her nothing and took everything.

"Mama," he said. "You're beautiful."

Then the pressure exceeded capacity. His channels, full, overfull, and straining, hit their structural limit. They closed to keep from breaking. His body, overwhelmed, shut down. The perception collapsed. The vision of a world made of light folded inward and went dark.

Wei fell.

His eyes rolled. His body went limp. He dropped where he stood, too suddenly to stagger or reach for anything. His limbs were loose, his face blank, his breathing fast and shallow, the breathing of a body processing an event it hadn't been built for.

His mother screamed.

The sound cut the air, the only sharp sound in a landscape that had been nearly silent all morning. She lunged for him.

"Don't move him!" Elder Li was already there, the old man's reflexes less an act of speed than of position, his arm under Wei's head before it hit the stone, his body bracing to absorb the boy's weight.

"What happened to him?" Chun knelt by them. "What's wrong with his skin?"

"He's burning," his mother said. Her hands were on his face, his arms, checking as mothers check, with touch, fear, and the urgent grammar of hands that need to be doing something.

"He's not burning." Mrs. Liu's voice came from behind. Calm. Steady. "But he needs her." She looked east. Toward the tree line. Toward the place where I had gone.

Wei's skin was warm. Qi-warm, distinct from fever. Along the lines of the channels I had opened: light. Faint. Threads of luminescence running under his skin like capillaries filled with light, pulsing weakly, irregularly, the residual glow of channels that had been flooded and shut down but hadn't fully discharged their load.

His mother held his face. His name was on her lips. "Wei, Wei, Wei." She repeated it as names are repeated when the person they belong to has left the room and the only thing that might bring them back is the sound of what they're called.

"Someone find her," Elder Li said. His voice had the crack of authority spent to its limit. "Find the woman."

"She went east," Mrs. Liu said. "Into the trees."
