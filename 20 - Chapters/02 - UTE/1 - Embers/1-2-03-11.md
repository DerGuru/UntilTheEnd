Late-afternoon light lay golden across the clearing where he sorted his herbs. He would soon ask a question I should have seen coming. I had been busy not paying attention to him, which was becoming difficult on account of his refusal to be ignorable.

Sun reached sideways below the canopy line, lighting dust above the ground with a richness a painter might have ruined by trying to capture it. We sat near the flat rock, with deer-cropped grass around us and the stream murmuring along the clearing's southern edge. He had spent half a watch separating his latest collection into piles. The piles had improved. Twenty-one days ago, every one was wrong. Now one in four was right. Before placing each specimen, he'd begun glancing at me for small changes in my expression that I didn't know I was giving.

"Foxglove?" He held up a stem with purple flowers.

I glanced at it, then away.

He set it in the discard pile. "Foxglove," he confirmed to himself, like a man crossing an item off a list.

The clearing was quiet. We had spent enough time together that a pause could be just a pause, without him supplying the next question. He sorted while I sat nearby. Insects moved above a patch of clover.

He stopped sorting.

His hands stopped moving, and his hands always moved. When they weren't sorting herbs, they were stripping reeds, or stacking stones, or braiding grass into shapes that didn't resemble anything but occupied his fingers. Stillness, for him, was an event.

He looked straight at me with the unguarded focus of a child who hasn't yet learned how uncomfortable that makes adults. He let the plant in his hand rest on the rock.

"What's your name?"

He'd asked before, during that first frantic morning when one question followed another so quickly I could usually let them pass. I'd said no. He'd moved on. Now he waited, hands flat on the rock, giving the question more weight than I wanted it to have.

I said nothing. I had guarded my names for so long that offering one required thought. He was asking for a word. I sat there as though he had asked me to account for everything that had once belonged to it.

The stream murmured. A leaf detached from a maple overhead and spiraled slowly down through still air. It landed on the flat rock between his two piles. He left it there, his attention on me.

He started to shift. His jaw tightened, and his shoulders drew inward. He was about to take the question back, apologize, or fill the silence with something less dangerous.

I looked up.

A single cloud, white and shapeless, drifted through an otherwise empty sky. It crossed the gap between the trees slowly, its edges changing.

"Yun."

One syllable. I offered only that, as simply as handing a stone to a child: here, hold this, it doesn't mean anything. Except stones could mean everything when you were twelve and someone trusted you with one.

The word hung above the stream's murmur. In the quiet before he answered, it was a sound I had allowed someone else to keep.

"Yun," he repeated, testing the sound with the same care he gave an unfamiliar herb. He said it slowly enough to hear both the beginning and the end.

He nodded. Once.

"I'm Wei."

I knew that. The village knew that. Even the goat had had plenty of chances to hear it. His mother's voice came back to me, sharp with exasperation as it carried across the village square. Wei. I'd heard it twenty-one days earlier, standing at the tree line. Here, with the afternoon warm on the sorting stone, he offered it as an exchange: name for name, weight for weight.

"In case you were wondering," he added.

"I wasn't."

He grinned. It lingered at the edge of my vision after I turned back to the herbs. His face had relaxed so completely that looking away did little to spare me. The clearing was warmer, though the sun had barely moved.

He went back to sorting herbs. Foxglove in the discard pile. Silverleaf in the keep pile. His hands moved with their usual restless energy, but his shoulders had settled. A question he had carried since our first day finally had its answer.

I sat with the sound of my name in a stranger's mouth. I had been free to keep it to myself. I had been equally free to give it to him. That was the problem.

The cloud drifted on. By the time I looked up again, it was gone.
