I was at the eastern barrier wall when his mother came to me. I'd rebuilt it twice and reinforced it three times. I was now checking it for the fourth time because the tremors were increasing in frequency and each one tested the joints in ways that previous tremors hadn't. The stones held. They would hold through the tribulation's secondary effects. They would not hold through the primary shockwave, but the primary shockwave would be addressed differently. That plan lived in a part of my mind I hadn't shared with anyone.

She appeared at the bottom of the slope. Alone. No Wei, no other villagers, and no pretense of being in the area for any reason other than the one that had brought her. She climbed the slope carefully, thin from the long burden of illness in the house, conserving energy for an expenditure she couldn't yet identify. Wei's father had died a month ago. The herbs had made it easier on him, but as predicted, they hadn't cured him. That hadn't made it easier on them. His mother had been left to care for a son already too old to be a child and too young to be an adult. The stress of that responsibility had started showing in her face and her movements long before the drought made it impossible to ignore.

She reached me and didn't sit; this wasn't a sitting conversation. Her hands were at her sides, cracked at the knuckles. Old cracks. Untreated.

We looked at each other. Her eyes asked for an answer.

"He doesn't listen to me," she said.

No preamble, no hedging. She had spent her entire life being indirect and had, these last days, lost interest in the practice.

"He listens," I said.

"He doesn't stop."

She was right. He didn't stop. The water runs, the rationing, the patrols, and the repairs. She'd asked him to rest; he'd nodded, then went on working. She'd asked again and he'd said "after." After never came because there was always something after after.

"He listens to you," she said.

This was also true. He listened as students listen to teachers they've chosen rather than been assigned, with an attentiveness that couldn't be commanded because it had never been extracted by command. I'd earned it, stone by stone, lesson by lesson, in the currency of accuracy, consistency, and a refusal to waste his time with comfort when he needed competence.

"He's twelve," she said.

The words landed differently than she intended. She meant a reminder: *he's a child, he shouldn't be doing this, and someone needs to tell him to stop.* What I heard was a statement of fact so obvious and so painful that repeating it was the only way to acknowledge it.

He was twelve. He was carrying water, rationing food, and patrolling the perimeter of a village threatened by a force he couldn't see or understand. He was twelve. His hands shook; he gave away part of his already rationed food. He was twelve.

"Please, look after my son."

Request, demand, and prayer converged in the words. It was a transfer more than a sentence: a mother placing her son's safety into hands she didn't understand because her own weren't enough.

The wind blew. Warm. Dry. Carrying dust.

Cracked hands. Eyes that had watched a husband die and a son grow up too fast. She'd walked up a slope to find a woman she barely knew, a woman who lived at the forest's edge and whose qualification was nothing more than the observation that her son, her stubborn impossible self-destructing son, listened when this woman spoke.

"He should eat more," I said.

I had avoided a promise. Four words about food where she'd needed reassurance, a categorical statement of protection.

But she nodded, slowly, like someone who had heard more than was said.

She turned and walked down the slope, her footsteps careful on the loose stone. The light was failing. The valley was gold, then orange, then gray. The horizon pulsed.

I turned back to the wall. I checked the stones and adjusted one that had shifted a fingerbreadth in the last tremor. The adjustment wasn't really necessary, but I did it anyway.

The boy's mother reached the village and disappeared among its warm lights. She went to Wei, who rested by the fire. I stood on the hillside with my raw hands, my ancient bones, and the weight of four words that did the work of the promise I'd avoided making.

He should eat more.

Yes. He should.
