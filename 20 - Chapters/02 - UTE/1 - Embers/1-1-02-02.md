The boy was on his knees in the dirt, digging. I stood behind an oak, wherever I happened to be. The trunk was wide enough to hide me without effort. Thirty paces between us, give or take. He had his side to me, shoulders hunched, fingers working at something in the soil. A basket sat next to him, half-full of roots, mushrooms, and the kind of leafy bundles that village herbalists wrapped their guesses in.

He was small. Twelve, maybe, though hunger and hard work could shave years off how a child looked. Thin arms, knobby wrists, and clothes mended so many times the patches had patches. His hair was tied back with a strip of cloth that might once have been a sleeve. His bare feet were callused. Mine were bare by choice; his clothes suggested he couldn't afford much choice about anything.

The mushrooms in his basket were wrong.

The caps were pale with a faint blush of pink along the edge. Blushing Cap lookalikes. The real ones had white gills; these had cream-colored gills with a yellow tinge at the base. Close enough to fool a child. Close enough to kill a man with a weak liver.

I noted it and said nothing. I could have said something. In retrospect, with the bark still clear against my sleeve, I can think of enough things I could have done differently that morning to fill a book. You'd know. You're the one writing them down.

The boy looked up as if frightened, then relaxed. He pried a root from the ground, squinted at it, and dropped it into the basket. His concentration was absolute and unself-conscious, a child with something important to do.

"This one's good," he muttered. He turned the next root in the light filtering through the canopy, squinting at it like a jeweler with a questionable gem. "Yeah. This one's really good."

He was narrating his own expedition. Children did that, filling the silence with a voice because the forest was too large for the small stretch of it they knew. I'd forgotten how that sounded. The earnestness of it. Someone could talk to no one and mean every word.

"Two more," he told the basket. "Two more and we go home."

I understood the appeal of a basket as a companion. It left him little to argue with.

He didn't hear the boar.

I did.

The spirit beast came through the undergrowth with enough force to shake soil from the roots. It went through trees, or between them, or close enough to strip bark from the trunks it shouldered past. The sound arrived a heartbeat before the sight: a deep, guttural snorting, the crack of branches, and the heavy thud-thud-thud of something too large moving too fast.

It was a boar. The basic shape was right: four legs, tusks, and a blunt head built for ramming. Its size was another matter. It was as large as a draft horse, maybe larger. Bristles stood rigid as quills along its spine, each one thick as a finger and tipped with the dull gleam of saturated qi. Its eyes were too bright, the sickly amber of the irises pulsing under the brow. Yellowed, scarred tusks curved upward past its snout.

Low tier two. It had been eating well; the qi in this forest was rich enough to feed a beast for decades without plateau.

For me, it would have been a warm-up. For anyone trained, a fight. A mortal child alone in the forest had fewer options, with a basket of poisonous mushrooms next to him and a voice that carried farther than caution.

The boy looked up.

The root fell from his hand.

"No—" A whisper. Just the one syllable. As if saying it could make it true.

For one breath, everything was still. The boar stood with its head lowered, nostrils flaring wide enough to show the pink tissue inside. The boy knelt with both hands flat on the dirt. His mouth was open. His eyes widened as he stared at the tusks, his fingers pressing into the soil.

Then the boar charged.

The boy scrambled. The basket tipped, scattering mushrooms and roots across the forest floor. A few rolled into a patch of ferns. He got one foot under him and caught his other heel on the root he'd just dug up. He fell sideways, caught himself with one hand, and pushed up again. His breath came in short, high gasps. Each attempt to stand brought another hurried breath, too little air for the effort he was making.

He ran.

His arms pumped, his head dipped, and his feet slapped the dirt. He didn't know this part of the forest; I could tell from his path. The boar had cut him off from the village, forcing him deeper among trees that grew too close together for a clear run.

The boar followed. It was faster. It was always going to be faster.

I stood where I was and calculated, the old habit so well established that I scarcely noticed beginning it. The beast's speed, the boy's stride, and the distance between them gave him maybe four heartbeats. Three, if he tripped again.

He tripped again.

A root caught his foot, one of the big knotted ones raised above the forest floor. He went down hard, face-first into the dirt, the breath knocked out of him in a sound that was half gasp, half whimper. His ankle twisted under the root. He clawed at the ground, trying to pull free.

The boar was five paces behind him. Four. Its hooves tore up the earth. Its breath was a wet rasp, audible even over the boy's panicked struggling. The bristles along its spine had flattened. Its whole weight carried forward in the charge, its lowered head steady despite the uneven ground.

The boy twisted around to see it coming. His gaze passed the tusks, the amber eyes, and the impossible mass of the beast. Past it, he saw me.

I was standing between the trees, still as the oaks on either side of me. Arms at my sides, no weapon, no stance. A woman watching.

His face changed. He knew nothing about me, only that I was an adult standing there while he lay trapped. It was the look a child gives any adult when the world breaks. You're big. Do something.

He screamed.

At first the sound was raw, tearing at his throat. Then he found words.

"HEY! Help me! *HELP ME!*"

His voice cracked, then dropped into a lower register. His next words came sharper than the first appeal.

"*Move!* Why are you just *standing* there?"

He had no breath for please. He screamed with everything he had, as urgently as someone drowning, leaving nothing behind for dignity.

The boar was one pace away. Its head was lowered, tusks angled upward. The boy's ankle was still caught in the root. He strained against it once more, then fixed his eyes on me. Screaming was all he could do while he waited for something to happen.

I studied him.

Loud, I thought.

The boar lunged.
