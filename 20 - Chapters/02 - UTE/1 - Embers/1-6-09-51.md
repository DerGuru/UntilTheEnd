Dawn was one watch away, the river still filling the widened channel. The trench was done. The water spread with quiet persistence, rising to the same level on either side of each remaining stone. By dawn, the new section would be fully integrated into the flow. The absorption capacity would peak at midday, when the sun warmed the surface layer and the thermal differential increased the rate of qi-energy dissipation.

The village wasn't at the village.

They were on the southern slope, exactly where Wei had directed them. Fifty-four people, counted automatically as I crossed the ridge, spread across the low ground between the valley and the river plain. Sleeping bundles. Extinguished cooking fires. The particular disorder of a temporary camp established by people who'd packed in haste, arrived in confusion, and organized themselves with the slow, grudging competence of a community that couldn't afford to be incompetent.

The families were grouped by proximity to their original houses, the elderly positioned nearest the ridge (flattest ground, easiest access), the water containers centralized, and the perimeter marked with stones in a pattern that was mine. Wei'd borrowed the logic of my barrier design and applied it to people instead of qi-flows.

He was asleep.

I found him at the camp's edge, lying on the bare ground with his pack as a pillow and no blanket. He'd given his blanket to Mrs. Zhao, whose dislocated shoulder made lying down painful and who needed padding more than warmth. His face in the moonlight was gray with exhaustion.

He was thin, thinner than seven days ago, the cheekbones prominent and the jaw defined, the softness erased by labor, rationing, and a boy's growth outpacing his food. His breathing held the third-step pattern, even in sleep, even on bare ground. It had become part of his architecture.

His hands lay curled against his chest, dirty and raw, the nails broken. Unlike mine now, his left palm had a blister that looked infected.

Twelve years old.

The number recurred like a bass note, keeping everything in the same key. Twelve. Twelve. The age at which most children in stable environments were learning to read, or fish, or negotiate the social hierarchies of other twelve-year-olds. The age at which this twelve-year-old was leading an evacuation.

I knelt next to him. Somewhere behind me, a child turned over in their sleep and whimpered once, before going quiet again.

Wei had to learn to carry this himself.

The thought arrived with its usual clarity: sharp, clean, and undeniable. I'd had it before. It had evolved from observation into something that felt like preparation.

He needed to learn to do this alone. I wasn't planning to leave; that option had been removed from the table by congee and a mother's plea. But the world was no place for the permanent presence of someone like me. I'd spent thousands of years proving that.

He needed to not need me. Eventually. Now he was twelve, the ground was shaking, the sky was glowing, and the village was sleeping on a hillside because a boy had told them to. But eventually. When the crisis passed. When the landscape stabilized. When the drought broke, when the well was clean again, and when the village returned to the ordinary, grinding process of existing.

He needed to stand up in a square and not look for me at the edge of the crowd. He needed to build barriers without being shown how. He needed to read terrain, count resources, and make decisions larger than his experience. He needed to trust the gap between his capacity and his reach was a space he could grow into, not fall through.

He needed to not need me.

A thought that returned heavier each time, like a nail going in.

I looked at his hands. Small, broken, and dirty. Holding nothing. The hands of a boy who would someday be a man, who would need to hold a world without a very old woman standing behind him with clenched fists and singing bones.

I stood up. I walked to the ridge and looked east.

The qi-column had stabilized into a steady, sustained glow, the final compression before ignition. Xu Ran was ready. The tribulation would come with dawn.

Dawn was in one watch.
