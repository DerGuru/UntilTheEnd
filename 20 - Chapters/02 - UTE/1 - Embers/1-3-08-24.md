Wei fell asleep by the fire in the middle of a sentence. The remark lost its verb, then its subject, and finally its speaker. He had been talking about the market, the fish, or Old Lan's goat getting through Chun's fence again. As the pauses lengthened, he tipped sideways against the stone he'd been sitting beside. His legs drew up. His arms folded across his chest, his head resting on nothing in particular.

Twelve years old. Asleep, he looked younger. His jaw relaxed, leaving his face softer than it had been while he argued about herbs or decided what to do next. Firelight caught the curve of his cheek, the parting of his lips, and the shadows under his eyelashes. A child's face. Curled against the stone, he looked small enough that I could forget how much work he expected of himself.

His breathing was slow and regular, deep enough that a small movement near the fire left him undisturbed. I found that harder to understand than his stubbornness, his intelligence, or his talent for following a woman who meant to be left alone. He slept here in the open, by a fire that would die before dawn. Around him, the forest was emptying of animals. A cultivator close enough to threaten the valley gathered power.

And he slept.

The thin moon gave just enough light to deepen the shadows. Crickets sang, fewer than seven nights earlier; even the insects were thinning. Far northwest, the cultivator's qi pulsed at the edge of my awareness. His repeated pull was steady, stronger than before, and closer than yesterday.

The fire crackled. A log collapsed into embers, sending a brief shower of sparks upward. One landed on Wei's sleeve. The bright point dimmed against the fabric without burning through. Small marks from previous fires already spotted the cloth. Warmth had to be earned in his household and earning it left evidence.

Not my problem.

The thought was clear. I had returned to it so often that believing it took no effort. The boy, his father, the village, and the cultivator emptying their forest could all become somebody else's concern. I could walk away from this place as easily as I had walked into it. Distance had severed other connections reliably enough.

Wei shifted with a soft murmur in his sleep. His hand moved across the ground, found nothing, and curled back against his chest.

I had done it before, more often than I could count. Cities, villages, forests, and civilizations had become places I remembered imperfectly after I left them. That was my immortality: the ability to be present for everything and responsible for nothing. A spectator's existence. Clean. Efficient. Unburdened.

The boy shivered in the cooling air. The embers still glowed orange, but in half a watch they would be ash. After another half watch, a child sleeping on damp ground would feel the cold through his thin clothes. His fingers were already curled close to his chest. The cloth stretched between his shoulder blades, leaving that small space exposed next to the stone. He kept what warmth he could around his hands.

I didn't need warmth. Temperature had stopped mattering around the fall of the Third Dynasty, whichever third dynasty that was. There had been several. Heat and cold reached me without requiring anything of me, as little disturbing as the weight of my clothes. I wore those because nudity attracted attention.

The outer robe was dark fabric, worn soft by years of travel. It rested on my shoulders with the weight of something I didn't need. I replaced such robes when they fell apart and wore them anyway. Habits outlive their reasons, if you live long enough.

I stood and walked to where he lay. I slipped the outer robe from my shoulders and laid it over him.

I settled the fabric over his shoulders and tucked the edge under his chin, leaving room for him to turn in his sleep. It took little care to cover him properly. Somebody watching from the other side of the fire would hardly have noticed.

He slept on, his breathing steady. Warmth gathered under the robe until the shivering stopped.

I turned my back and walked into the dark.

I gave myself the simple explanation as I went. It was an outer robe. The night was cold. He was a child. Fabric retained warmth against skin, which I understood as well as stone or water. An ordinary exchange of heat was a sufficient account of what I'd done.

Not my problem. The village had its own people. Wei had a family to look after him. Showing him herbs did not make him my student, however often he came back with his basket. Whatever fight the cultivator was preparing in the forest was his own.

Darkness closed around me. The depleted forest was quiet, with too few small sounds to disguise a traveler's passage. My footsteps made no sound on the moss. I left little evidence between the trees, walking in a direction I had chosen for no reason I intended to examine.

Behind me, the fire's last embers turned from orange to gray.

Behind me, a boy slept under a robe he hadn't asked for, given by a woman who would deny it happened if the question were ever asked.

Not my problem.

The words were clear. They were final. They were, for the first time in four thousand years, unconvincing.
