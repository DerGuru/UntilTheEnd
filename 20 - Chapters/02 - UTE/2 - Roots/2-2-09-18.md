On the eighth day, rain soaked the path. It fell through the morning and into the afternoon. The forest faded gray against the sky. Mud sucked at Wei's soles with every step.

Late in the afternoon, we found a rock overhang just deep enough to keep the rain off if we sat close to the wall. Wei tried shifting his legs. Water dripped onto his knee. He drew it back under the ledge and settled for the narrow strip of dry ground.

He built a small fire under the stone. The damp wood smoked heavily, giving little heat. He wrapped his robe tighter. His jaw was set as he held trembling hands between his sleeves and the flames.

He set the rice to cook again. Rain pattered just beyond his feet. He tended the pot as carefully as on a dry evening, checking it whenever the flames rose.

The fire barely crackled under the rain on the rocks. We were a long way from any house.

Wei looked at me across the fire. Orange light deepened the shadows under his eyes. He looked older there, huddled in his wet robe.

"The whole world is afraid of you."

He said it with the casual certainty he used for weather, his hands still held out to the fire.

I turned to him. He looked back.

"I'm not."

His voice stayed level. The wet wood smoked between us.

The rain fell. The fire popped. I waited for him to add something, a question I could answer instead. He held my gaze.

"Should you be?"

He considered it, looking down at the flames before meeting my eyes again.

"No."

One word. I still remember his face above that miserable fire when he said it.

Wei eventually fell asleep next to the embers. I lay awake under the cold stone, listening to rain in the darkness.

As the fire dimmed, I thought of a scholar who had once met my gaze that easily. A thin, precise man. I'd met him in a library large enough to deserve the name, a whole building set aside for keeping knowledge where people could find it.

He'd found me rereading books I already knew. I was looking for something I might have missed in the first three hundred readings.

Other people had given me room, even those who couldn't have known why. He came close enough to see the page. He asked precise questions, listening carefully to each answer. When he wanted to know more, he simply asked again.

A log collapsed inward. Sparks rose into the rain before reaching the overhang's edge. Wei murmured in his sleep, his words indistinct.

I answered some of his questions. More than I'd answered for anyone in centuries.

He wrote books from the fragments I'd given him. They made him famous, though I had told him so little of what I knew. His fame brought him influence. Eventually, powerful institutions began asking him their own questions about the woman in the library.

He grew formal with me. Later, he stopped asking questions. In the end, he stopped speaking to me at all.

For forty years we crossed the same streets. He would look straight past me. He died old, respected by the students around him. They quoted his books without knowing their source.

His books survived him two hundred years before they became dust.

Reverence always tips.

Across the dying fire, Wei slept on. He had said "no" as readily as he might tell me he was hungry.

I took a slow breath and fed the fire. Its embers brightened under the damp branch.

Wei treated me as someone he could argue with. Even after what he'd seen, he complained about my answers. How long could he keep doing that? The scholar had asked good questions too.

The rain fell. The fire held. The boy slept.

I didn't have an answer. I rarely did.
