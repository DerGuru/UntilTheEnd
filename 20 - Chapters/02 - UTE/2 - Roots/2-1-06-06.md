By afternoon, the path had narrowed to something that might once have been a deer track. We were barely a day out of the village, climbing steadily northward through forested hills. Wei hadn't complained. His legs were shorter than mine and he had little experience of travel, but he kept pace. I had learned to rely on that stubbornness.

We stopped at a clearing. A stream ran through it, its cold mountain water tasting of stone and iron. Wei drank, washed his face, and sat on a rock.

"I want to try something."

I didn't respond. I was refilling the water container, my back to him, my attention split between the task and the ambient qi-signatures in a two-li radius that were, at this point, entirely wildlife.

"I'm going to try the circulation."

He meant the basic exercise: routing qi through the primary channels in a closed loop. It was little more than stretching before a run. He'd done it hundreds of times, in every posture I'd prescribed. We'd practiced on the hill, in the hut, and elsewhere around the village.

His channels had since been torn open, rebuilt, and widened by a flood of qi meant for a tribulation. That strength had ended up in a thirteen-year-old boy, caught in the wrong place at exactly the right time.

I should have said no. Or wait. Or "let me watch your channels first." They had only just healed, and I should have tested how they held the flow first.

I went on refilling the water container. I wanted to see what would happen. Standing there with cold water over my fingers, I let him begin. There is no kind way to describe that choice.

Wei closed his eyes and breathed. Three counts in. Three counts out.

The qi moved.

It moved wrong.

It was too fast. A circulation that used to take thirty heartbeats finished in four. The qi completed the primary loop before he'd exhaled. It went around again, then again. Each cycle ran faster through channels that offered far less resistance than they had seven days earlier. He was still trying to control the slower flow he knew.

I turned. "Wei—"

An arm-thick pine stood four paces to his left. Excess qi spilled from his channels without direction. It struck the tree. The wood broke from the inside: bark split, heartwood splintered, and the whole trunk came apart with three sharp cracks.

Wei fell off the rock.

Silence.

Wood fragments settled. Dust motes drifted. A bird screamed from the canopy and was gone.

Wei sat on the ground, staring at the remains of the tree. He turned his hands over in front of him, then looked back at the splintered trunk.

"I didn't mean to do that."

His hands were shaking.

He stood and brushed dirt from his pants. A grin began to form as he looked at the wreckage. Then he looked more closely. His mouth went slack.

Then: stillness.

He stood in the afternoon light with the destroyed pine behind him. The stream ran past him. The tremor in his fingertips spread to his wrists, shaking both hands.

He made fists. The shaking stopped. He opened his hands; it returned.

He shoved his hands into his pockets and walked away. He didn't look at me.

The ruptures in the tree were clean. The bark had come apart grain by grain, and pale wood showed along the splits, fresh where the qi had torn through.

A standard qi-circulation exercise. A thirteen-year-old with four months of training. An arm-thick tree, dead.

He'd used so little effort. What he'd drawn into the circulation should never have been enough to do this.

I stared at the splinters. There was too much I could no longer explain.
