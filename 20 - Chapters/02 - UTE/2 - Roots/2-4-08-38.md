In the early afternoon, our trail descended onto a floodplain. The river spread wide over water-smoothed stones. A small child sat on its bank, dangling her feet in the shallow current.

She was four, maybe five. Dirt marked her face. She stared into the water while we approached.

No adults were nearby. The girl was thin inside clothes that had belonged to someone larger. Their torn edges hung loose around her limbs.

Wei had seen her first. He stopped, his qi gathering more tightly as he faced her.

"We take her with us."

He used the same tone he'd used to announce our departure from the village. He waited for me to accept the decision.

"No."

He turned fully toward me. I knew that tight mouth. He would be difficult to move from here.

"She's alone."

"Yes."

"She'll die here."

"Maybe."

"*Maybe?*"

I met his eyes and held them.

"We can't. We're being tracked. She'd slow us down. She'd make us visible. She'd need food we are already low on and care we can't provide. She'd be in danger she doesn't deserve because of a situation she didn't create."

I held my voice steady through the list. All of it was true. He had to hear it before we could decide.

Wei's jaw set. The muscle in his cheek flexed and held.

"So we just leave her."

"I didn't say that."

"What then?"

The small girl by the river hadn't turned toward our argument. Her feet were still in the water.

"There's a farm. Half a li south. I saw smoke from the chimney this morning."

Wei looked from me to the girl, then south.

He walked over and crouched by her. He spoke too softly for me to catch without effort. I left the words to them. He used that gentle voice with children, animals, and occasionally me.

The girl stood, looked up at Wei, and took his hand.

They walked south. I followed.

The farm consisted of a stone house, a garden, and a few scrawny chickens. A middle-aged man came out. His tired expression sharpened into irritation when he saw us.

"What?" the farmer said.

"She needs a home." Wei had never bothered much with an introduction when something mattered to him.

The farmer looked at the girl. At Wei. At me. At the girl again.

"Not mine."

"I know."

"Food's tight."

Wei reached into his pack. His hand found the pouch, the thin leather fold that held everything we had in currency. He opened it. Inside: one coin. The last one.

He held it out.

"Look after her."

The farmer took the coin, looked at the girl, and looked back at the money.

"Come on, then," the farmer said evenly to the girl.

She went with him, holding Wei's hand until the farmer offered his. She stood between them, a hand in each. Then she let Wei go.

Wei watched her walk into the house. The door closed.

He turned toward the trail, passing me without a word. The empty pouch hung from his pack.

A quarter of a li later, he spoke toward the ground. "That's not enough. One coin. That's nothing."

"It's what we had."

"It's not enough."

"No."

We walked until the sound of the river faded. Wei's empty hands hung still at his sides. It was the first time in days I had seen them so steady.

By nightfall, the tremor had returned. My qi still flowed into him. We had left our last coin with the farmer; Wei walked on with his hands empty.

His hands swung at his sides, shaking again. He had given what we had, then walked away from a child whose future we couldn't know. I doubted he would forget the weight of that last coin.
