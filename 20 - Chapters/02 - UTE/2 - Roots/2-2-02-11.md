In the following days, Wei pulled his robe closed against the mountain wind again and again. His cough grew worse. It was still a thin, persistent hack, short of anything dangerous. The robe that had served him in the village was too light for this cold. He coughed, wiped his mouth, and walked on. He'd grown up hungry, and discomfort barely registered with him.

His breathing grew shallow. The cough interrupted the third-step pattern again and again. Qi flowed toward his lungs to warm them, draining strength he needed for the climb. He wasn't directing it there; the surges came uneven with each cough.

We walked through high country, past scrubby pines rooted sparsely along the ridges. A steady north wind carried the smell of stone and ice.

"How much further up?"

"Until it levels out."

"When does it level out?"

I didn't answer. He interpreted the silence correctly.

Wei kept training as he walked. If he let the circulation slow, qi pooled in his channels until his hands shook. He had to draw it in, move it through him, and release the excess. Even an ordinary breath required attention now. He carried more qi than he could comfortably hold, with no chance to set it aside.

When we rested, he tried to hide the shaking. He picked up sticks, adjusted his pack, or clasped his hands behind his back as if stretching. There was nobody else to hide it from.

I let him keep those small evasions. He watched my face whenever he made one. I started walking again only when he was ready. He had little enough of his own on this journey. I could at least leave him the choice of when to show me his hands.

In the afternoon we reached a ledge overlooking a valley. Wei sat on a rock with his legs dangling, his face tipped toward the sun. For once it was warm on his skin. His breathing steadied as the cough receded. The left corner of his mouth softened. He rested his hands on his knees.

His hands were nearly still. A fine tremor ran through the fingers. He lifted them from his knees to study them, turning each one slowly in the light.

"They're fine when I'm doing stuff."

"I know."

"Does it go away?"

"It's manageable."

He picked up a stick and began peeling bark. His knife moved with the care he'd learned preparing herbs. Thin strips fell past his knees. His hands steadied as he worked; the excess qi eased into each deliberate movement.

He whittled the stick into nothing and picked up another.

By evening, a pile of wood shavings lay at his feet. His hands were steady. Neither of us mentioned it.
