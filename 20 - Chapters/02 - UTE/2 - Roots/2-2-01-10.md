Twenty-one days north, the mountains leveled into foothills, then rose again. Darker, weathered stone marked an older range. We followed animal tracks and seasonal shepherd paths, walking single file where the trail narrowed. Wei had grown used to the long stretches of silence between us.

He trained while walking, adapting the exercises I'd drilled into him. Every third step, he routed energy through the primary channels. On downhill stretches he exhaled the excess. On the climbs he drew fresh qi from the air, where it grew thinner and cleaner with altitude.

He stumbled over roots regularly. Dividing his attention between footwork and qi-work cost him stubbed toes. He muttered curses under his breath. Occasionally he turned a full stumble into a crouch, pretending he'd intended it.

"Eyes on the path. Not on your qi."

"I can do both."

He tripped over a rock he'd been looking directly at.

"Clearly."

He walked on without answering, jaw set. He took the next stretch of path with exaggerated care, determined to prove me wrong.

We passed a small village: eight houses, a well, and an old man on a stool. He faced the road but hardly saw it. Wei slowed to look at the houses. Smoke rose from a cooking fire. He watched it drift over the roofs, lingering where the path turned away.

"I could buy rice."

I walked on. "Too many eyes."

"It's eight houses."

"Eight houses talk. To traveling merchants. Who talk to towns. Which have cultivators."

He followed. The village fell behind us, its cooking smoke thinning among the trees. The old man sat on his stool. We had passed within a hundred paces of those houses without anyone looking up.

Mid-afternoon, the trail cut through a saddle between two ridges. Wei stopped.

"What are those?"

Three waist-high stones stood at even intervals. Centuries of weather had worn the carved characters smooth. Their spacing was too precise for boundary markers. The arrangement was too small for a shrine.

"Ward stones. Old ones."

He crouched beside the nearest and ran his fingers over the eroded surface. "They don't do anything."

"Not anymore."

"What did they do?"

"They kept things out. Or they kept things in." I walked on. "It doesn't matter which. They stopped working before your grandparents were born."

He lingered another moment, then caught up. Twice he glanced back toward the stones.

That evening Wei cooked rice over a small fire, adding dried herbs from his bundle. The edges burned while the center stayed raw. He studied the result, then the pot, as if checking which part of the instructions he'd missed.

"That looks worse than it tastes."

He tried it.

"No. It tastes like that too."

"You could cook."

"I could."

He waited. I didn't move.

"But you won't."

"Learning opportunity."

He stabbed at the rice with his chopstick. I tried some without a word. Wei had cooked, so the least I could do was taste it.

He unrolled his blanket near the fire and lay on his back, staring up through the canopy gap.

"We **are** being followed."

The statement was sharper than I'd expected from him. I let it sit for a beat.

"What makes you say that?"

"You keep looking behind us. When you think I'm not watching."

"Go to sleep, Wei."

"That's not a no."

"That's a *go to sleep*."

He rolled onto his side. His breathing evened out before the fire settled to embers. At thirteen, exhausted from the climb, he could still fall asleep despite his questions.

Clear stars showed through the gap in the canopy.

I left.

I went south, a distance that would take Wei half a watch to cover. At my pace, I was there before the embers could dim much further. I placed each foot carefully, leaving the forest floor undisturbed.

I stopped on a ridge overlooking the valley we'd crossed that morning. Facing southeast, I extended the faintest touch of awareness. It took no more effort than breathing on a mirror to mist its surface.

Then I laid a qi trail, varying its strength to mimic someone passing through. I made the traces close enough to mine for a tracker to follow with confidence. They led southeast into lower terrain, toward warmer valleys. A fleeing woman heading for civilization might choose that route.

The skill was older than these mountains.

When I returned, I chose a new route for us: northwest, over steeper ground. We would cross exposed ridges and wind-scoured plateaus. We would have cold camps on stone.

I sat by the embers, listening to him breathe. A small cough caught in his chest at intervals. His thin robe lay close around him.

His comfort or his survival. I chose survival. I always chose survival.
