On the third night, I settled us in a hollow between two ridges. We'd covered more ground than a village boy should have managed in three days of mountain travel. Wei's legs had held steady on the climbs. He could still breathe easily where the path steepened. Qi fed his muscles with strength his food alone couldn't have supplied. Anyone watching might have put it down to determination.

I put my attention on choosing our camp, not on how he had reached it.

The hollow sheltered us from the wind. Anyone on the path would pass without seeing us. I chose such places out of habit, and Wei accepted them without question. He had begun to look around when we stopped, checking the approaches. His eyes followed the ridgeline above us, then the gap where we'd come in. He was learning my caution simply by traveling with me.

I waited for him to finish looking before I unpacked the rice.

We made a fire. There was rice for him, nothing for me. After only three days we had a routine: he put the pot next to the flames while I checked the ground outside our camp.

Wei watched the fire.

"Do you think someone will follow us?"

I'd expected the question, though he asked it sooner than I'd thought he would. He held his bowl against his knees, watching me over the fire. We had left his home behind. He deserved to know whether that would be enough.

"Yes."

He tightened his jaw. His eyes narrowed above the bowl, but he held my eyes.

"Who?"

"Everyone."

He lowered his bowl to the ground and went very still.

"Everyone?"

"Everyone who felt what I did. Everyone who heard about it from someone who did. Everyone who has people who look for things like me and report back to people who collect things like me." I paused. "Everyone."

Wei was quiet. The fire popped. A spark rose and died in the cold air.

"But you—" He stopped, then tried again. "You stopped the sky."

He meant: why run.

"That's why they're coming."

He looked down at his hands, where the light had shone under his skin. I had saved him in front of everyone. We were sitting on cold ground far from his home because of it.

"And then?"

"Then we go further."

He considered that with his hands in his lap. The tremor had subsided to a faint vibration, visible only if you knew what to look for. I was looking.

"How long?"

"I don't know."

That was honest. Two honest answers in a row was unusual for me. Across the fire, he studied my face. I found I had no wish to take either answer back.

"Okay."

He lay down, but his breathing came irregular. He held each breath too briefly, then let it out too fast. I had told a thirteen-year-old that people everywhere would be looking for the woman next to him. All I had offered him was "go further." He shifted on the ground, looking toward the gap between the ridges.

Eventually the breathing steadied. The pattern locked in. One, two, three. One, two, three. Sleep.

I sat in the dark, listening to the wind move over the mountain. Somewhere past it, people would be passing news of what I had done. Something they couldn't explain was moving north through the passes with a boy who shook when he stood still. He couldn't cook rice either. His empty bowl sat next to the fire, grains dried fast to its rim.

They would come. I'd meant "everyone," though the news would take time to spread. Cultivators tended their sources of gossip as carefully as farmers tended irrigation ditches. Scouts would arrive first. Representatives would follow. Eventually, someone would decide that asking politely wasn't going to work.

You'd gone south by then. Here, smoke blew northward across my hands.

We had a few stretches of seven days. Perhaps months, if luck held and the northern route was as unwatched as I believed. That would give us time to put distance behind us. I could change direction often enough to make our next destination hard to guess.

Time enough for Wei to learn what his body could do. And what it couldn't.

I fed the fire. For him, though I would have said for warmth if anyone had asked. His collar had slipped away from his neck in sleep.

The stars turned. The mountain held still. The boy slept.

I watched.
