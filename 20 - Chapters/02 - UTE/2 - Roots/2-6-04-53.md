Upstairs, I listened to Xu Ran begin his story to the innkeeper. My qi-perception carried his voice through floor and walls as clearly as if I stood beside him.

Pig Nose listened. Professional obligation.

Xu Ran's voice was damaged at its source. The qi-channels that had supported it were depleted, and his once-clear voice rasped through every sentence.

"I followed a lead south. A sect elder told me she'd been seen near the Jade Pass." A pause. "He lied. For the core fragment I gave him."

Pig Nose wiped a glass.

"Before that, another lead. A merchant near Qinzhou. Said he'd met her. Described a woman who matched: height, bearing, and the way she walked." Another pause. "He'd described his aunt. She'd been dead for three years."

Xu Ran's hands rested steadily on the counter. A bowl scraped on the counter, but he barely moved.

"Months. Five, I think. The counting stopped being useful. In each town, the same question. Every answer was wrong, or a lie, or a piece of truth wrapped in enough fiction to make it useless."

He picked up his bowl and set it down.

"I was the strongest of my generation." Quietly, as if speaking of someone dead. "Now I'm less than a novice."

You were the strongest. The bowl settled in its saucer below me, your hands withdrawing. Your core sealed correctly. The breakthrough was clean, elegant, and earned. You were a Core Formation cultivator for approximately two hundred forty heartbeats before the consequences of what I did caught up with you.

The shockwave was yours. The excess energy a new core can't contain. It goes outward. That's normal. That's how breakthroughs work. What's not normal is someone dissolving it mid-flight with a thought. The energy had already left your body when I stopped it, but it remained connected to its source. The disruption traveled back along the channel it had exited through. The channel led to a core that was two hundred forty heartbeats old, still setting.

I cracked you. You were ready; I was the one who broke what you'd made. I'm sorry, but given the same situation, I'd do it again.

I came downstairs just after sunrise. Wei was already at the table, eating rice porridge. Xu Ran sat across the room in the chair he'd occupied the night before. His clothes were crumpled from sleeping upright against the wall, the one support he trusted.

His eyes scanned the room. Habit. The habit was all that remained of the training that had produced it.

His eyes found Wei and lingered.

Wei's qi, suppressed into a tight field, still leaked at the edges. Small fluctuations escaped his control. Xu Ran watched him: a thirteen-year-old with qi-density three stages above his level and an instability pattern Xu Ran would recognize.

He recognized it because he'd worn it.

The signature of cultivation that had outgrown its container: too much, too fast, growth outpacing structure.

He'd been this before, and it had ended with a crack.

The recognition landed. His jaw tightened, his eyes widened, and he lowered his gaze without a word.

"He looked at me," Wei said. Quiet. Under the porridge.

"Yes."

"Like he knew something."

"He recognized your instability."

Wei's jaw set. The word *instability* landed hard; he'd heard it before and didn't like it.

"He was the same," Wei said.

"Similar."

"What happened to him?"

"His core cracked."

Wei looked at his hands. Under the table. Trembling.

Will that happen to me?

The question he didn't ask. The question I couldn't answer without the lie collapsing.
