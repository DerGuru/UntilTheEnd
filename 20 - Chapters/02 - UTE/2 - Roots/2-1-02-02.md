Gray light came through the window on the fourth morning. The air held the night's chill. His mother had finally fallen asleep in her corner, leaving me alone with him. I listened to his breathing, the slow pulse of his qi, and the creak of the walls. The wind still smelled of ash.

His lips moved, shaping sounds as deliberately as if he were awake. The sounds became syllables, then words.

"...tried before..."

I leaned closer. I could hear a sparrow's heartbeat at fifty paces, but I wanted to be certain of these words. His voice was low, lost under the creaking walls.

"...the stopping never worked..."

My hands went still on my knees.

"...she kept going back. Every time. She said she wouldn't and she did and she did and she—"

He trailed off. His face was smooth against the bedding. He looked as comfortable as any boy talking in his sleep.

Something tightened at the back of my throat. I tasted iron. The floor tilted. My vision smeared at the edges and, for a breath, the walls of the hut slid out of place.

Then it passed.

I sat still. Three heartbeats. Five. Hands on my knees, fingers pressing crescents into skin.

He murmured something else, too softly for even me to make out.

Then silence. His breathing settled into the third-step pattern: three and three. His qi followed it steadily.

I unclenched my hands and checked his pulse. His wrist was warm under my fingers, the beat steady. I pulled the blanket straight where his turning had bunched it.

The wind shifted. The gray light didn't change.

I went back to waiting.

He woke up a watch later. I was still at the mat when he opened his eyes.

They were the same brown, in the stubborn face I'd been watching for months. But now qi shimmered behind them like heat above summer stone, flickering and uneven. His newly filled channels still struggled to carry a steady flow.

He blinked at the ceiling, then looked at his hands. He raised them, turned them over, and examined his palms as if deciding whether to keep them.

"What happened to me?"

His voice was rough. Four days of silence had dried it.

"You lived."

"That's not an answer."

"Yes it is."

He sat up slowly, testing each movement before shifting his weight. His shoulders were stiff, but he could move them. He swung his legs off the mat, placed his feet on the floor, and pushed himself upright. He stood.

His mother woke and saw him standing. A broken cry escaped her. She reached toward him from her corner.

Wei looked at her, then at me. He held his jaw tight. Four days ago I would have expected questions before he even stood up. Now he pressed his lips together, studying us both.

"I feel..." He searched for the word without finding it.

"Different," I said.

"Yes."

He walked to the door and opened it. Morning light fell across his face. He stood in the doorway, narrowing his eyes. We'd carried him through it unconscious. Now he could stand there by himself, though I doubted the sunlight felt as it had before. His open channels would admit the qi in the air, the warmth of the light, and subtler currents he had only just begun to sense.

Then he stopped shifting his weight. He stood perfectly still, but his hands began to shake.

Qi pushed through channels wide enough to carry it. They could withstand the pressure, but the flow was still uneven. It surged into his hands.

He looked down and made fists. The shaking stopped. When he released them, it returned.

He looked at me.

"Is this normal?"

"It'll get better."

But I didn't know if that was true.
