The merchant left us at the crossroads. He went east. We went west.

I waited until he was gone. Then I thought about what I'd heard.

Three sects. Three approaches. We were heading into the territory of the one that organized everything, including its searches.

I needed distance. More than the Black Moor feint provided.

Wei was walking ahead. Fourteen steps now.

"Yun."

He'd stopped and was looking past me at the road behind us.

"Someone's following."

I turned to look. Nothing. The road was empty.

"Nobody's following us."

"Not following. Watching."

I extended my senses. Road behind: empty. In the trees, wildlife stirred among the windblown branches. The sky was clear.

Nothing.

But Wei's face was still intent. He felt something my senses couldn't confirm.

"The trees are too even," he said.

I looked at the trees. Oak, birch, and the usual northern mix. Their spacing looked natural at first.

No.

The spacing was wrong. The trees stood at even intervals, symmetric, as if planted by someone whose cultivation philosophy extended to the physical world.

Heaven's Order. Their fingerprint extended to the landscape. They organized every space they occupied. The result was a world imperceptibly too tidy.

I was already a hole in the qi field, a place where a signature should have been. But Wei was a beacon. His unstable core leaked presence like light through a cracked lantern. In organized territory, in a landscape tuned to reveal asymmetry, he was obvious.

"Give me your wrist," I said.

"Why?"

"Because you're loud."

He looked down at himself. "I'm not—"

"Not your voice."

I held his wrist, pushing the technique through contact: a pulse, then a compression, and then the hollow sensation of a presence folding in on itself. He flinched.

"That feels wrong."

"That's because you're used to projecting. This is the opposite."

He tried it and failed. The spiritual equivalent of sucking in your stomach. The effort itself was visible.

"Again."

He tried again. Better. Still obvious, but the shape of the attempt was correct. I let him practice while we walked, correcting his posture each time: chin down, shoulders forward, and steps shorter. Concealment was physical as well as spiritual.

After half a watch, he could hold the compression for thirty heartbeats before it leaked.

The cost was plain. Each compression pushed his qi inward, packing what was already too much for his channels into less room. The stream grew denser and hotter, like a river forced through a narrower bed. The tremor in his hands sharpened with each attempt.

I knew what I was doing. Every cycle of press-and-release trained the qi to push harder when it escaped. The rebounds would grow larger, the instability with them.

But I would rather have him hidden despite the instability than found and dead. So I let him practice.

"The western ranges," I said. "Into the high passes."

"Why?"

"Because wild terrain resists organization. Their symmetry doesn't survive scree fields and goat trails."

Wei's expression said it was true and insufficient.

He turned and walked fifteen steps ahead.

I matched his pace, keeping the compression active. The road stretched behind us between the evenly spaced trees.

But we were quieter now.
