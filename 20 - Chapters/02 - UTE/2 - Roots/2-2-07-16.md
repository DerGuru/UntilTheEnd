Predawn wind blew across the treeless plateau as I packed our camp. Wei lay asleep in his outer robe, curled against a rock outcrop that offered little shelter. His breath made small clouds. His qi pulsed in the third-step pattern; his hands were still against his chest.

I picked up the water container, following the usual order of tasks. We had broken camp so often that I scarcely needed to think about it.

And then I knew.

The Iron Lotus Sect was three days east, on the pass above the Lin valley. The one with the broken bridge. Four cultivators were there: two experienced Core Formation members, one nervous young Foundation Establishment cultivator, and a Dao-Seeking elder leading them. A sect sent an elder like that when it expected resistance.

I knew their formation. I knew their pace. I knew that the Foundation Establishment one had a knee injury from a training accident and favored his left side.

I was as certain of these details as I was of my own name.

Iron coated the back of my throat. The water container trembled in my hand. My qi remained steady under a disturbance I couldn't reach.

I stood still.

I hadn't seen the Iron Lotus in two hundred years. I had neither scouts nor contacts in any sect to send me reports. Even with my perception unfettered, three days' travel was too far to reliably distinguish such details about an organization. Yet I knew about the young man's injured knee.

I just knew.

I turned each detail over. I could question where the knowledge had come from, but I couldn't make it feel uncertain. The formation, the pace, and the knee injury were as clear as before. I stood holding the water container, unable to account for any of it.

"Wei."

He stirred. Within three heartbeats he was looking at me. Forty-two days of uncertain mornings had taught him to wake quickly.

"What?"

"We're taking the west path."

He sat up and rubbed his eyes. "We were going north."

"West."

"Why?"

He waited while I gripped the container. I could have told him about the sect formation on the eastern pass. Then I would have had to explain how I knew. That troubled me more than the cultivators.

"Instinct."

He studied me, his hands resting on his bundle. I was beginning to recognize that look: he wanted more than the answer I'd given.

He didn't press.

"Okay."

We went west into softer hills in the rain shadow of the higher peaks. More vegetation grew here. The walking was easier; Wei's cough improved as his color returned.

I had given Wei the word "instinct." Usually that would have meant a vague sense of direction. This knowledge had arrived complete with particulars I could repeat as easily as a report read that morning.

I had felt fragments of it before: things I knew without learning them, decisions I couldn't account for, and actions that proved correct despite my uncertainty. I had learned to trust those results. Their accuracy was never explained.

Wei stumbled on a root, caught himself, and walked on. I adjusted my pace to his shorter stride.

The western path was easier for him. That much I could explain.

We walked under a clearing sky. I tracked each root near his feet, each loose stone. I gave the path far more attention than it needed.
