We moved in quiet the following days. West, then northwest. Forested hills gave way to river-valley lowlands. I set the pace. Wei followed.

He'd changed.

The changes were small, invisible to anyone who hadn't been watching him for months. His posture was the same. His stride. His voice carried the same inflection and the same Wei-ness that made him recognizable at distances too far to see.

But the pauses were longer. The gaps between observations widened. He still pointed out rocks that looked like animals and clouds that looked like faces. He still filled the silence I generated with a running account of everything we passed. But in the gaps, where his words usually lived, there was something new.

A question. Unasked. Present.

He'd accepted my answer. He'd complied. He'd said "Okay," closed his eyes, and slept.

And he didn't believe me.

His trust was total but he wasn't stupid. He trusted me with his body, his training, his direction, and his life. He didn't trust me with information that contradicted what his own senses told him. His senses told him my answer was incomplete.

He didn't push. Pushing wasn't Wei's method.

He waited, and the waiting was worse than being asked.

On the fifth morning we had breakfast with foraged mushrooms and dried meat. Wei ate with the efficient focus he brought to food, appreciative but functional. He was still growing, and his body consumed everything offered without discrimination.

"These taste like feet," he said.

"Don't eat feet."

He shrugged and ate another one. The shrug of a boy who had spent years eating whatever was available. To him, edible and tolerable were the same thing.

The exchange was small. Everything about it was normal, and the normalcy made the other thing visible: careful jests from a boy who kept up the surface while he worked through something underneath.

He held my gaze half a heartbeat too long for casual conversation.

"Yun?"

"Hm."

Three heartbeats.

"Nothing."

He went back to his mushrooms. The "nothing" was not nothing. But it was the closest he would come to asking and the closest I would come to answering.

A sound. Distant. Sharper than thunder. The crack of qi hitting stone, at least a li away. Maybe more. Both of us heard it. Wei's head turned north. The direction we'd come from.

When the sound didn't repeat, we broke camp. We started our daily journey to everywhere and nowhere in particular.

The trail narrowed into a gorge where the river had cut through limestone over centuries that I could count and Wei couldn't imagine. Ferns grew from the walls. Water dripped. The air was cool and carried the mineral smell of stone that had been wet for longer than it had been dry.

Wei walked ahead. His hand trailed along the rock face, his fingers tracing the rough patches that a child notices without needing words for them. He stopped at a fossil. A shell, pressed into the limestone. Spiral. Ancient. Something that had lived in an ocean that no longer existed.

"This was underwater?" he asked, when I explained.

"Yes."

"The whole mountain?"

"The whole mountain."

He touched the spiral. His finger followed the curve inward, the geometry of something that had grown turn by turn until it stopped.

"How long ago?"

"Before the sects. Before cultivation. Before people."

He turned to me. The question in his eyes was not about the fossil.

I walked past him. He followed. The gorge opened into a valley where late afternoon light turned the grass amber. The shadows were long, the walking was easy, and the silence between us was full of things that neither of us would say today.

We camped near a stream before the sun went down. The grass gave way to forest again. We made a fire, cooked, and ate. Night came with its stars, its insects, its quiet.

I sat with my back to him, staring into the fire, when I heard him stand. Quietly. He walked to the edge of the forest and began to train.

Stabilization exercises. My exercises. He performed them correctly, precisely, with the care of a student who was *testing* rather than *trusting*.

He was testing my answer.

His qi-pattern showed it: the methodical quality of a boy told to "train slower" who was training slower and watching whether it produced results.

He trained for half a watch, then returned. He lay down and slept.

Before the lie, he would have asked me to show him. Every time. The question cost nothing to ask. It included me in his practice and made his growth our project.

He didn't ask now.

I sat in the dark. Eyes closed. The bolt held.
