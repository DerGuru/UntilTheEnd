Wei crouched by a small fire, scraping rice out of the pot. I held the flames low to avoid drawing attention. His cooking would sustain life if you ate with enough determination to ignore the texture. Before these last few months, I'd gone ages without eating. With our supplies limited, I returned to that habit. He ate his portion with the stubborn focus of a boy refusing to acknowledge that his cooking still wasn't good.

Up in the mountains, the cold was sharper than it had been in the sheltered village. Wei had pulled his collar up, but his shoulders still shivered. His breath made clouds. When he set down his chopsticks or stopped poking the fire, his fingers shook. Some of it was cold. Some was the tremor that had been there since he woke.

The qi still pressed too strongly through his channels. They had widened faster than he could learn to manage the flow. Even sitting over supper, he had to hold it in check.

Smoke from the fire caught in my clothes. It smelled like other cooking fires in places I'd left.

A village, centuries earlier, in a valley I could no longer place. I'd stayed long enough for the people to give me a name of their own. They had found "the woman on the hill" insufficient after a while.

I'd left that village one morning too, carrying less than Wei had packed. I walked through its gate without explaining myself or saying farewell. The direction had mattered less than leaving. Perhaps the name they gave me lasted a generation or two. I had been gone too long to hear anyone use it again.

The first step had always been easy. Later, in the next place, I would catch the smell of a cooking fire. A door would close somewhere nearby. I'd look up at morning light through an unfamiliar window, remembering the one I had left.

A branch cracked in the dark. Perhaps a fox had stepped on it, or a rock had shifted in the cold. A thread of sparks rose from our fire.

A woman had lived there. I could almost see her by the door. I moved a branch deeper into our fire until sparks caught along its bark and the image faded.

You'd know about the sharp ones.

I'd stopped sleeping under roofs after a while. Staying meant learning names, recognizing faces, and getting used to someone else's cooking. I had managed to avoid all that for a long time. Wei scraped another lump of rice from the pot.

"Yun?"

I looked up. Wei was across the fire, staring at me. Firelight from below deepened the shadows around his eyes, and his jaw was sharp above his raised collar.

"You were somewhere else."

"No."

"You were. Your eyes went—" He made a gesture. Distant. Gone.

"Eat your rice."

He ate his rice, chasing the stubborn clumps with his chopsticks. Once he glanced back at me. I waited for another question, but he bent over his bowl again.

The fire crackled in the cold. Stars appeared one by one above us. This high above the lowland haze, the sky was clearer than it had been in the village. Faint stars showed between the bright ones I knew.

"Yun."

"What."

"Are you going to tell me where we're going?"

"No."

"Okay."

He lay down, pulled his thin outer robe tighter, and closed his eyes. Before the next branch burned through, his breathing had settled into the third-step pattern. He followed it even in sleep now.

I sat by the fire, watching the embers and the boy. I hadn't planned to sleep.

Cold darkness filled the space past our little circle of light. Three paces away, Wei breathed in counts of three.
