Six farmers were spreading vegetables on blankets near the well. The village did this every eight days, calling it a market. A traveling merchant occasionally appeared with salt, needles, and questionable dried fish. Today his cart stood at the village entrance. His voice carried across the square as he haggled with Mrs. Liu over the price of lamp oil.

The noise would cover our departure. So would the unfamiliar face holding everyone's attention. It was enough.

I'd been on the stone all night, watching the stars turn above the village. By dawn I had chosen our route. I'd left too many places to need much time over the decision.

North, up through the passes. The terrain was steeper there, cold enough to discourage casual travelers who might report unusual sightings. There was no sect presence. The southern route was a trade road, as was the eastern one. Trade roads meant cultivators: some traveling, others stationed there, all observant. To the west lay two hundred li of flat, exposed farmland. We would have nowhere to disappear.

In the mountains, the air thinned and the trees turned to scrub. The few people you met were shepherds. I had generally found them more interested in sheep than conversation.

I visited three vendors while the market was busiest. From the first I bought two bags of rice, enough for a month if rationed. From the second, dried meat that was tough, bland, and filling. I bought a small knife from the merchant's cart. It would serve for peeling vegetables or gutting fish. It cost little enough to make the purchase unremarkable.

I placed the coins, took the goods, and left without speaking.

Twelve coins total. Most of what I had. I would leave the rest on the hut's table. Whoever found it would have use for it; there was no need to explain.

Wei was packing when I returned.

I hadn't told him. I'd walked into the hut intending to explain the route, when we would go, and why. Some of my reasons were sound. Some would take careful phrasing to pass for sound. I had prepared all of them, but he was already packing.

His bundle held a clean change of clothes, threadbare at the elbows despite the patches. Next to it lay the knife he'd carried since summer. He was adding dried herbs, the ones he'd collected and preserved so carefully. I'd taught him which plants mattered. He had taken every part of that teaching seriously, down to how each stem should be tied. That was Wei.

He was separating the herbs. Half went into his bundle, half into a cloth bag. The bag also held three worn copper coins, the entirety of his savings.

"What are you doing?"

He looked up. "Packing."

"I can see that. Why?"

"You are leaving, so we're leaving."

I waited for the explanation, the reasoning, the argument.

"I know they want us to go." He went on sorting the herbs. "And I saw you at the market. We go."

He tied the cloth bag, stood up, and walked out.

I followed him to his family's house and stood at the door while he went inside. Through the gap in the doorframe, he handed the bag to his mother. She took it, then looked from the tied cloth to his traveling bundle.

"That's enough for three months. If you're careful."

His mother opened the bag. She touched the neat bundles of herbs, then the three coins. Wei stood waiting with everything else he owned tied up for the journey.

She started to speak. He shook his head.

"I'll come back."

He said it with the certainty of thirteen. He stood straight under his bundle, looking her in the eye.

Her mouth trembled. She looked down at the open bag, closing her fingers around its neck, then met his eyes and held them.

Then she nodded once. I knew that tight nod from his father too. Both of them used it when Wei had made a decision they couldn't change.

His sister stood in the doorway behind their mother. She didn't come out.

Wei turned and walked past me without stopping.

We left while the market was still busy, passing under the crossbeam of the north gate. Its two posts cast narrow shadows across the path. The merchant's voice covered our footsteps; Mrs. Liu was still arguing about lamp oil. Wei carried his bundle ahead of me. We reached the treeline while everyone was watching the cart.

Nobody else came to say goodbye. The village lay behind us, its windows shuttered toward the clearing. I had sat so long on that hill, watching a boy gather herbs between the trees and the river.

Wei walked two steps in front of me. He'd held the same distance when he'd first followed me out of the forest. Now he led, with his eyes fixed on the path.

The bundle bounced on his thin shoulders with each step. When his foot found an uneven root, he stumbled. His qi shifted just too late to steady him.

The path climbed. The village fell behind us. The trees closed in.

We didn't look back.
