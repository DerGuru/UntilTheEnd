Wei walked carefully away from the hut, placing each foot before committing his weight to it. It was his first time outside in four days. The qi in his channels shifted with every step. He paused over the rough ground, then lifted his face to the sunlight. Even the air would feel different through those channels now. Everything around him carried qi.

He walked through the village and nobody greeted him.

The fisher saw Wei coming and turned away. He'd shown the boy how to tie nets. He called him "kid," sometimes cuffing him gently on the back of the head when he dropped a knot. Now he bent over a net that didn't need mending. His hands moved with their usual care.

"Uncle Chun?" Wei's voice carried across the space between them, clear and steady. He hadn't yet understood why the greeting wouldn't be returned. "I wanted to ask about the—"

The fisher's hands moved faster on the net. That was all. That was enough.

Mrs. Zhao, whose shoulder I'd accidentally helped during the healing, was sitting outside her door. She saw Wei, stood, and went inside. She closed the door carefully behind her. The latch settled so softly I doubted he heard it.

Two children ran when he approached. They glanced back at him before their mother appeared in a doorway, gathered them, and vanished.

"I'm not going to—" Wei stopped speaking to the empty doorway.

Wei halted in the village center, with the well behind him and the granary ahead. The paths to the fields, the river, and the forest were empty. Wherever he looked, someone had just gone indoors. Nobody had called a warning. They had seen him coming; that had been enough.

I stood thirty paces away, leaning against the wall of the hut with my arms crossed.

Wei stood in the empty square. His hands shook at his sides. He kept his eyes on the houses.

"What did I do?"

He said it to the empty square, to the closed doors and shuttered windows. The faint flicker still showed in his eyes, and nobody nearby was willing to meet them.

He turned to me across the thirty paces. I remember how tightly he held his mouth, there in the sunlight. He had come out ready to speak to them all. Now he stood with his shoulders drawn in, waiting for me to explain.

"Is it always going to be like this?" His voice carried easily across the distance. In that silence, he hardly needed to raise it.

I held his gaze. I had no answer to give him.

"Fine." He looked away from me.

He walked back to the hut and sat on the doorstep without a word. His hands shook in his lap. He clasped them together, pressed them between his knees, and held them until they stopped.

The wind moved the grass. A door creaked, somewhere. Nobody opened it.
