The evening light had turned the fields copper when Wei's mother came up the slope toward me. I sat on my usual flat stone at the edge of the village, where the rising ground gave me a view of the forest and the northern mountain path.

I'd seen her a dozen times in all the months I'd lived on the hill. She was usually busy with a meal or working in the yard. Sometimes I saw her in the doorway when Wei left in the morning, there again when he returned. She didn't speak much. When she did, she sounded unaccustomed to being listened to.

She was thinner than Wei, which was notable given that he had spent six months underfed. Her hands were raw and calloused from working the earth every day. Decades of sun, wind, and worry had lined her face. I could have passed her among the other farmers without recognizing her, before those days at her son's mat.

She came on, though her steps slowed near the stone. Her mouth moved once before she reached me, as if rehearsing. She stopped two paces away.

She faced me. The muscles around her mouth tightened. Her hands hung stiffly at her sides.

"Please go."

Two words, spoken so carefully that I could hear the breath she took between them. She had watched what I could do with my hands. Now she stood in reach of them, asking me to leave.

Her shoulders were rigid. When I shifted on the stone, she pressed her heels into the ground but didn't move.

"My son," she said. She stopped, then tried again. "He was a normal boy."

Was. Past tense. She'd moved him there already.

"He caught fish. He collected herbs. He argued with me about eating his rice. He was—" Another stop. Her jaw worked. "Normal."

I waited.

"Then you came."

She let the words stand. I had come; her son had changed. I could hardly dispute the order.

"He has light in his eyes now," Wei's mother said. "People are frightened. I don't sleep. The elder won't come near this hut." She swallowed. "He's my son. I want him to be safe."

"He is safe."

"Is he?"

She waited for an answer. She watched my face as closely as she'd watched his breathing in the hut.

Her raw hands would be in the field again tomorrow, feeding the same family while people shut their doors against her son.

"Yes."

She waited to see if I'd say more. I didn't.

She nodded. Once. Tight.

She turned and walked back down the slope. Her steps were slower going down. Halfway to the hut, she rubbed one hand over the other, working at a callus with her thumb.

She was a thin woman walking back to a family she couldn't protect from something she couldn't understand.

She was the best of them. She'd come all the way up the slope to say "please" to me. I still remember the effort it took her to stand at that stone.

The village lay below my stone. We could no longer stay.

Tomorrow I would tell Wei. Tonight I would let his mother have one more night of pretending that her son might stay.
