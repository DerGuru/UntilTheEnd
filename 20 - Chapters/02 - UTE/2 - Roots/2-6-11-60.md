We wandered until night fell in the forest, then made camp. Wei poked the fire with a green stick. The wood was damp. The resulting smoke was thick and dark.

"You let him go," he said.

"He left. There's a difference."

"You could have made him stay."

"Why would I?"

"Because his core is cracked, he's walking north into nothing, and you watched him leave the way people watch things they're not done with."

The fire cracked. Sparks rose orange into the dark, burning out before they cleared the canopy.

"He's not my responsibility."

"I didn't say he was." Wei set the stick down. "I said you watched."

True. I said nothing. The small sounds of burning wood filled the pause, each crack leaving less of the log.

"At breakfast," Wei said. "When he told me about the tremor. About slowing down. You didn't say anything."

"He was right."

"I know he was right. That's not what I'm asking." Wei studied me through the smoke. "He told me the truth. You gave me a theory."

"They're the same."

"They're not. He said it breaks. You said I'm growing too fast."

I exhaled, a breath that cost something.

"If a core grows faster than the body can regulate, the channels overheat. The tremor is the warning. If the growth doesn't slow, yes. It could break and does so most of the time." I looked at the fire. "Growing too fast is the cause. Breaking is what happens when the cause isn't addressed."

Wei was quiet. The explanation was sound, and he considered it.

"Then why didn't you tell me that part?" he asked. "The breaking part. Why just the cause?"

"Because the cause is the thing you can act on. Slowing down avoids the break. Focus on the problem, not the consequence."

Correct, precise, complete. An answer that closed a door and locked it.

Wei nodded slowly, accepting the answer like a coin whose weight, edges, and face he had checked. Valid currency. Potentially counterfeit.

He picked up another stick and stripped bark with his thumbnail. The motion was patient. I mistook the patience for resolution.

"You asked him something," Wei said. "At the inn. After I went inside."

"We talked."

"You asked what it felt like. When the core breaks."

I turned to him. He wasn't guessing. He'd heard through whatever gap existed between my assumptions about privacy and his actual range of perception.

"I did."

"It was the first question you've asked anyone since I've known you."

"That's not true."

"Name another, that's not pleasantries, but a genuine request for knowledge."

I opened my mouth and closed it. A damp branch hissed in the fire.

"You don't ask," Wei said. "You answer. You explain. You correct. Sometimes you instruct. But you don't ask. Asking means not knowing and you always know."

He let that sit. So did I.

"But you asked him," he said. "About breaking."

"Curiosity."

"You're not curious. You're the least curious person I've ever met. Everything you see, you've already seen."

Also true. The boy catalogued.

"He was a new case."

"He was a mirror." Wei held my eyes. Steady. "You asked him about breaking because you wanted to know what happens to me."

The stick in the fire shifted. A log settled.

"That's a reach," I said.

"Is it?"

Silence. The fire burned cleanly now, offering no distraction.

"You worry," Wei said, reading my face. "About him. About me. About the tremor, the breaking, and all of it. And when I ask, you give me the version that sounds like an answer but has the weight removed. You told me 'growing too fast.' You didn't tell me 'it could break.' You let a stranger tell me that and then you didn't correct him and then when I bring it up you explain how they're technically the same and they are technically the same and that's exactly the problem."

He wasn't raising his voice. He was lowering it. Each sentence quieter than the last.

"You do this," he said. "Every time. Someone matters and you say they don't. Something hurts and you say it's nothing. I ask and you give me the smallest true answer that still fits inside a lie."

He was organizing the evidence against me.

"'Sleep.' 'It doesn't matter.' 'Just a traveler.'" He listed them, my deflections from days and months, in sequence. "And tonight: 'He's not my responsibility.' 'They're the same.' 'Curiosity.' 'A new case.'"

He looked at me across the fire and the stinging smoke.

"I don't believe you anymore. Not as long as you keep feeding me breadcrumbs, while keeping the loaf for yourself."

His voice was low. Steady. Every half-answer I'd given since we'd met added to the weight of it.

"Wei—"

"I'm not asking you to tell me everything. I'm telling you I notice when you don't."

He lay down facing the fire and pulled his blanket up. He'd put the question aside like a tool whose work would have to wait.

Leaves stirred overhead. The fire burned down.

I don't believe you anymore.

The sentence sat in me. It had been coming since the first lie.

The fire went out and I didn't relight it.

Wei breathed. Sleep or its imitation. He deserved my not poking.

I sat in the dark. It was a long night.
