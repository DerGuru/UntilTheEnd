I sat by Wei's mat with my hands on my knees. Three days had passed since the wave, since I'd pressed my hands to his chest and his channels had reshaped under my fingers. The village had watched me crouch over a boy and do something they couldn't name. I had offered no explanation.

Wei hadn't woken up.

He was sleeping deeply, each breath steady while his body rebuilt itself. He had no say in any of it. His channels were wider than they'd been. Stronger. The qi flowing through them had settled into a rhythm that matched his heartbeat. The third-step pattern I'd drilled into him for so many days now ran like a second pulse.

The faint glow under the skin of his wrists and temples had finally faded. The channels had finished reshaping. All that remained was the waking.

The mat pressed rough against my legs. The hut still smelled of ash, dried herbs, and the mineral tang of dispersed qi.

Outside, a chicken scratched at the doorstep. Claws against packed earth. It had made the same sound yesterday. Presumably it had been doing so ever since it left the egg.

I waited.

I don't wait. Thousands of years of existing had given me a functional relationship with time: I moved through it, or it moved through me. Either way, I had better things to do than sit over a boy counting the intervals between his breaths. I even knew how deeply he should exhale. Twenty-one days earlier, he'd been a stubborn twelve-year-old who couldn't hold a qi-breathing count past four.

He was thirteen now. His birthday had passed somewhere in the crisis: during the drought, the tribulation preparation, or the days when everyone was too busy surviving to count dates. I hadn't noticed. He hadn't mentioned it.

The lie tasted stale.

His mother sat in the corner. She'd been there since the healing, since I'd pulled my hands back. The glowing lines had faded and the boy had shifted from unconsciousness to sleep. She hadn't left. She had barely eaten, as far as I could tell. Her hands were folded tight in her lap, her knuckles white.

She didn't look at me.

She watched her son's face. His chest rose and fell steadily under the blanket. He was alive. For now, she could see that much for herself.

Her pulse was too fast at her throat. Her breathing was shallow. Every so often her eyes flicked toward me, then back to Wei. Whenever I moved, her hands tightened.

On the second night, Wei's qi had spiked: a single pulse, sharp enough to rattle the clay cups on the shelf.

His mother had gasped. I'd already moved. With a hand on his forehead, I traced the spike through his channels and smoothed it before the cups stopped rocking.

She was on her feet, halfway across the room. "What's happening to him?"

"Growing pains." I pulled my hand back. "His qi-channels are restructuring. Like a river finding a new bed."

"That—that is not—I don't understand."

I kept my hand near his forehead. She watched it until the cups were still.

Wei slept on. Undisturbed. The glow at his wrists pulsed once, faintly, then stilled.

Outside, the village was quiet enough for me to hear her breathing.

Doors had closed and windows had been shuttered. The paths around the hut were empty. Children used to pass under the window; now there was only the occasional chicken.

Elder Li had come to the door twice. His measured footsteps slowed as he approached, and both times he stopped three paces away. He stood there for a while, then turned and walked back.

The third time, he made it to the threshold.

"How is the boy?"

His voice was steady enough. His hands weren't.

"Sleeping."

Elder Li lingered. His eyes moved from Wei to my hands. Then he gave a tight nod and left.

I sat and counted the intervals again.

The chicken had moved on. Its scratching faded toward the well, replaced by the low drone of insects in the late-afternoon heat. In the hut, the boy breathed in counts of three.
