On the third morning after we left the main road, I watched Wei train in a clearing among the pines. We'd moved into the foothills, where every change in elevation made walking a war with gravity. His training had changed too.

He'd woken before dawn and reached the clearing before I'd noticed his absence.

He was training, but the focus had shifted.

The old training: maximization. More force. More speed. More range. He had trained like fire, expanding in all directions, consuming whatever fuel was available.

The new training: subtraction.

He was compressing his core, drawing his qi-signature inward until it sank below perception. He was practicing invisibility.

The techniques were his own adaptations, extensions of what he'd learned. I recognized some movements as fragments of Xu Ran's form, observed during their tension-filled mornings at the inn. Wei had watched, catalogued, and translated. He was taking principles from a broken cultivator's muscle memory and folding them into his own practice.

I stood at the treeline and said nothing.

His compression technique was producing results: his qi-signature dimmed, flickered, and nearly vanished. For three breaths, standing in a clearing in the predawn gray, Wei was invisible to qi-sense. Gone.

Three breaths. Then the compression failed. His signature snapped back, louder for having been suppressed.

He stumbled, gasping, with his hands on his knees. Both hands trembled, the right worse than the left.

He straightened and studied his hands with a clinical gaze. Then he did it again.

I watched him do it twelve times. Twelve compressions. Twelve periods of near-invisibility, ranging from one breath to five. Twelve rebounds. Twelve tremors.

On the thirteenth attempt, his nose bled.

A thin line, left nostril. He wiped it. Backhanded.

He stopped, sat, and drew a slow breath. His qi settled.

Then he saw me.

He didn't startle. Either he'd known I was there, or he'd decided the training mattered more than being observed.

"How long?" he asked.

"Long enough."

"The third one lasted five breaths. Did you count?"

"I counted."

He was pleased. Trying not to show it. The effort produced a face that showed it more.

"Getting better," he said.

"Getting more costly."

His hand went to his nose. The check had become reflex. Clean this time. He wiped his fingers on his trousers anyway, the ghost of previous blood.

"Cost is acceptable."

It wasn't acceptable. The nosebleed showed what the pressure was doing to him. His hands still shook. But I'd taught him what I could: the basics, the framework, and the principles available to any patient cultivator. My own techniques went further, deeper, and cleaner. They also required being what I was. A hole doesn't compress. It simply is.

I had nothing better to offer him. So I said nothing.

"Xu Ran's technique," I said. "The second position. You've changed it."

Wei looked at his hands, opening and closing them. "He had good form. Stable. Like someone who built the house before he lit the fire."

"Show me."

He showed me how he'd merged Xu Ran's stability principles with his own expansive tendencies. The framework held firm while allowing movement, compressing his qi without constraining the flow.

"Good," I said.

"Good?"

"Good."

"You never say 'good.'"

"I said it now."

He gave me a look I hadn't seen in many days. We had a long way to go toward trust, but he knew how high my standards were, and the word mattered to him.

He trained for another half-watch. I offered technical corrections to refine what he was already doing.

Stability count: four. Out of ten. He'd gotten better, faster, and his body couldn't keep pace.

Four. Where ten was healthy and one was Xu Ran.

I didn't tell him the number.
