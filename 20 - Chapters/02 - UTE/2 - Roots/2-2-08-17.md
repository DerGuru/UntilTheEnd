For seven days, we followed the western route through rolling hills. Clear streams crossed our path. The undergrowth was thin enough that we could walk without constantly ducking branches or stepping over roots. A villager would have called the country pleasant. For once, I would have agreed.

Wei improved in the warmer air. His cough receded as the color settled in his cheeks. However much qi he carried, he was still a thirteen-year-old traveling in a thin robe. The lower country suited him.

He practiced his breathing at camp each morning, then circulated the qi as we walked. Every day he had to tend his channels and regulate the flow. He was diligent. The stubbornness that had sustained six months of village training served him on the road, where he had fewer distractions. I corrected him whenever his form slipped. Otherwise I let him work.

He was getting used to my teaching. After a correction he would glance at me, repeat the movement, and wait to see whether I spoke again.

On the fifth day, he caught a fish.

This requires context. He'd been trying to fish since the second day of our journey. The results had been educational. He'd lost two hooks, broken a line, and fallen into a stream. Once he pulled so hard on a hooked rock that it flew from the water into his chest. The bruise lasted three days.

This time he stood in a knee-deep mountain stream, waiting. The cold soon numbed his fingers. He cast his handmade line, a length of cord tied to a stone chip he'd knapped into something resembling a hook. It was poor equipment even by his standards.

The fish took. It was silver-sided, three hand-widths long. These cold mountain streams held mostly small fish. Few people came this far to catch them.

He held it up with a full grin, turning toward me so I could see the catch properly.

"Dinner."

My eyes went from the fish to the grin spreading across his face.

"It's small."

"It's MINE."

That evening he cooked it on a stick over the fire, adding herbs from his bundle. He turned it carefully. All those attempts were beginning to teach him something.

The fish was good. I didn't say so.

He waited while I chewed. When I handed it back without comment, he grinned again. This one was smaller. He bent over the fish before I could say anything else.

I stared into the fire and pretended I hadn't noticed the grin.

But I had.
