We settled at an inn that smelled of mold, old soup, and too many people sharing too little air. Rain tapped its few windows.

Wei loved it.

After three days of rain, his cough had returned. It sounded wet now, deep in his chest. Qi warmed his lungs each time he coughed. He wiped his mouth afterward as if that settled the matter. Thirteen years of insufficient food had given him more endurance than he should have needed.

I'd chosen the inn despite the close walls and crowded room. Wei needed a roof. He needed to get warm enough to stop shivering. Here someone else would cook for him. He could sit among strangers for a while, listening to ordinary talk instead of wind.

The crossroads settlement consisted of five buildings, a well, and a stable housing two mules. The inn was the largest structure. About twelve people could fit inside, provided all of them were optimistic about personal space.

The round, efficient innkeeper fed people and left them alone. I preferred that sort of hospitality. I'd walked past three other inns in three other settlements before choosing hers.

Wei sat at a table near the hearth. The innkeeper had built a proper fire, tending it often enough to keep the room warm. His shoulders lowered as the heat reached him. He loosened his collar. For once he could sit without bracing himself to get up again.

His eyes widened at the first spoonful of soup. He bent over the bowl, blowing on the next one impatiently.

I sat across from him with a cup of the cheapest tea. Its rim was chipped. I ordered no food. The innkeeper accepted that I had paid for something while occupying her table. I was unlikely to cause trouble, at least while the boy was eating.

A fat merchant sat at the bar, telling a story loudly enough to include every table. He paused whenever someone laughed, then spoke even louder. Apparently the rest of us were his audience too.

I hadn't been listening closely. Conversation mixed with the clatter of crockery and occasional laughter. I had heard much the same sounds in inns across more provinces than I could remember.

Then Wei stopped eating.

"...the genius from the east," the merchant was saying. "You know the one. The one whose core cracked during ascension. Wanders the provinces like a ghost. Asks in every town about a woman."

I went still.

"What woman?" someone asked.

"A woman who stopped the sky, he says." The merchant laughed heartily at his own story. "Stopped the sky! Can you imagine? The man's core is shattered and he's chasing fairy tales."

"Maybe it's not a fairy tale."

"Everything's a fairy tale until someone proves it isn't. And nobody's proved anything about a woman who stops skies."

Wei stared at me, steady and evaluating.

"After a woman?" Wei asked me quietly.

I stared at the still surface of my tea. The hearth light shone brown in it.

"Go to sleep."

He didn't move.

"Yun—"

"Go to sleep."

He went reluctantly, pausing at the table as if he might try once more. I kept my eyes on the cup.

I sat alone while the merchant moved on to other stories. More rumors, more half-truths. People would repeat them at the next inn, certain they'd learned something here.

Xu Ran was wandering the provinces, asking about me.

The merchant made it sound more like a search for answers than revenge. There was little he could do with a cracked core. Perhaps he wanted to understand what had happened to him. Perhaps he thought I could give him a reason.

I knew what he'd seen. With the cold cup in my hands, I imagined him asking. I had no answer that would make what happened bearable.

He would keep looking.

I drank my tea. Cold now. Bitter.

The inn settled into the quieter noises of evening. I waited too. For what, I didn't specify. I'd stopped specifying a long time ago.
