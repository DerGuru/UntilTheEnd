For three days, I tracked Heaven's Order through the marks they'd left on the slopes. They worked like bureaucrats: systematically, comprehensively, and with the thoroughness of people who confused documentation with understanding.

Their evidence was everywhere, though the people themselves stayed out of sight. The symmetry in the landscape: regularized tree spacing, graded stone arrangements, and the subtle qi-residue of cultivation used for ordering. Organizing. Imposing the sect's philosophy on the physical world. They believed reality was disorderly. Disorder was the root of suffering, so correcting it would correct suffering too. They applied qi to the structure of things until the world around them reflected the sect's ideology.

It would have been admirable if it weren't terrifying.

The marks were days old, some of them dozens of days. They were already fading. Loose stones had tumbled out of line; fallen branches lay across the ordered spaces. Weather had been undoing arrangements for longer than organizations had existed.

But their path through the ordered terrain curved northwest, away from the Black Moor where I'd directed them. Toward the mountains. Toward us.

The false trail might still be working exactly as intended. They were following a different trail entirely, one I hadn't laid. Wei's qi-signature was a lighthouse beam, suppressed during the day and released when he slept.

They were following the glow. The shadowless luminescence that Wei produced in sleep, visible to anyone with qi-perception above a certain threshold. A sect dedicated to detecting disorder, one that classified anomalies like librarians classified books, would have registered it, found its source, and followed.

We needed more distance. More altitude. Terrain hostile enough to discourage organizational pursuit and steep enough to attenuate the signal.

More practically: I needed Wei to stop glowing.

"We're pushing harder today," I said. Morning. Camp broken. Packs on.

"Why?"

"Because the terrain requires it."

A half-truth. We could cross this terrain at any pace. But its height would help us, and we needed to gain that height quickly. I was willing to share "the terrain requires it." I told myself that explaining the rest would produce more problems than withholding it.

We climbed. Hard. Two watches and a half without rest. Goats had worn the trail into the steep slope, far from any sect's designs. It switched back on itself repeatedly, without a single stretch of level ground.

Wei kept up. His body, whatever its instabilities, was robust. Qi strengthened his muscles enough to make a climb that should have been brutal manageable. His breathing was easy. He barely sweated. He was climbing a mountain like normal people climbed stairs.

The tremor was worse with exertion. His fingers vibrated on the handholds, a faint percussion against the stone.

He didn't mention it or slow down. He simply climbed, tremor and all.

Around midday, the symmetry stopped.

It ended abruptly. One step: ordered. The next: randomness. Natural. Trees where seeds had fallen. Rocks loosened by erosion. The wild, unmanaged, and beautiful chaos of ground untouched by the sect.

We had crossed out of their territory. Here, on slopes this high, this steep, ordering the whole landscape would cost them more qi than the effort was worth.

"Feel that?" Wei said.

"What?"

"The... looseness. Like the air isn't holding its breath anymore."

He was right. The qi drifted freely here, following the contours of the slope.

"Heaven's Order," I said. Giving him this one.

"They're down there?"

"They are. We're above their operational range."

He processed. "Operational range. They... organize the landscape?"

"They believe disorder is the source of suffering. They correct it."

"By lining up rocks?"

"By lining up everything."

He looked back down the slope. "Sounds exhausting."

"It is."

"Do they know about me?"

"Possibly."

"My qi. The beacon thing."

"Your cultivation signature is distinctive."

"Distinctive enough to follow?"

"Distinctive enough to notice. Following requires resources they may not commit."

"So we stay high," he said.

"We stay high."

"For how long?"

"For however long it takes."

He nodded. Practical acceptance.

We made camp high above the treeline, among rocks, grass, and air so thin that each breath came cold.

The mountains sheltered us simply by being large, steep, and difficult to cross.

I sat with my back against a rock. Wei sat four paces away. The fire was small, because fuel was scarce this high. The sky was enormous, with no branches overhead to hide its full reach.

Stars, more than usual. Thousands. Millions. I gave up counting and let the magnitude stand.

Wei looked up.

"Yun."

"What?"

"I glow at night. Don't I."

He said it with certainty. He'd discovered it himself. Perhaps he'd seen the light in those drifting moments before sleep, when his control eased and awareness lingered.

Three heartbeats of silence. Four. Five.

"Yes," I said.

He nodded once. He looked at the stars, at his hands, and at me.

Then he slept.
