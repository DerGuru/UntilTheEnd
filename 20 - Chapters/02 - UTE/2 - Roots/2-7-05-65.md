The incline steepened over a single day as the foothills became mountains. Cliffs rose above us, then sheer walls. We had to climb stretches of the trail we'd expected to walk.

Wei climbed well. What was remarkable about him was how completely he used his qi. His muscles moved with an efficiency I'd seen in cultivators three times his age. He climbed as if the angle didn't concern him.

He was also hiding from me.

The big things were visible: the nosebleeds, the tremor, and the midnight surges. What he hid were the small things. The progress.

He'd developed three new compression techniques in four days. His qi-signature shifted at every rest stop. Changes that took most cultivators many days of guided practice, and he'd reached them through independent experimentation.

He wasn't showing me or bringing the techniques for evaluation. He had learned from my example that certain kinds of growth were handled privately.

On the fifth day in the mountains, I tested him.

"Channel qi through the second meridian set. Sustained. Thirty heartbeats."

He did it. Clean. Perfect. The execution was so far beyond the exercise's difficulty that the exercise became transparent.

"That was a test," he said.

"It was an exercise."

"It was a test. You're checking my stability."

"Yes."

He sat down on a rock. Flat-topped, positioned by geological processes that had nothing to do with comfort and produced it anyway.

"How am I?" he asked.

"Better than expected."

"Is 'better than expected' good or bad?"

"That depends on what was expected."

He gave me a look that had run out of patience with non-answers.

"Your stability is decreasing," I said.

"I know."

He knew. He'd worked it out from his own observations.

"How long have you known?"

"Since the inn. Since the boy with the broken core showed me what 'worse' looked like."

Xu Ran had become the benchmark, the end state on display.

"And you're still training," I said.

"The compression helps. When I suppress the signature, the pressure drops. Like releasing a valve."

"Temporarily."

"Everything is temporary."

"You're right," I said.

He stared. Surprised.

"I'm right?"

"You're right. Everything is temporary. Including the valve."

He understood. The compression would fail too, eventually.

He nodded, stood, and picked up his pack.

"More walking?" he said.

"More walking."

"Good. Walking helps too."

For a while, he walked next to me. The fifteen-step distance was gone.

Walking really helped. Exertion eased the qi-pressure. A delay, nothing more, but every drop counts.

We walked up into the mountains. Stability counts, compression techniques, and a thirteen-year-old boy whose cultivation was a lighthouse: I carried all of it with me.

Above us, the bare slopes stretched on.
