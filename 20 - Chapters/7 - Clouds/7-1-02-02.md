Another attempt.

Eyes closed. Hands on my knees, the posture of intention, the bodily arrangement that said, that I was focusing. The air entering my lungs with the measured control of someone preparing for an exertion that was not physical but might as well have been, for the effort it demanded.

"I. Am. Mortal," I said, pronouncing each word with the deliberate pacing of someone trying to convince not just another person but themselves, trying to force the words through the layers of disbelief and resistance that had built up over millennia.

And I pushed. Hard. Not with qi, but with the internal instrument. The one that had been creating reality since before I understood what creating meant. The conviction-engine, the deep machinery of belief that lived beneath thought and above instinct. Which operated in the space where what-I-know became what-is. I pointed it at the statement and I pushed.

The self-protection responded.

Not like before. Not the wall, not the headaches, not the deletion-mechanism that had operated through suppression and erasure. This was different. The reality around me — the physical, material, here-and-now reality of a mountainside camp — it twitched. Like a muscle receiving an unexpected signal. Like a fabric being pulled from beneath.

Ten meters away. A single tree. A pine, young and small but rooted. A single tree within the rocks and rubble, that dominated this altitude. The trunk bent the way a tree bends in hurricane wind, except there was no wind. The trunk curved forty degrees from vertical, held for a heartbeat and straightened. The branches shedding pine needles like an animal shaking off water.

"You just bent a tree," you said.

I opened my eyes. Looked at the tree. It was straight again, standing as if nothing had happened, the trunk aligned, the branches still. Only the scattered pine needles on the ground testified.

"I did not."

"You did."

"I was trying to —"

"I know what you were trying to do. You tried to believe yourself mortal but instead you bent a tree." You were writing. The pen moving, documenting something you hadn't anticipated. "The qi. I could feel it. It erupted."

I could feel the residue. The tingling in my fingers, the vibration in my jaw, the aftertaste of power that had discharged without permission. Like a sneeze. Like a reflex. The body's autonomous response to a perceived threat, that was the sentence *I am mortal*.

"Again," I said.

"Is that—"

"Again."

Next attempt. Harder. The push was deeper, more committed, the full weight of whatever I could bring to bear pressed against the conviction that sat in my bones like marrow. I am mortal. I am mortal. I am —

The stones. Around the camp, the ordinary, innocuous stones that littered every mountainside, started vibrating. The high-frequency trembling of objects responding to a force that wasn't physical. Some of them cracked. Small fractures, hairlines, the kind that stones develop when subjected to temperature extremes or seismic events. Except the temperature was constant and the ground was still.

The scrub grass that grew in rock crevices and curled. Toward me. The blades twisting in my direction the way iron filings orient toward a magnet, the biological response to a force that biology wasn't designed to respond to.

My qi was flaming.

Not the controlled, channeled qi of cultivation. Not the deliberate, shaped qi of the warping. Autonomous qi. The kind that operated without consent, that fired in response to the threat-alarm that my body had raised when I tried to tell it something it didn't want to hear. The qi was defending the conviction. The qi was reinforcing the hold.

"Stop," you said.

I stopped. The exhaustion was immediate and draining. The depletion that followed the expenditure of force. I sat there, hands on my knees, breathing. The metallic taste was stronger, thick, copper-bright, the taste of a mouth that had been too close to something electrical.

You were looking at the cracked stones and the curling grass. Your eyes narrowed. The assessment of a scholar recalculating. You took your time. I waited.

"The problem," you said, "is not that you can't believe it."

A pause.

"The problem," you said, "is that every part of you does believe — that you're immortal. And when you try to contradict that, it doesn't argue. It doesn't resist. It —" you gestured at the surroundings — "it defends. Autonomously. Like you pulls a hand from fire. As an instinctive reaction. A reflex. You have to unlearn the reflex."

"I know."

"Now." You wrote something. Underlined it. "How does one unlearn a reflex?"

The question sat in the mountain air between us. Xiǎohú had relocated. She was now twenty meters away, at the edge of the disturbed radius, watching us from a distance that suggested she preferred not to be too close to the next autonomous qi discharge.

How does one unlearn a reflex?

The clouds moved overhead. I watched them. Shapeless. Forming, dissolving, reforming. Clouds didn't have reflexes. Clouds didn't grip. Clouds didn't hold themselves in any particular shape. They were what the wind and the moisture and the temperature made them, moment by moment, without resistance.

I was not a cloud.

I was a fist.

And every time I tried to open, the fist clenched harder.
