The world didn't know what to do with me.

A pair of mountain deer that had been grazing on the sparse scrub a hundred meters from our camp. They raised their heads when I approached. Not the alert-but-assessing posture of animals encountering a potential threat. The instant, full-body flinch of animals encountering something wrong. And then they ran, the panicked, inefficient galloping of creatures whose instinct had received a signal that bypassed the evaluation stage entirely and went straight to leave.

Then the birds. The morning bird-sounds — the ambient, unremarkable chatter of species communicating about territory and food and the routine logistics of avian existence — stopped. A radius of silence that moved with me like a shadow, the quiet that existed within a circle whose center was wherever I was standing.

Xiǎohú was immune. Or indifferent. Or operating on a frequency that didn't register the same wrongness that the other animals detected. She trotted beside me, amber eyes forward, unbothered by the silence or the fleeing or the fact that the natural world was evacuating in a sixty-meter radius around the immortal woman who was trying to die.

Plants were slower to respond, their biology operating on timescales that made animal panic look impatient. But by the second afternoon, it was visible. The scrub grass within range of our camp was leaning — but instead of leaning away, it leaned toward me. The blades and stems tilting inward, orienting in my direction the way sunflowers track light, except I was not light. The animals fled. The birds went silent. And the grass reached for me.

I didn't know what to make of that. Neither did you, when I pointed it out.

You were cooking. Rice again. Supplemented with bitter herbs and dried meat. The bag was getting lighter but not empty yet. The pot was on the fire. The fire was adequate. The scenario was ordinary.

You were watching the rice, monitoring the bubble-rate, performing the culinary calculus that informed your cooking — and the pot cracked. The ceramic surface, a traveling merchant's pot that scholars bought because it was light and functional, split. A hairline fracture appeared on the side facing me, thin as a thread, running from rim to base. Water seeped through, hissing where it met the embers.

"I am currently losing my rice," you said, "because your qi is cracking the pot."

I looked. The pot. The crack. The rice inside, half-cooked, the water draining out through the fracture in a slow, persistent leak.

I blinked.

"It's still doing it," you said. You didn't alarm. Observational. Clinical. The calm, measured tone of a man documenting a phenomenon while the phenomenon was actively ruining your dinner. "Your qi. I can feel it — not like before, not the discharge. More like... pressure. Ambient. Constant. Three meters in every direction, roughly. Everything within three meters is being — compressed. Slightly."

I looked at my hands. They were doing nothing. They were resting on my knees, open, still, not gripping, not producing qi intentionally. And yet: the pot was cracked. The grass was leaning. The birds were gone.

"I'm sorry," I said.

The words came out before I processed them — the automatic apology of someone who is causing damage without intent, the reflexive social response to being the source of inconvenience. Sorry. But to what? To the pot? To the rice? To your dinner? To the sixty meters of evacuated wildlife?

To everything.

To the world that twitched and bent and fled because inside of me two forces were at war and the pressure between them was leaking outward. The conviction that I could not die. The will that I wanted to. Neither yielding. Neither winning. The collision between them producing a field of distortion that cracked ceramic and silenced birds and bent grass toward the source of the conflict, the way debris orbits the point of an explosion.

You salvaged the rice. Transferred it to the bowls before the last of the water escaped, half-cooked on one side, acceptable on the other. You ate. I looked at the bowl.

The appetite was absent. Not gone in the grief-sense of those first years after Wei, not absent in the existential-void sense of the field. Just not there. The body that didn't need food wasn't producing the signals that requested food and the mind that was exhausted from three days of pushing against itself didn't have the energy to override the body's indifference.

"Eat," you said.

"I'm not hungry."

"Eat anyway."

The flat insistence of a man who understood that biological nourishment was irrelevant and symbolic nourishment was not. You pushed the bowl toward me. The way — Wei. The pushed bowl. Be quiet. Eat.

I didn't eat.

But I stayed sitting. At the fire. With the rice I wasn't eating and the man who was and Xiǎohú who had found her spot at the edge of the disturbed radius and was sleeping with the commitment of an animal that had decided the universe's problems were not her problems tonight.

I stayed.

Clouds moved across the stars. Thick tonight, the accumulated moisture of the mountain range assembling into the heavy, low formations that promised rain. The stars appeared and disappeared behind the cloud-cover, flickering like uncertain lights, the sky itself unsure whether to reveal or conceal.

You washed the pot. The crack was still there, a fine line running the length of the ceramic like a vein.

"I can seal that with clay," you said.

You would. With river clay and patience and the pragmatism of a man who responded to supernatural phenomena with practical solutions. The pot would hold water again. The morning would come. The practice would resume.

I sat. The rice cooled in the bowl against my palms. Above us, the clouds covered the last of the stars and the dark was complete.
