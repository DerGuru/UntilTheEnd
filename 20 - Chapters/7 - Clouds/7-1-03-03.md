I was pushing again — an eighth attempt, a ninth, the repetition becoming rhythm, becoming its own kind of meditation — when the sediment stirred. It felt like a river's bottom stirs when you walk through it. Particles that have been settled over centuries become suddenly suspended, swirling, filling the water with the opacity of disturbed time.

The memory was old.

Older and deeper than the shaping hands. The oldest sediment, the layer at the bottom of everything, compacted by the weight of all the layers above it into something nearly geological.

I was in a cave. It was cold. The cold of stone that has never been touched by sun — the absolute, permanent cold of underground. The walls were close — closer than expected, the space narrowing even more as the image sharpened. I felt rough stone under my fingers that were not the fingers I saw every day. Younger fingers. Smaller. 

I was alone.

"Where are you?" you asked.

Your voice was distant, filtered through the membrane that separated the present from the deepest past. I was in both places simultaneously — on the mountain and in the cave, sitting in spring air and breathing cold stone, an adult who had lived for eons and a — what? A child? Much younger than what I was now. Existing at the origin point of whatever I had become.

"Nowhere," I said.

But my face — I could feel it betraying me. The moisture at the corners of my eyes that wasn't crying, the tension in my jaw that wasn't anger, the hands reaching for something that existed in memory but not here. My face said everything that my mouth refused.

"Describe nowhere," you said.

The precision of a scholar. The clinical instruction that bypassed the emotional blockade and addressed the intellectual apparatus. Describe. Not what do you feel, not are you okay, not any of the empathy-coded questions that would have permitted me to deflect. Describe. Clinical. Factual.

"It's cold," I said. "And tight. Rough stone."

"Where?"

"I'm in a cave. Small. Underground, I think. The light — there is no light. Or very little. What comes in is grey."

"When do you think, this happened?"

"I'm not sure. I'm smaller than now. I must be a child. So the beginning? The very beginning. My beginning? I don't know how long ago that was. But it feels like — it feels like—" I stopped, unable to continue. 

You stopped writing. The pen paused — mid-stroke, the motion arrested by the weight of two words. The beginning. You looked at me. The clinical mask recalibrated, becoming less of a scholar and more — something else. Something that lived beneath the clinical surface and emerged at moments of gravity.

"Your beginning?"

"I think so."

The images were fragmentary but clear. The self-protection was weaker — weakened by the pushing, by the repetitive attempts at mortality that had been battering against it like waves against a seawall. Not broken yet but weakened. Enough that the deepest memories could surface through the cracks.

Stone under my fingers. Cold. The absence of other bodies — the physical absence that a child registers not as concept but as sensation. No warmth from another source. No breathing that isn't yours. No sound other than your echoes from stone, the gurgling of water or the small, involuntary noises of a body existing in a space too small and too cold for comfort.

"I was alone," I said. "There was a cave and I was in it. And I was alone."

You wrote. The pen moved with the careful, measured strokes of a man documenting something irreplaceable — each word placed with the weight-consciousness of someone who understood that this record might be the only record, that what I was saying now might never be said again.

"How old were you in the memory?" you asked.

"Young. I don't — the memory doesn't give ages. Just the body. Small. Cold. The hands were smaller than mine."

"What else?"

"Water. The sound of it — constant. Like the cave's heartbeat."

The image faded — settled. The way the swirling sediment of the river settles when the walking stops. The origin had shown itself.

I opened my eyes. The mountain. You. Xiǎohú at her careful distance, watching.

"That's where it started," I said.

"A cave."

"A cave."

You looked at me. Something in your expression — a recognition, an understanding.

You wrote.

I sat in the mountain air. The metallic taste was there and the memory was there. 

The clouds moved overhead.

I had been a child in a cave, alone, at the beginning of everything.
