# Language Model for the Prequel

## Purpose

This model is meant for dialogue in an early Homo sapiens setting, roughly **150,000 years before present**, where language already exists but is not yet fully standardized, abstract, or stable across groups. The default speech level is a **mix of Level 2 (Practical Everyday) and Level 3 (Social/Emotional)** — these people can converse, argue, plan, and comfort. Level 1 is reserved for danger; Level 4–5 for rare moments of weight.

The goal is not to write "caveman grunts." The characters are intelligent, emotional, socially aware humans. Their speech should feel **human but early**: practical, concrete, local, gesture-heavy, and still flexible enough that individual speakers can help shape it.

This supports a detail from *Until The End*:

> "She helped the languages form."

The protagonist of the Origins series is Yun from UTE, here under her birth name (Mei in Book 1 — other names in later books). She did not invent language. But across millennia, she helped shape spoken patterns through use, repetition, naming, and influence on children and neighboring groups. In this first book, the seeds are planted: she names things no one has named before (what she feels, what she senses). In later books, this grows.

> **Note on examples:** All names in this document (Ara, Naru, Maku, etc.) are generic placeholders. Each Origins book will have its own characters and names. The language model applies across all of them.

---

## Core Principle

The language should feel like this:

> **Already useful, not yet fixed.**

Characters can warn, ask, deny, remember, comfort, accuse, name, plan, and argue. But they do so with short, concrete structures and heavy reliance on context.

They do not speak in polished modern sentences. They do not use complex abstractions easily. They do not explain themselves with long chains of reasoning unless the narration translates the meaning rather than reproducing exact speech.

---

## General Features

### 1. Short Sentences

Most spoken lines should be between **1 and 7 words**.

Occasionally, emotionally intense or important lines may be longer, but still simple.

**Good:**

> “Fire low.”
> “Bring dry wood.”
> “Child cold.”
> “We go when sky opens.”

**Too modern:**

> “The fire is getting low, so someone should gather more dry wood before the child gets colder.”

---

### 2. Concrete Vocabulary

The language is strongest with things that can be seen, touched, heard, smelled, eaten, feared, carried, or remembered through place.

Common words should include:

* people: I, you, we, child, mother, elder, stranger
* body: hand, eye, blood, bone, belly, mouth, heart
* survival: water, meat, fire, stone, shelter, cold, dark
* danger: tooth, claw, snake, bear, blood, bad smell, enemy
* movement: go, come, run, stay, hide, carry, follow
* perception: see, hear, smell, know, feel
* relation: mine, yours, with, alone, near, far
* time markers: now, before, soon, sun-up, sun-down, cold-light, dark-time

Abstract ideas should be expressed through concrete images.

**Instead of:**

> “I do not trust him.”

Use:

> “My belly turns from him.”
> “My eyes do not sleep near him.”

**Instead of:**

> “She is grieving.”

Use:

> “Her heart sits with the dead.”
> “She hears him in the night.”

---

### 3. Unstable Grammar

Grammar exists, but it is not fully standardized. Word order may vary depending on emphasis, emotion, or speaker.

A common default pattern may be:

> **Subject – Action – Object / Place**

Examples:

> “I see deer.”
> “You bring fire.”
> “We go river.”

But variation is allowed:

> “Deer, I see.”
> “River we go.”
> “Fire you bring. Now.”

This gives the language a forming, living quality.

---

### 4. No Fully Developed Tenses

Past and future exist, but they are often marked through time words rather than grammar.

Use:

> “Before, he came.”
> “Sun-down, herd moves.”
> “Tomorrow maybe rain.”
> “When cold-light comes, we go.”

Avoid:

> “He had already come before we realized the herd would be moving tomorrow.”

---

### 5. Repetition for Emphasis

Repetition replaces complex grammar and intensifiers.

Examples:

> “No. No go.”
> “Bad water. Bad bad.”
> “Run fast. Fast.”
> “I saw. I saw with both eyes.”

Repetition can also show fear, grief, insistence, or ritual importance.

---

### 6. Gesture as Part of Speech

Much meaning comes from gesture, posture, touch, and direction of gaze. Dialogue should often be incomplete without action beats.

Example:

> “There.”
> She did not point with her hand. She pointed with her chin, toward the reeds.
> “Long-tooth.”

Another example:

> “You stay.”
> He pressed two fingers to the child’s chest, then to the ground.
> “Stay. Quiet mouth.”

The words alone are simple. The full meaning comes from body, context, and shared knowledge.

---

### 7. Local Words and Named Sounds

Some words should remain untranslated or semi-translated. These are local group-words: names for animals, places, spirits, dangers, weather, kinship roles, or taboo things.

Examples:

> “Ka-ru comes.”
> “No. Not ka-ru. Smaller. Red back.”

> “We sleep near Doma-stone.”

> “The old one heard the vaa in the dark.”

These local words help show that the language is not universal. Different groups may use different sounds.

## Levels of Speech Complexity

### Level 1: Urgent Survival Speech

Used during danger, hunting, injury, fire, storms, or escape.

Very short. Often commands or warnings.

Examples:

> “Stop.”
> “Blood.”
> “Hide child.”
> “Claw there.”
> “Run river.”
> “No sound.”

Mini-scene:

> “Down.”
> Ara shoved the boy into the grass.
> “No mouth.”
> The reeds moved. Slow. Heavy.
> “Long-tooth,” she breathed. “Near.”

---

### Level 2: Practical Everyday Speech

Used for food, tools, travel, family, weather, work, and camp life.

Short, but more relaxed.

Examples:

> “Stone edge bad.”
> “I make new.”
> “You take small meat.”
> “Old mother needs warm skin.”
> “River high today.”

Mini-scene:

> “Fire eats fast,” Naru said.
> “Wood wet,” Ara answered.
> He touched the blackened branch and frowned.
> “Wet wood makes angry smoke.”
> “Then find dry.”
> “You find. My foot bad.”

---

### Level 3: Social and Emotional Speech

Used for trust, anger, shame, affection, grief, jealousy, fear, belonging.

Still concrete, but more metaphorical.

Examples:

> “Your words cut.”
> “My heart runs from this place.”
> “She keeps his shadow.”
> “You left me in dark.”
> “I came back.”
> “Late. Too late.”

Mini-scene:

> “You left child,” Ara said.
> Maku looked away.
> “I came back.”
> “Child cried long.”
> “I brought meat.”
> “Meat does not hold a child in night.”

---

### Level 4: Planning and Memory

Used for hunting plans, routes, remembered danger, seasonal movement, or teaching.

This is where the language can stretch. Still not modern, but capable of more than immediate survival.

Examples:

> “Before, herd went east after red moon.”
> “If rain comes, stone path breaks.”
> “We go high ground. Watch. Wait. Then drive them down.”
> “Young ones stay behind old tree. No shout until bird-call.”

Mini-scene:

> “Herd drinks at sun-up,” Ara said.
> She drew a line in the mud. River. Then stones. Then the narrow place.
> “We wait here.”
> “Wind wrong,” said Naru.
> Ara touched her nose.
> “Then go above. Wind carries us away from deer.”

---

### Level 5: Myth, Death, and the Unseen

This level should be used sparingly. It is poetic, but still grounded in image.

They may not have formal religion, philosophy, or theology, but they may already have stories, fears, taboos, and explanations shaped through metaphor.

Examples:

> “The dead do not eat, but they listen.”
> “Night has many eyes.”
> “Fire remembers hands.”
> “The cave keeps old breath.”
> “Do not speak his name near water.”

Mini-scene:

> “Where is he now?” the child asked.
> The elder looked toward the ridge where the sky had swallowed the sun.
> “Far walking.”
> “He comes back?”
> “In dream. In smoke. Not in body.”

## How the Protagonist Can “Help the Languages Form”

The character does not need to consciously invent language. Instead, show her shaping it through repeated use.

### 1. Naming

She gives a repeated sound to something important.

Example:

> The others called it “big cat.”
> Ara called it “long-tooth.”
> The children took her word first. Children always took the sharpest words.

Dialogue:

> “Long-tooth,” Ara said.
> “Cat?”
> “No. Long-tooth. It waits. It thinks.”

Over time, the group adopts her term.

---

### 2. Combining Words in New Ways

She links two words into a useful phrase that becomes common.

Example:

> “Quiet mouth.”

At first it is just her phrase for keeping children silent near danger. Later others use it too.

Dialogue:

> “Quiet mouth,” Ara whispered.
> The child pressed both hands over his lips.
> Two days later, Naru used the same words near the river.
> “Quiet mouth. Snake place.”

---

### 3. Making Metaphors Stick

She creates concrete metaphors for emotional or invisible things.

Example:

> “My heart goes cold.”

Dialogue:

> “Why no sleep?”
> “My heart goes cold when he watches.”

Later, another character uses the same structure:

> “My heart goes cold near the black cave.”

This shows linguistic formation without making it the plot’s main focus.

---

### 4. Children Preserve and Change Her Words

Children are crucial. They imitate, simplify, mishear, and spread words.

Example:

> Ara said “Doma-stone” once, because the stone looked like Doma’s bent back.
> The children shortened it to “Doma.”
> By the next season, even strangers knew the place by that sound.

---

### 5. Contact With Other Groups

Different groups should not speak exactly the same way. Communication with outsiders should be possible but imperfect.

Example:

> “Water?” Ara asked.
> The stranger stared.
> Naru made the drinking gesture.
> The stranger nodded quickly. “Awa. Awa.”
> Ara touched the river and repeated it. “Awa.”

This shows language as exchange, not invention.

## Dialogue Rules for the Novel

### Rule 1: Keep Spoken Lines Short

Most direct speech should be short enough to feel early.

**Good:**

> “You bleed.”
> “Small blood.”
> “Not small. Sit.”

**Too modern:**

> “You’re bleeding more than you think. You need to sit down before you make it worse.”

---

### Rule 2: Use Narration to Carry Complex Meaning

If a character needs to express something complex, do not put all of it into dialogue. Let narration translate the intent.

Example:

> “You walk like broken branch,” Ara said.
> She meant: you are hurt, you are hiding it badly, and if you keep pretending, you will slow us all.

This allows intelligence without modern speech.

---

### Rule 3: Avoid Modern Idioms

Avoid phrases like:

* “That’s not fair.”
* “I need space.”
* “We need to talk.”
* “You have no idea what I’ve been through.”
* “This changes everything.”

Use concrete equivalents:

> “You took more meat.”
> “Do not stand close.”
> “Words now.”
> “You did not walk my dark.”
> “Now all paths bend.”

---

### Rule 4: Let Grammar Vary by Speaker

Not every character needs the same speech style.

* Elders may use old ritual words.
* Children may simplify and repeat.
* Hunters may use precise practical terms.
* Outsiders may have strange word order.
* The protagonist may be unusually good at combining words.

Example:

**Child:**

> “Bad dark. Bad bad.”

**Hunter:**

> “Dark not bad. Smell bad. Something dead near.”

**Elder:**

> “Dead near water brings teeth.”

---

### Rule 5: Keep Abstraction Rare and Earned

Characters can think and feel deeply. But spoken abstraction should be rare, powerful, and image-based.

Instead of:

> “I am afraid that the group will abandon me.”

Use:

> “When I fall, no hands come.”

Instead of:

> “I remember him and cannot let go.”

Use:

> “His shadow walks behind my eyes.”

## Sample Dialogue: Hunting Warning

> “Stop.”
> Ara lifted one hand.
> Naru froze behind her.
> “What?”
> “Grass wrong.”
> He looked. The grass moved with the wind. Nothing more.
> “I see no tooth.”
> “No see.” Ara touched her ear. “Hear.”
> A low sound rolled through the reeds.
> Naru’s face changed.
> “Long-tooth?”
> “Maybe. Take child. Slow feet. No mouth.”

---

## Sample Dialogue: Conflict

> “You took meat,” Maku said.
> Ara did not look up.
> “Child hungry.”
> “All children hungry.”
> “Your child has mother milk.”
> “My child has thin arms.”
> Ara set the strip of meat on the stone between them.
> “Then cut. Half. No loud words.”

---

## Sample Dialogue: Emotional Moment

> “You sleep here?”
> “No.”
> “Night cold.”
> “Cold better than eyes.”
> “Whose eyes?”
> Ara looked toward the men near the fire.
> “His. His eyes have teeth.”

---

## Sample Dialogue: Teaching a Child

> “This stone?” the child asked.
> “Sharp-stone.”
> “This?”
> “Dead-stone. Breaks in hand.”
> The child struck it anyway. It split badly and cut his thumb.
> Ara took his hand.
> “Dead-stone bites. Sharp-stone cuts for us.”

---

## Sample Dialogue: Outsider Contact

> The stranger pointed at the river.
> “Awa.”
> Ara followed his finger.
> “Water.”
> “Awa,” he said again, touching his mouth.
> Naru laughed softly. “He has wrong water-word.”
> Ara shook her head.
> “Not wrong. His.”

This moment subtly supports the idea of multiple languages forming.

---

## Sample Dialogue: The Protagonist Shapes a Phrase

> The child whimpered when the distant roar came again.
> “Quiet,” Naru hissed.
> The child cried harder.
> Ara crouched and touched two fingers to her own lips.
> “Quiet mouth,” she whispered.
> Then she touched her chest.
> “Loud heart. Quiet mouth.”
> The child stared at her, breathing fast.
> Ara repeated it.
> “Loud heart. Quiet mouth.”
> His hands covered his lips.
> Around them, the grass trembled.

Later in the story, someone else can reuse the phrase:

> “Quiet mouth,” Maku said to the younger hunters.
> “Loud heart if you must. But quiet mouth.”

This shows her linguistic influence without making the story about linguistics.

## Recommended Style Balance

The default dialogue balance is:

* **50% practical everyday speech (Level 2)**
* **30% emotional/social speech (Level 3)**
* **15% planning/memory speech (Level 4)**
* **5% urgent survival speech (Level 1) — only during danger**

Level 5 (Myth/Death/Unseen) is available but should be used sparingly and only when the story earns it.

Too much poetic language will make them sound like fantasy shamans. Too little will make them sound cartoonish.

The sweet spot is:

> intelligent people with fewer inherited words than we have, using body, place, repetition, and image to make meaning.

## Protagonist Alone: Speech Without an Audience

After the protagonist is separated from her group (Book 1: Mei is exiled at age 11), she spends years alone. During this phase:

- **The narrator describes her inner world** using the narrator's rules (literary, sensorisch, Mei's perception level — see Concept.md). The language model does not apply to narration.
- **When she speaks aloud**, the language model applies fully. She speaks to herself — out of frustration, to steel herself, to fill the silence, to work through a problem. These utterances follow the same rules as dialogue: short, concrete, gesture-adjacent.

Examples:

> "No. Again."
> She struck the flint wrong. Again.
> "Stupid hand."

> The wound bled faster than she expected.
> "Stop," she said, pressing the warm thing toward it. "Stop."

> She stared at the dark mouth of the cave.
> "Not bad dark. Dry. Warm underneath."
> She went in.

This also means her speech may simplify over years alone — fewer social phrases, more self-directed commands and observations. When she eventually encounters people again (in later books), the contrast should be audible.

## Quick Checklist

Before keeping a line of dialogue, ask:

1. Is the sentence short?
2. Is the vocabulary concrete?
3. Could gesture or context carry part of the meaning?
4. Is the grammar slightly simpler than modern speech?
5. Does it avoid modern idioms?
6. Does it still feel emotionally human?
7. Could children repeat or reshape this phrase?

If yes, it likely fits the model.

---

## Final Guideline

Do not write them as stupid.

Write them as people whose minds are fully human, but whose shared language is still young enough that words feel handmade.

They can love, lie, mourn, joke, fear, plan, and remember.

They simply do it with fewer fixed tools — and every time they speak, those tools become a little more real.
