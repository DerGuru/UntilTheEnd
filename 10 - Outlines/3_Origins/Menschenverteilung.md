# Encounter Probability Around 150,000 BC

> **Scope:** Origins-Serienressource. Gilt für alle Bücher, die im vergleichbaren Zeitrahmen spielen — nicht nur Buch 1.

## Purpose

This note is meant as a worldbuilding guide for a Stone Age / early Homo sapiens prequel set around **150,000 BC**.

Mei's group (and Mei herself) are **Homo sapiens**. Other groups she encounters may also be Homo sapiens, or — depending on region — archaic humans (Denisovans, late Homo erectus-related populations). The distinction is left open for narrative use in later books.

The central question:

> If someone was travelling alone around 150,000 BC, how likely was it that they would meet another human group?

The short answer:

> **Possible, but not common. Highly dependent on landscape, season, and whether the traveller follows natural resource corridors.**

A lone person wandering randomly through empty terrain might see no one for weeks or months. But a person following rivers, lakes, coasts, game trails, or seasonal gathering places could plausibly encounter signs of people within days and possibly meet a group within days to weeks.

---

## Core Principle

Human groups were probably not evenly spread across the landscape.

They would cluster around:

* reliable water
* seasonal food sources
* animal migration paths
* rivers and lakes
* coasts
* caves or shelters
* tool-stone sources
* safe crossings
* known camp areas

So the chance of meeting people depends less on “how many humans exist in the world” and more on whether the character is moving through places humans would naturally use.

---

## Practical Probability Model

### Very High Chance of Signs, Moderate Chance of Contact

**Environment:** river valleys, lakeshores, coastal routes, fertile wetlands, seasonal hunting grounds

**Likely signs:**

* old fire pits
* footprints near mud or water
* stone flakes
* broken bones
* butchered carcasses
* smoke in the distance
* discarded shells
* trampled grass near a crossing

**Direct encounter:** plausible within days to weeks.

**Narrative use:** best option if the plot needs the lone traveller to eventually find people without it feeling forced.

Example:

> She followed the river because every living thing followed the river. Deer. Cats. Birds. People. By the third sunrise, she found ash under a flat stone. By the fifth, she saw smoke.

---

### Moderate Chance

**Environment:** open savanna, mixed woodland, known migration corridors, scattered waterholes

**Likely signs:**

* old camps near water
* animal bones cracked for marrow
* distant smoke
* tool marks on stone
* footprints after rain

**Direct encounter:** possible, but not guaranteed. Days to several weeks.

**Narrative use:** good for tension. The character can find hints of people before meeting them.

Example:

> The waterhole was not empty. No people stood there now, but the ground remembered them: heel marks, black ash, a child’s small print hardened in the mud.

---

### Low Chance

**Environment:** dense forest, broken hills, dry scrubland, unfamiliar interior routes

**Likely signs:** rare and easy to miss.

**Direct encounter:** unlikely unless the traveller knows where people move.

**Narrative use:** good for isolation, fear, starvation, or the feeling of being lost between worlds.

Example:

> For seven days she saw no smoke. No cut bone. No stone shaped by hand. Only trees, rain, insects, and the growing sound of her own breath.

---

### Very Low Chance

**Environment:** desert, semi-desert, high mountains, cold barren land, deep unproductive interior

**Likely signs:** extremely rare.

**Direct encounter:** unlikely. Survival itself becomes the main problem.

**Narrative use:** use only if the story wants danger and near-impossibility.

Example:

> No one lived where the ground held no water. No deer. No birds. No people. Only wind drawing lines through dust.

---

## Recommended Timeframes for Fiction

These are not hard numbers, but useful writing estimates.

| Situation                        |        Signs of people |            Direct encounter |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------: | --------------------------: |
| Following a river/lake/coast     |               1–7 days | several days to a few weeks |
| Moving through good hunting land |              3–14 days |                   1–4 weeks |
| Crossing mixed forest/hills      |              1–4 weeks |             weeks to months |
| Dry or marginal land             |            maybe never |                    unlikely |
| Known seasonal meeting area      | same day to a few days |              very plausible |

---

## Best Story Pattern

For realism, the character should usually encounter **signs before people**.

Good sequence:

1. She finds an old fire pit.
2. She finds stone flakes or cracked bones.
3. She sees smoke.
4. She hears voices or rhythmic tool strikes.
5. She watches from hiding.
6. The group notices her or she chooses to reveal herself.

This makes the meeting feel earned rather than random.

Example:

> First came the ash.
> Then the split bone.
> Then the stone flake, sharp enough to open skin.
> People had been here. Not ghosts. Not memory. People.
> On the second evening, she saw smoke beyond the trees.

## Why Water Matters

Water is the most believable reason for paths to cross.

A lone traveller needs water. Animals need water. Human groups need water. This creates natural meeting points.

Useful encounter locations:

* river bends
* shallow crossings
* lakeshores
* springs
* waterholes
* marsh edges
* coastal shellfish areas
* places where animals come to drink

Example:

> She did not find them because the world was full of people.
> She found them because the dry season had made all paths bend toward water.

## How Suspicious Would a Lone Traveller Be?

Very suspicious.

A person alone in this period would raise questions:

* Are they sick?
* Were they exiled?
* Are they dangerous?
* Are they being followed?
* Are they bait for an ambush?
* Do they carry a curse, taboo, or bad omen?
* Did their group die?
* Are they useful enough to risk feeding?

A group would probably not instantly welcome them.

They might:

* watch from hiding
* send one or two people forward
* show weapons
* demand distance
* test their words
* offer food only after caution
* keep children behind adults
* avoid direct touch

Example:

> The first one she saw was not the leader. It was a watcher.
> Young. Still. Spear low but ready.
> Behind him, in the trees, others waited without moving.
> She knew then that they had seen her long before she saw them.

## Communication Between Groups

Different groups may not speak exactly the same way.

At 150,000 BC, for fiction, it is plausible that neighboring groups can understand some of each other’s words, especially for basic things:

* water
* fire
* food
* danger
* child
* go
* come
* no
* trade/give
* kin terms

But more complex speech may be difficult.

Misunderstanding is useful and realistic.

Example:

> “Water,” she said.
> The man frowned.
> She touched her mouth, then pointed to the river.
> His face changed.
> “Awa,” he said.
> She repeated it carefully. “Awa.”

This supports the idea that languages are forming: related but local, overlapping but not standardized.

## Setting in China or East Asia (Xinaxia)

Around 150,000 BC, the situation is a bit complicated.

A story set in China or East Asia at this time could plausibly include archaic humans or other hominin groups rather than clearly modern Homo sapiens.

Possible options:

* archaic Homo groups
* Denisovan-related populations
* late Homo erectus-like or mixed archaic populations, depending on the exact setting and interpretation
* early modern humans only with more speculation

For fiction, this can be powerful. The lone traveller may meet people who are human, but not exactly her kind.

This could also make language contact stranger and more fragile.

Example:

> They had hands like hers. Fire like hers. Fear like hers.
> But their words sat differently in the mouth.

## Encounter Probability by Narrative Need

### If You Need Isolation

Set the route through:

* dry uplands
* broken hills
* deep forest
* post-disaster territory
* land between known water sources

Use few signs of humans.

Example tone:

> The world was too large. Every hill opened into another empty hill.

---

### If You Need Hope

Let her find signs first.

Use:

* ash
* footprints
* a shaped stone
* bones cracked by tools
* smoke
* distant calls

Example tone:

> Someone had sat here. Someone had eaten. Someone had kept fire alive.

---

### If You Need Tension

Let her find a group, but make the meeting dangerous.

The group may be hungry, frightened, hostile, or cautious.

Example tone:

> Finding people did not mean finding safety. People had teeth too.

---

### If You Need Language Development

Make the meeting a contact point between speech systems.

Use partial understanding:

> “Fire?”
> “Ha.”
> “Fire,” she repeated, pointing.
> The woman shook her head and touched the flame. “Ha.”
> Later, the child used both words.

This supports the later idea that the protagonist helped languages form.

---

## Good Fictional Rule of Thumb

For a lone traveller around 150,000 BC:

> **No people for several days is normal.**
> **Signs of people within a week near water is plausible.**
> **A direct encounter within a few weeks in a rich landscape is believable.**
> **Months without contact is plausible in marginal or unfamiliar land.**

---

## Recommended Approach for the Prequel

The strongest version is not:

> She randomly meets a group in the wilderness.

Better:

> She follows water, finds signs, realizes others are near, and only then encounters them.

This makes the meeting feel grounded.

Suggested structure:

1. She travels alone and is vulnerable.
2. She finds an old camp.
3. She realizes the camp is recent.
4. She follows signs cautiously.
5. The group notices her first.
6. The first contact is tense.
7. Basic words and gestures bridge the gap.
8. Her words or phrases begin influencing them over time.

---

## Sample Scene: First Signs

> The ash was cold, but not old.
> Rain had not touched it. Wind had not taken it.
> Beside the black circle lay bone, split clean for marrow.
> She crouched and touched the edge of a stone flake. Too sharp for river work. Too sharp for chance.
> People.
> Her chest tightened.
> Not safety. Not yet.
> But people.

---

## Sample Scene: First Contact

> She saw the boy first.
> He stood between two trees with a spear too large for his arms. His eyes were wide, but his feet did not run.
> “Water,” she said.
> He did not answer.
> She touched her mouth. Pointed to the river.
> “Water.”
> From the trees behind him, a woman spoke one sharp word.
> The boy stepped back.
> Then the woman came forward, slow, with three others behind her.
> Not welcome.
> Not attack.
> Something between.

---

## Sample Scene: Language Contact

> The woman pointed to the river.
> “Awa.”
> “Water,” Ara said.
> “Awa.”
> Ara nodded. “Awa.”
> The woman touched her own chest. “Sena.”
> Ara understood that. Names were old, even when words were strange.
> She touched her chest.
> “Ara.”

---

## Final Guideline

A meeting with another group around 150,000 BC should feel rare enough to matter, but not so rare that it becomes impossible.

The world is not empty.

It is patchy.

People gather where survival gathers them: water, food, fire, shelter, stone, and memory.
